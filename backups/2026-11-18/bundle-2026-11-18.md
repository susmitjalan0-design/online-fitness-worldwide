## Verdict: **Ready with fixes. Not for clients until the must-fix items are done.**

The section is well built. "Never swim alone," lifeguard and race rules, local conditions, an exit plan, a help signal, a gradual first-swim progression and a "we never supervise" statement are all there. Brand, worldwide framing, no prices and no endorsement all pass. However:

- one heat sign is misleading;
- there is no medical-condition check before cold open water;
- the cold-water get-out and rewarming points are incomplete (no afterdrop, and no emergency route); and
- almost every safety fact traces only to a book whose interior was not reviewed.

All of these can be fixed with wording changes.

## Must-fix (before any client use)

### M1. Line 473: "stopping sweating" is listed as a heat-illness sign
> "Heat illness signs (feeling faint, confused, nausea, headache, stopping sweating): **stop, get out, cool down and get help.**"

**Why:** It suggests that a client who is still sweating is fine. The CDC/NIOSH page lists heat stroke as "hot, dry skin **or profuse sweating**". The NHS lists heat exhaustion with "excessive sweating". This line also doesn't separate "stop and cool down" from "call emergency services now". Confusion is an emergency sign, not just a reason to stop.

**Suggested replacement:**
> - **Stop, get out of the water and the sun, and cool down** if you feel tired, weak, dizzy, faint, sick, have a headache or cramps, or are very thirsty (NHS; CDC).
> - **Call your local emergency number now** if someone is confused, slurring words, uncoordinated, has a seizure, collapses or passes out, or is still unwell after cooling down. Do not use sweating, or its absence, to decide. Heat stroke can happen with dry skin **or** heavy sweating (CDC; NHS).
> - A coach never manages heat illness remotely (Coaching Standards rule).

### M2. No medical-condition check before cold or open water (section 4 as a whole, best placed in 4.2)
**Why:** RNLI says cold water shock raises heart rate and blood pressure and "can cause heart attacks, even in the relatively young and healthy". The Outdoor Swimming Society (OSS) says it can trigger panic and asthma attacks. The pack header (line 13) and 7.1 (line 714) route conditions to a clinician. Section 4 itself does not, though it is the place a client will actually read before swimming. Under Coaching Standards section 2, the client's clinician manages conditions and the coach adapts training.

**Suggested new checklist item for 4.2:**
> - [ ] If you have a heart or blood-pressure condition, asthma, diabetes, epilepsy or seizures, are pregnant or postpartum, take regular medication, have a weakened immune system, or have any other medical condition, **check with your own doctor before your first open-water or cold-water swim.** Tell your coach. We will hand this on to **Metabolic Health** (heart, blood pressure, diabetes), **Women's Health** (pregnancy, postpartum), **Masters** (roughly 40+) or **Physio** (injury). We don't give medical advice or clearance.

(CDC: if your ability to fight germs is affected by health problems or medicines, check with a healthcare provider before swimming in natural water.)

### M3. Lines 461–464: the cold-water get-out signs are incomplete, and afterdrop and the emergency route are missing
> 461: "The first minute can take your breath away (cold shock). Stay calm, float, breathe slowly, then swim."
> 463: "Get out if you shiver hard, feel clumsy, confused or numb."
> 464: "Rewarm gradually: dry off, warm layers, warm drink. Do not drive until fully warm."

**Why:** The direction is right, but some things a client needs are missing:

- **Swim failure:** arms and legs weaken (OSS).
- **Stroke changes:** the swimmer becomes more upright in the water, or has trouble speaking (OSS).
- **Afterdrop:** you keep cooling for up to about 30 minutes after getting out, and are usually coldest around 10 minutes after (OSS).
- **Hot showers or baths when hypothermic:** OSS says don't use warm ones.
- **Shivering that stops:** this can mean severe hypothermia, not recovery (OSS). Line 657 in 6.5 already says this, but 4.4 does not.
- **When to call emergency services.**

**Suggested replacement for lines 461–464:**
> - **Cold shock:** the first minute can make you gasp and breathe fast. Don't try to swim straight away. Float, let your breathing settle, then call for help or swim to safety (RNLI, *Float to Live*).
> - **Get out now** if you can't stop shivering, feel numb or clumsy, your stroke changes or you start swimming more upright, your arms or legs feel weak, your hands won't work properly, you have trouble speaking, or you feel confused (OSS). Get out early. Don't set time targets.
> - **After you get out you keep cooling** for up to about 30 minutes, and are often coldest about 10 minutes after. Get out of the wind, remove wet kit, dry off, layer up, and have a warm drink and some food (OSS).
> - If you are very cold, don't take a hot shower or bath to warm up. Don't drive or ride until you are fully warm (OSS).
> - **Call your local emergency number** if someone is confused, can't speak clearly, collapses, becomes drowsy or unresponsive, or their shivering stops while they are still cold.

### M4. Safety facts are not traceable to opened sources (lines 431, 459–481; Sources, lines 671–698)
**Why:** The only source given for section 4 is Lynne Cox, "general-level ideas only" (line 431). Casa is given for the heat rule (line 473). I opened the Google Books page for each. Both are real and their catalogue descriptions fit the topic: Cox covers currents, waves, weather and marine organisms, and Casa covers heat safety. Neither page provides a sign list, a temperature figure or a rewarming method. Claims checklist item 5 requires every safety fact to have a real, checkable source that was actually opened. As written, the cold, heat, rip-current and water-quality points have no traceable source.

**Suggested fix:**
- Cite the public pages below in section 4 next to each point, or in a short "Safety sources" sub-list.
- Add them to the Sources section, keeping the existing no-endorsement line and adding RNLI, OSS, NHS and CDC to it.
- Keep Cox and Casa as background only.

All of these were verified for this review:

## Should-fix

### S1. Line 462: the 15 °C figure is supported but not attributed
> "Many swimmers find water below about 15 °C (59 °F) cold."

RNLI says "anything below 15°C is defined as cold water and can seriously affect your breathing and movement". So the number is supported, but the wording weakens it and has no source.

**Replacement:**
> "Water below 15 °C (59 °F) counts as cold water and can seriously affect your breathing and movement (RNLI). Colder water means shorter swims and more caution. A wetsuit keeps you warmer for longer but does not remove the risk (OSS)."

### S2. Line 466: the seasons sentence is unsourced and garbled
> "Northern Hemisphere lakes and seas (Europe, North America, Japan) are often coldest in spring and early summer swims; Southern Hemisphere waters (Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Chile, Argentina) warm up from around November."

It is unsourced. "Coldest in spring and early summer" is only true of the swim season, not of the year. I did not verify the November timing for each country.

**Replacement:**
> "Water temperature depends on the place, the season and the day. Early-season swims in either hemisphere are often the coldest of the swim season, and inland water can be colder than the sea even in summer (RNLI). Check the local water temperature on the day."

### S3. Line 454: the "about 5–10 min" first-swim figure is unsourced
OSS advises starting with short swims close to shore, building up gradually, and *not* using a time formula ("don't use x degrees = x minutes").

**Replacement:**
> "First swims are short: a few minutes, close to and parallel to the shore, in standing depth where possible. Get out while you still feel fine. Don't set time targets (OSS)."

If Triathlon Coach wants to keep 5–10 min as a coaching suggestion, label it as coach judgement, not a safety threshold.

### S4. Lines 435 and 445: "never alone" can be read as "a buddy is enough"
Line 435 is good. But 4.2 (line 445) only requires "a buddy is with you", and line 435 allows "with a safety boat or kayak", which could mean a solo swimmer with a friend paddling.

**Replacement for line 435:**
> "**Never swim alone.** Swim at a lifeguarded venue or an organised session with safety cover (lifeguards, safety boat or kayak). Swim with a buddy as well. Your coach on a video call is not supervision."

### S5. Line 465: wetsuit rules could be clearer, and rules vary by race
"Race wetsuit rules are set by the race. Check them." is correct. I verified that World Triathlon's 2026 rules make wetsuits mandatory at 15.9 °C and below, forbid them above set temperatures for age-groupers, and allow the swim to be shortened or cancelled at very low or high water temperatures. Other organisers and national federations may use different rules.

**Suggested wording:**
> "Wetsuit rules and swim cancellations are set by each race and governing body (for example, the World Triathlon Competition Rules). Rules differ by organiser and country. Check your race's rules and the official water temperature announced before the start."

Don't copy the specific temperature numbers unless they are kept up to date.

### S6. Lines 477–480: hazards need sourcing and a clearer "stay out" rule
- **Water quality (CDC):** stay out if signs say the area is closed; the water is cloudier than usual, discoloured or smelly (possible harmful algal bloom); pipes drain into the area; you have diarrhoea; or you have an open cut or wound. Check advisories, especially after heavy rain, and don't swallow the water.
- **Rip currents (RNLI):** don't swim against a rip. If you can stand, wade out. If not, swim parallel to the shore until free, then head in. If in difficulty, raise one hand, shout and Float to Live. Choose lifeguarded beaches and swim between the flags where that system is used.
- **Wildlife:** dangerous marine life and wildlife differ completely by country and season. Follow local signage, lifeguards and authorities, and don't rely on general advice. Don't give sting or bite first-aid steps in our content. Treatment differs by species and region, so follow local lifeguard or medical advice.

### S7. Line 439: attribute the help signal
"Often one arm raised and waved. Float on your back while you wait" matches RNLI ("raise one hand, shout for help and Float to Live"). Add "(RNLI)", and keep "the signal your venue or race uses", since signals vary.

**Quick jargon key**

- **RPE:** rate of perceived exertion. How hard it feels, on a 1–10 scale.
- **HR:** heart rate, in beats per minute.
- **Power:** watts on the bike, from a power meter or smart trainer, if the client already has one.

- **Drill:** a simplified swim movement that isolates one skill.
- **Cadence:** pedal or step rate per minute.

## 1. Multisport principles: one page for clients who also lift twice a week

Three sports. Two strength sessions. One life.
The plan has to fit the week, not the other way round.

### 1.1 Weekly time budget

Start from the hours you really have. Then add strength on top.

| Goal | Endurance time per week (peak weeks) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| First sprint | about 3.5–5 h | 2 × 45–60 min | Low-volume style, in the spirit of Friel's *Your First Triathlon* |
| Olympic | about 6–9 h | 2 × 45–60 min | Short-course focus (Foster & Bolton) |
| 70.3 (half distance) | about 8–12 h | 2 sessions, moving to 1–2 maintenance | Long weekend sessions |
| Full distance (long course) | about 10–15+ h | 1–2 maintenance | Work and family balance (Friel & Byrn, *Going Long*) |
| Duathlon | similar to the matching triathlon, minus swim time | 2 × 45–60 min | Run impact needs care |

These are planning ranges, not targets. A busy week means a smaller week. That is fine.

### 1.2 Easy versus quality: about 80/20 across all three sports

- Count training **time**, across swim, bike and run together.
- About 80% easy (Zone 1–2). About 20% quality (Zone 3 and above).
- Easy means truly easy. You can talk in full sentences.
- Most athletes drift into a "grey middle" effort every day. That middle effort tires you without building much. (*80/20 Endurance*, Fitzgerald; *80/20 Running*, Fitzgerald.)
- For first-timers, "quality" can be short pick-ups or technique work, not hard intervals.
- Strength sessions sit outside this count. Strength Coach owns them.

### 1.3 Progression limits

- Raise total weekly time by **no more than about 10%** a week. This is a rule of thumb, not a law.
- Change **one variable at a time**: duration, or frequency, or intensity. Never all three.
- Grow the long session in one sport at a time.
- Every 3rd or 4th week is a **lighter week**: about a third less time, same habits.
- Technique before volume in the water. Add metres (yards) only when the stroke holds.
- Two easy weeks of base before any hard intervals.

### 1.4 Missed-session rules

- **Missed one session?** Let it go. Do not cram it in.
- **Missed 2–4 days?** Pick up the plan where it is today. Drop the missed quality session.
- **Missed 5–7 days?** Repeat the previous week before moving on.
- **Missed 2+ weeks?** Tell your coach. We step the plan back and rewrite it.
- **Never** stack two hard sessions on one day to "catch up".
- **Short on time this week?** Keep the one key session per sport. Cut easy volume first.
- **Ill or injured?** No training through fever or chest symptoms. Talk to your clinician. Pain goes to Physio before you resume.

### 1.5 Sequencing with two strength sessions

The goal: heavy leg days and hard bike or run days never collide.
Treat interference as a scheduling problem (*The Hybrid Athlete*, Viada; *Concurrent Aerobic and Strength Training*, Schumann & Rønnestad, eds.).

- **Strength A = heavy lower body.** Strength B = upper-body focus with light legs.
- Keep Strength A **at least 48 hours** (two days) before the week's hardest bike or run.
- The day after Strength A: easy swim, easy spin or rest only.
- **Experienced clients only:** a hard session and a lift can share one day. Hard endurance first. Lift later, with as many hours between as possible (aim for 6+ h).
- Swim quality and heavy upper-body pulling should not sit back to back. Space them by a day where possible.
- As the race nears, strength shifts to **maintenance** (fewer sets, same quality). Strength Coach sets the details.
- Race week: last heavy strength session usually 7–10 days out, agreed with Strength Coach.

**Template week shape (used by all plans below)**

| Day | Endurance | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Easy swim or rest | **Strength A** (heavy lower) |
| Tue | Swim quality and/or easy run | — |
| Wed | **Bike quality** (48 h after Strength A) | — |
| Thu | Run quality or easy run | **Strength B** (upper focus, light legs), later in the day |
| Fri | Easy swim or rest | — |
| Sat | Long bike, often a brick | — |
| Sun | Long easy run | — |

Clients in the Southern Hemisphere, the tropics or the Gulf may swap days for heat, daylight or work weeks (for example, Sunday–Thursday working weeks). The rules stay the same.

## 3. Remote swim-video review protocol

We coach from client-filmed video. We never supervise swimming in person or in real time.

### 3.1 Before filming: permission, consent and privacy

- **Ask the pool first.** Many pools restrict or ban cameras and phones. If filming is not allowed, we do not film. We use a written self-check instead.
- **Client consent.** The client agrees in writing that we may view and keep the video. Privacy & Data Care sets how it is sent, stored and deleted.
- **No one else identifiable.** Film at a quiet time, in an end lane if possible. Frame tightly on the swimmer.
- **Never film children** or anyone who has not agreed. If someone else is in shot, re-film or crop before sending.
- **Never post** client swim video anywhere. Video / Social only use it with separate written consent and Susmit's approval.

### 3.2 Filming instructions

A friend films from the deck. The swimmer swims easy, then moderate.

| Angle | How | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Side view, from the deck** | Filmer walks level with the swimmer at mid-pool. Phone held at hip height, landscape. Film one length each way: 25 m / 27 yd | Body line, head, rotation, kick, timing |
| **2. Front view, from the end** | Filmer stands or crouches at the end wall. Swimmer swims towards the camera for one length | Hand entry, crossover, breathing, symmetry |
| **3. Underwater side view (only if pool rules allow)** | Phone in a waterproof case the client already owns, held just below the surface from the deck edge. The filmer stays on the deck and never gets in to film | Catch, pull path, kick depth |

- 2 lengths easy, 1 length moderate, per angle.
- Good light. Hold the phone steady. No zoom.
- Note the pool length (25 m / 27 yd, 50 m / 55 yd, or 25 yd / 23 m) and how the swimmer breathes (every 2 or 3 strokes).

### 3.3 The 8-point stroke checklist

Each point is scored green (good), amber (working on it) or red (priority).

1. **Head and body line.** Head neutral, eyes down and slightly forward, hips near the surface.
2. **Breathing.** Turn the head with the body roll; one goggle stays in the water; breathe out steadily under water.
3. **Rotation.** Shoulders and hips roll together, side to side.
4. **Hand entry.** Fingertips first, in line with the shoulder, no crossing the midline.
5. **Reach and catch.** Long reach forward, then the forearm angles down and starts pressing water back.
6. **Pull and finish.** Press back past the hip; no pushing down or lifting water up.
7. **Arm recovery.** Relaxed arm, elbow leads, hand close to the body.
8. **Kick and timing.** Small, steady kick from the hip, legs close to the surface; a smooth rhythm with no long pauses.

### 3.4 Drill menu

Balance first, a long streamlined body and quiet, unhurried strokes follow the general approach of Laughlin's *Total Immersion*. Drills below are attributed at a general level only.

| Drill | What it builds | Checklist point | Easier version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Push-and-glide (arms forward, face down) | Body line, relaxation | 1 | Short glides from the wall, stand often |
| Side kick (one arm forward, on your side) | Balance, rotation, breathing position | 1, 2, 3 | Kickboard under the lead arm |
| Six-kick switch (side kick, then one stroke to the other side) | Rotation and timing | 3, 8 | Fewer kicks, more rest |
| Catch-up (one hand waits in front until the other arrives) | Entry, reach | 4, 5 | Hold a kickboard as the "waiting" hand |
| Fingertip drag | Relaxed, high-elbow recovery | 7 | Slow pace, short lengths |
| Front sculling (small figure-of-eight hand movements) | Feel for the water at the catch | 5 | Sculling while standing in shallow water |
| Single-arm swimming | Pull path and finish | 5, 6 | Other arm resting at the side or on a board |
| Breathing every 3 strokes (bilateral) | Symmetry, calm breathing | 2 | Every 2 strokes, alternate sides each length |
| Stroke-count lengths (count strokes per length, try to swim "quietly") | Efficiency, rhythm | all | Count only, no target |
| Head-up sighting (lift eyes forward every 6–8 strokes) | Open-water skill | 1, 2 | Every 10 strokes, then build |

### 3.5 Review flow

1. Watch every angle. Score the 8 points.
2. Pick **1–2 priorities**. Never more.
3. Give **2–3 drills** from the menu that match those priorities.
4. Client does the drills in 2–3 swims a week, for 2–3 weeks. Drills take about 10–15 min of each swim.
5. **Re-film** the same angles. Compare. Pick the next priority.

Feedback is short, plain and kind. Adult-onset swimmers are welcome. We never shame pace, body or ability.

### 3.6 Dry-land options

Anatomy-based swim strength ideas at a general level from McLeod's *Swimming Anatomy*. Strength Coach fits these into Strength A or B, or adds a 10–15 min block 2–3 times a week.

- Band pull-aparts (upper back).
- Band external rotation (rotator cuff).
- Prone Y-T-W raises (shoulder blades).
- Lat pulldown or assisted pull-up (pulling strength).
- Push-up plus (shoulder blade control).
- Dead bug and side plank (trunk control).
- Glute bridge (hips, for the kick and the bike).
- Thoracic rotation and ankle mobility.

### 3.7 Shoulder pain goes to Physio

- Any shoulder pain, clicking with pain, numbness or weakness: stop the drill or set and hand to **Physio**.
- Common triggers to watch for: a sudden jump in swim volume, lots of hard pull sets, or new equipment. We flag; we do not diagnose.
- Background reading for Physio only: Ellenbecker & Wilk, *Sport Therapy for the Shoulder*.

## 4. Open-water safety and acclimatisation checklist

> **FOR COACHING STANDARDS REVIEW.** Draft only. Not for clients until Coaching Standards and Susmit sign it off.
> We educate. We never supervise open-water swimming, in person or remotely.
> Adults only. Questions about a child or teenager swimming in open water go to Susmit.
> Background reading: Lynne Cox, *Open Water Swimming Manual*, and Casa (ed.), *Sport and Physical Activity in the Heat* (general-level ideas only). The specific safety points below come from the public sources named next to each one (RNLI, Outdoor Swimming Society (OSS), NHS, CDC, World Triathlon). They are cited as references only. None of them endorses OnlineFitnessWorldwide.

### 4.1 Non-negotiables

- **Never swim alone.** Swim at a lifeguarded venue or an organised session with safety cover (lifeguards, safety boat or kayak). Swim with a buddy as well. Your coach on a video call is not supervision.
- **Follow lifeguard and race rules.** Always. They know the water that day.
- **Local conditions vary.** Tides, currents, wind, boats, water quality, wildlife and temperature change by place and by day. Check local advice before every swim.
- **Have an exit plan.** Know where you get out, and where help is.
- **Know the help signal** your venue or race uses. Signals vary. A common one: raise one hand, shout for help, and float on your back while you wait (RNLI, *Float to Live*).

### 4.2 Before you go

- [ ] **Health check first.** If you have a heart or blood-pressure condition, asthma, diabetes, epilepsy or seizures, are pregnant or postpartum, take regular medication, have a weakened immune system, or have any other medical condition, **see your own doctor before your first cold-water or open-water swim.** Cold water shock raises heart rate and blood pressure and can cause heart attacks, even in the relatively young and healthy (RNLI). It can also trigger panic and asthma attacks (OSS). If health problems or medicines affect your ability to fight germs, check with a healthcare provider before swimming in natural water (CDC).
- [ ] **Tell your coach** about any of the above. We hand it on: **Metabolic Health** (heart, blood pressure, diabetes and other conditions), **Women's Health** (pregnancy, postpartum), **Physio** (injury), **Masters** (roughly 40+). We don't give medical advice or clearance.
- [ ] Venue has lifeguards or a safety boat, and open-water swimming is allowed there.
- [ ] Water quality, advisories and weather checked with local sources, especially after heavy rain (CDC).
- [ ] Safety cover is in place **and** a buddy is with you. Someone on shore knows your plan.
- [ ] Bright swim cap and goggles; wetsuit if allowed and the water calls for it. A brightly coloured tow float where the venue allows.
- [ ] You can swim your planned distance continuously in a pool.
- [ ] Open-water nerves? Tell your coach. Mindset can help. Panic in the water is a safety issue first: practise floating in a supervised pool (RNLI).

### 4.3 Acclimatisation steps (first swims)

1. Walk in slowly. Splash your face and neck.
2. Put your face in and blow bubbles until breathing is calm.
3. First swims are short: a few minutes, close to and parallel to the shore, in standing depth where possible. Get out while you still feel fine. Don't set time targets (OSS).
4. Build time slowly over several sessions, never alone. Your body is not a machine: wind, tiredness, hunger or poor sleep can make you cold faster (OSS).
5. Add skills one at a time: sighting, swimming straight, turning at a buoy, getting in and out.
6. Practise with other swimmers around you only in an organised group session.

### 4.4 Cold water

- **What counts as cold.** Water below 15 °C (59 °F) counts as cold water and can seriously affect your breathing and movement (RNLI). Colder water means shorter swims and more caution. A wetsuit keeps you warmer for longer but does not remove the risk (OSS).
- **Cold shock.** The first minute can make you gasp and breathe fast. Don't try to swim straight away. Float, let your breathing settle, then call for help or swim to safety (RNLI, *Float to Live*).
- **Get out now** if any of these happen (OSS):
  - you can't stop shivering, or you feel numb;
  - you feel clumsy, or your stroke changes, or you start swimming more upright;
  - your arms or legs feel weak or sluggish (this can lead to "swim failure", when you can no longer swim);
  - your hands go claw-like and won't work properly;
  - your jaw clenches, or you have trouble speaking;
  - you feel confused.
  Get out early. Don't wait to "finish the swim".
- **After you get out, you keep cooling.** This is called afterdrop. You can keep cooling for up to about 30 minutes, and are often coldest about 10 minutes after you get out (OSS).
- **Rewarm gradually** (OSS):
  - get out of the wind; remove all wet kit; pat dry;
  - put on dry, warm layers, including a hat;
  - have a warm drink and some food;
  - no hot shower or bath to warm up. If you might be hypothermic, skip warm showers and baths too;
  - don't drive or ride until you are fully warm.
- **Shivering is not a reliable guide.** Violent shivering you can't stop is a warning sign. Shivering that **stops** while you are still cold can mean severe hypothermia, not recovery (OSS).
- **Call your local emergency number** if someone is confused, can't speak clearly, stumbles or can't walk without help, collapses, becomes drowsy or unresponsive, or their shivering stops while they are still cold. Stay with them and keep them out of the wind while help comes.
- **Water temperature depends on the place, the season and the day**, in both hemispheres. Early-season swims are often the coldest of the swim season. Inland water (lakes, rivers, reservoirs) can be colder than the sea, even in summer (RNLI). Check the local water temperature on the day.
- **Wetsuit rules and swim changes are set by each race and governing body** (for example, World Triathlon publishes wetsuit rules and announces the official water temperature before the start). Rules differ by organiser and country. Check your race's rules and the official water temperature on race day (World Triathlon FAQs).

### 4.5 Heat and warm water

- Tropical and Gulf waters (Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, the Arabian Gulf, northern Australia) can be warm year-round. Sun, heat and humidity add load.
- Some races ban wetsuits in warm water. Race rules can also shorten or cancel the swim in some temperature conditions (World Triathlon FAQs). Follow the race.
- Swim early in the day. Cover up on shore. Hydration questions go to Nutrition.
- **Stop, get out of the water and the sun, and cool down** if you feel tired, weak, dizzy or faint, feel sick, have a headache or cramps, or are very thirsty (NHS; CDC). Move to a cool place, remove extra clothing, cool the skin with water, and sip fluids. Someone stays with you.
- **Call your local emergency number now** if someone (NHS; CDC):
  - is confused, slurring words or uncoordinated;
  - has a seizure;
  - collapses or passes out;
  - has fast breathing or a fast heartbeat and feels very unwell;
  - is still unwell after about 30 minutes of resting in a cool place, being cooled and drinking fluids.
  Keep cooling them while help comes.
- **Don't use sweating to decide.** Heat stroke can happen with hot, dry skin **or** heavy sweating (CDC; NHS). Heavy sweating is common in heat exhaustion too (NHS).
- A coach never manages heat illness remotely. It is a stop-and-get-help situation (Coaching Standards rule).

### 4.6 Other hazards to check locally

- **Rip currents** (RNLI): choose a lifeguarded beach and swim between the flags where that system is used. If caught, don't swim against the rip. If you can stand, wade out. If not, swim parallel to the shore until free, then head in. If in difficulty, raise one hand, shout for help and float.
- **Currents and tides:** ask lifeguards or local authorities.
- **Boats, jet skis, paddlers:** swim only in marked or supervised areas.
- **Stay out of the water if** (CDC): signs say the area is closed; the water is cloudier than usual, discoloured or smells bad (possible harmful algal bloom); you see pipes draining into the area; you have diarrhoea; or you have an open cut or wound. Check advisories, especially after heavy rain. Don't swallow the water.
- **Wildlife** (jellyfish and other marine life, animals, weed, rocks) differs completely by country and season. Follow local signs, lifeguards and authorities. We don't give sting or bite first-aid steps; follow local lifeguard or medical advice.
- Glare from low sun when sighting.

## 5. Bike FAQ

Neutral education only. No brands, apps, equipment recommendations or affiliate links. Traffic rules vary by country.

### 5.1 Indoor-trainer sessions

Indoor structure ideas at a general level from Friel & Rutberg, *Ride Inside*.

**Q: Can I do most of my bike training indoors?**
A: Yes, for many clients. Indoor riding is time-efficient and safe from traffic. It suits winter in the north (Canada, Northern Europe), summer heat (the Gulf, parts of Australia and the US South), dark evenings and hotel stays. Do some outdoor skills riding before race day if you can do it safely.

**Q: How do I keep indoor riding comfortable?**
A: A cool room, airflow, a towel, and drinks to hand. Stop if you feel unwell or dizzy.

**Indoor session menu**

| Session | Structure | Zone / RPE |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery spin | 20–40 min very easy, light gear | Z1, RPE 1–2 |
| Endurance | 45–90 min steady | Z2, RPE 3–4 |
| Cadence skills | 6 × 1 min fast, light pedalling, 2 min easy | Z2, RPE 4 |
| Tempo | 2–3 × 10–15 min, 5 min easy | Z3, RPE 5–6 |
| Threshold | 3–4 × 8–10 min, 3–5 min easy | Z4, RPE 7–8 |
| Short high intensity (build phase only) | 5–6 × 2–3 min, equal easy | Z5, RPE 9 |
| Race simulation | Race-length block at planned race effort | Z2–Z4 by distance |

**Q: I travel a lot. What then?**
A: Use a hotel gym bike with RPE only. Keep the session shape. Swap long rides for two shorter rides if needed.

### 5.2 Road safety and bike-handling basics

General ideas from Sumner and the Editors of Bicycling Magazine, *Bicycling Complete Book of Road Cycling Skills*. Citing the book implies no endorsement by Bicycling magazine.

**Q: What are the basics before every ride?**
- Check tyres, brakes, chain and quick releases or axles.
- Wear a helmet. In some countries it is the law.
- Be seen: lights and bright clothing, day and night.
- Tell someone your route.

**Q: What about traffic rules?**
- They vary by country. Learn yours.
- Some countries drive on the left (for example the UK, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Africa). Many drive on the right (for example the US, Canada, most of mainland Europe, much of South America and the Middle East).
- Hand signals and cycle-lane rules differ. Follow local law.

**Q: How do I ride safely around others?**
- Ride predictably. Hold your line.
- Look ahead, not at your front wheel.
- Shoulder-check before you move out.
- Group rides: call out hazards, no sudden braking, keep a safe gap.

**Q: Cornering and descending?**
- Brake before the corner, not in it.
- Outside pedal down, weight on it. Look through the turn.
- On descents, stay relaxed, hands near the brakes, speed you can control.

**Q: Drafting in races?**
- Many age-group triathlons ban drafting on the bike. Some allow it. Read your race rules.

**Q: My knee, back or neck hurts on the bike.**
- Stop and tell us. Pain goes to **Physio**. Position questions may need a qualified local bike fitter. We do not recommend products.

### 5.3 Field tests, explained inside plans

Bike periodisation and test ideas at a general level from Friel's *The Cyclist's Training Bible*.

**Why test?** To set your zones and check progress. Tests are scheduled in lighter weeks (weeks 4 and 8 in most plans).

| Test | How | What it sets |
|---|---|---|
| **Talk test (beginners)** | Note the effort where full sentences become short phrases | Rough Z2/Z3 line by RPE |
| **20-min bike test (indoors)** | Thorough warm-up, then 20 min at the best steady effort you can hold | Estimate of FTP and power zones |
| **30-min solo effort (bike or run)** | 30 min at a strong, even effort; the average HR of the last 20 min estimates threshold HR | HR zones |
| **Timed swim** | After warm-up, a steady timed 400 m (440 yd) swim | Swim pace guide alongside RPE |
| **Run zones** | Run Coach chooses the test | Run zones |

**Test safety**
- Not when ill, injured or poorly slept.
- Indoors or on a closed, quiet route. Never in traffic.
- Clients with any medical question get clinician clearance first.
- New clients and first-timers may use the talk test only.
- Power files and HR data are personal data. Privacy & Data Care sets how we collect and store them.

## 6. Transition, taper and race-day checklists

### 6.1 Transition practice

**T1 (swim to bike) set-up**
- [ ] Bike racked, in an easy gear for the start.
- [ ] Helmet open, sunglasses inside, on the bars or by the bike.
- [ ] Bike shoes and race number belt ready.
- [ ] Small towel to stand on.

**T2 (bike to run) set-up**
- [ ] Run shoes with easy laces, cap or visor.
- [ ] Number on the front for the run (if race rules require).

**Practice drills (once a week in build, 10–15 min)**
- Wetsuit off: unzip and pull to the waist while walking, then off at the rack.
- Helmet on and buckled **before** you touch the bike (a common race rule; check yours).
- Walk or run the bike to the mount line; practise mounting and dismounting at the line.
- Short bricks: bike 10 min, run 5 min, repeat 2–3 times.
- Walk the transition area the day before the race. Learn your way in and out.

### 6.2 Race-week taper

Taper principles at a general level from Mujika's *Tapering and Peaking for Optimal Performance*: cut volume, keep some intensity and keep training frequency.

| Race | Taper length (guide) |
|---|---|
| Sprint / Olympic / duathlon | about 7–10 days |
| 70.3 | about 10–14 days |
| Full distance | about 2–3 weeks |

**Rules**
- Volume down. A few short race-effort pieces stay in.
- Nothing new: no new kit, foods, sessions or shoes.
- No "test" long session in race week.
- Last heavy strength about 7–10 days out (Strength Coach).
- Sleep and travel come first. Crossing many time zones (for example Asia to Europe, or North America to Australia)? Arrive early if you can and adjust sleep gradually.

**Sample Olympic race week (race on Sunday)**

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Rest or easy swim 20 min |
| Tue | Bike 45 min with 3 × 3 min race effort (Z3–Z4) |
| Wed | Swim 30 min with 4 × 100 m (110 yd) race effort; easy run 20 min |
| Thu | Run 30 min with 3 × 2 min race effort |
| Fri | Rest. Pack kit (checklist below) |
| Sat | Short swim, bike and run, 10–15 min each, easy with a few short pick-ups. Rack bike if the race requires |
| Sun | Race |

### 6.3 Race-day checklist

**Kit**
- [ ] Race number, timing chip, photo ID, race-pack items
- [ ] Swim: cap, goggles (plus a spare), wetsuit if allowed
- [ ] Bike: helmet, shoes, bottles, tyres pumped, spare tube and tools you know how to use
- [ ] Run: shoes, cap or visor, number belt
- [ ] Sun protection, warm layers for before and after
- [ ] Fuel and drinks as planned with Nutrition

**Morning**
- [ ] Wake early enough for your planned breakfast (Nutrition sets the plan)
- [ ] Arrive early; set up transition; walk the in and out routes
- [ ] Easy warm-up; short swim warm-up if allowed
- [ ] Listen to the race briefing

**Pacing by effort**
- **Swim:** start controlled (RPE 5–6). Find your rhythm. Sight often.
- **Bike:** sprint and Olympic mostly Z3 (RPE 5–7); 70.3 and longer mostly Z2–Z3. Do not chase other riders.
- **Run:** first part easy-steady, then build if you feel good.
- If you feel unwell at any point: slow down, stop, and tell race staff.

### 6.4 Fueling: handed to Nutrition

- Race fueling, hydration, carbohydrate, electrolytes, caffeine, plant-based fueling and any supplement question go to **Nutrition**.
- Triathlon Coach never advises on supplements.
- Background for Nutrition: Larson-Meyer & Ruscigno, *Plant-Based Sports Nutrition*.

### 6.5 Red flags: handed to Physio and the client's clinician

Background for Physio and clinicians only: Migliorini (ed.), *Triathlon Medicine*. We do not diagnose or treat.

**Stop and get local emergency help now (call your local emergency number):**
- Chest pain, pressure or tightness
- Fainting or collapse
- Severe breathlessness, or breathlessness out of proportion to effort
- Racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat with dizziness
- Confusion, slurred speech, or not making sense
- Heat-illness emergency signs (see 4.5) or severe cold (shivering that stops while still cold, confusion, drowsiness; see 4.4)
- Swim distress in open water: signal for help, float, wait for rescue

**Stop and hand to Physio / clinician before training continues:**
- Pain that changes how you walk, run, swim or pedal
- Pain that gets worse during a session or lasts into the next day
- Joint swelling, numbness, tingling or weakness
- Shoulder pain when swimming; knee pain when cycling
- Severe headache, nausea or vomiting during or after a long session
- Any return after injury, illness or surgery
- Pregnancy, postpartum or menstrual-cycle questions go to **Women's Health**

## 1. Multisport principles: one page for clients who also lift twice a week

Three sports. Two strength sessions. One life.
The plan has to fit the week, not the other way round.

### 1.1 Weekly time budget

Start from the hours you really have. Then add strength on top.

| Goal | Endurance time per week (peak weeks) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| First sprint | about 3.5–5 h | 2 × 45–60 min | Low-volume style, in the spirit of Friel's *Your First Triathlon* |
| Olympic | about 6–9 h | 2 × 45–60 min | Short-course focus (Foster & Bolton) |
| 70.3 (half distance) | about 8–12 h | 2 sessions, moving to 1–2 maintenance | Long weekend sessions |
| Full distance (long course) | about 10–15+ h | 1–2 maintenance | Work and family balance (Friel & Byrn, *Going Long*) |
| Duathlon | similar to the matching triathlon, minus swim time | 2 × 45–60 min | Run impact needs care |

These are planning ranges, not targets. A busy week means a smaller week. That is fine.

### 1.2 Easy versus quality: about 80/20 across all three sports

- Count training **time**, across swim, bike and run together.
- About 80% easy (Zone 1–2). About 20% quality (Zone 3 and above).
- Easy means truly easy. You can talk in full sentences.
- Most athletes drift into a "grey middle" effort every day. That middle effort tires you without building much. (*80/20 Endurance*, Fitzgerald; *80/20 Running*, Fitzgerald.)
- For first-timers, "quality" can be short pick-ups or technique work, not hard intervals.
- Strength sessions sit outside this count. Strength Coach owns them.

### 1.3 Progression limits

- Raise total weekly time by **no more than about 10%** a week. This is a rule of thumb, not a law.
- Change **one variable at a time**: duration, or frequency, or intensity. Never all three.
- Grow the long session in one sport at a time.
- Every 3rd or 4th week is a **lighter week**: about a third less time, same habits.
- Technique before volume in the water. Add metres (yards) only when the stroke holds.
- Two easy weeks of base before any hard intervals.

### 1.4 Missed-session rules

- **Missed one session?** Let it go. Do not cram it in.
- **Missed 2–4 days?** Pick up the plan where it is today. Drop the missed quality session.
- **Missed 5–7 days?** Repeat the previous week before moving on.
- **Missed 2+ weeks?** Tell your coach. We step the plan back and rewrite it.
- **Never** stack two hard sessions on one day to "catch up".
- **Short on time this week?** Keep the one key session per sport. Cut easy volume first.
- **Ill or injured?** No training through fever or chest symptoms. Talk to your clinician. Pain goes to Physio before you resume.

### 1.5 Sequencing with two strength sessions

The goal: heavy leg days and hard bike or run days never collide.
Treat interference as a scheduling problem (*The Hybrid Athlete*, Viada; *Concurrent Aerobic and Strength Training*, Schumann & Rønnestad, eds.).

- **Strength A = heavy lower body.** Strength B = upper-body focus with light legs.
- Keep Strength A **at least 48 hours** (two days) before the week's hardest bike or run.
- The day after Strength A: easy swim, easy spin or rest only.
- **Experienced clients only:** a hard session and a lift can share one day. Hard endurance first. Lift later, with as many hours between as possible (aim for 6+ h).
- Swim quality and heavy upper-body pulling should not sit back to back. Space them by a day where possible.
- As the race nears, strength shifts to **maintenance** (fewer sets, same quality). Strength Coach sets the details.
- Race week: last heavy strength session usually 7–10 days out, agreed with Strength Coach.

**Template week shape (used by all plans below)**

## 2. Plan templates

### 2.0 Zones used in every plan

We use a simple 5-zone scale. Your coach turns your field tests (section 5.3) into HR and power ranges. Devices and formulas vary, so RPE always comes first (*Heart Rate Training*, Benson & Connolly).

| Zone | RPE (1–10) | One-line meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Z1 Recovery** | 1–2 | Very light. Loosening up. |
| **Z2 Easy / aerobic** | 3–4 | Full sentences. Most of your week lives here. |
| **Z3 Steady / tempo** | 5–6 | Short phrases only. Useful race effort for longer events; use sparingly in training. |
| **Z4 Threshold** | 7–8 | A few words. Hard but controlled. |
| **Z5 High intensity** | 9 | One or two words. Short repeats only. |

- **HR zones:** set from a threshold heart-rate field test, by sport (run and bike HR differ).
- **Power zones:** set from FTP, bike only, if the client already has power.
- **Swim:** RPE plus a pace from a timed swim test (section 5.3).
- **Run zones:** Run Coach sets and checks these (background: *Daniels' Running Formula*).

### 2.5 Duathlon variant (run-bike-run)

Use the matching triathlon plan (sprint or Olympic) and change three things:

1. **Swim sessions go.** Replace one with an easy bike (Z2, 45–60 min). Replace the other with rest or mobility.
2. **Run-bike-run bricks.** For example: run 10 min / bike 30 min / run 5 min, all easy at first, then the first run at race effort in build.
3. **Run load stays careful.** Running is high impact. Total run time grows slowly. Run Coach checks run quality sessions.

**Sample sprint-duathlon week (build)**

| Day | Session | Zone / RPE |

| Mon | Strength A + rest | — |

## 3. Remote swim-video review protocol

We coach from client-filmed video. We never supervise swimming in person or in real time.

### 3.1 Before filming: permission, consent and privacy

- **Ask the pool first.** Many pools restrict or ban cameras and phones. If filming is not allowed, we do not film. We use a written self-check instead.
- **Client consent.** The client agrees in writing that we may view and keep the video. Privacy & Data Care sets how it is sent, stored and deleted.
- **No one else identifiable.** Film at a quiet time, in an end lane if possible. Frame tightly on the swimmer.
- **Never film children** or anyone who has not agreed. If someone else is in shot, re-film or crop before sending.
- **Never post** client swim video anywhere. Video / Social only use it with separate written consent and Susmit's approval.

### 3.2 Filming instructions

A friend films from the deck. The swimmer swims easy, then moderate.

| Angle | How | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Side view, from the deck** | Filmer walks level with the swimmer at mid-pool. Phone held at hip height, landscape. Film one length each way: 25 m / 27 yd | Body line, head, rotation, kick, timing |
| **2. Front view, from the end** | Filmer stands or crouches at the end wall. Swimmer swims towards the camera for one length | Hand entry, crossover, breathing, symmetry |
| **3. Underwater side view (only if pool rules allow)** | Phone in a waterproof case the client already owns, held just below the surface from the deck edge. The filmer stays on the deck and never gets in to film | Catch, pull path, kick depth |

- 2 lengths easy, 1 length moderate, per angle.
- Good light. Hold the phone steady. No zoom.
- Note the pool length (25 m / 27 yd, 50 m / 55 yd, or 25 yd / 23 m) and how the swimmer breathes (every 2 or 3 strokes).

### 3.3 The 8-point stroke checklist

Each point is scored green (good), amber (working on it) or red (priority).

1. **Head and body line.** Head neutral, eyes down and slightly forward, hips near the surface.
2. **Breathing.** Turn the head with the body roll; one goggle stays in the water; breathe out steadily under water.
3. **Rotation.** Shoulders and hips roll together, side to side.
4. **Hand entry.** Fingertips first, in line with the shoulder, no crossing the midline.
5. **Reach and catch.** Long reach forward, then the forearm angles down and starts pressing water back.
6. **Pull and finish.** Press back past the hip; no pushing down or lifting water up.
7. **Arm recovery.** Relaxed arm, elbow leads, hand close to the body.
8. **Kick and timing.** Small, steady kick from the hip, legs close to the surface; a smooth rhythm with no long pauses.

### 3.4 Drill menu

Balance first, a long streamlined body and quiet, unhurried strokes follow the general approach of Laughlin's *Total Immersion*. Drills below are attributed at a general level only.

| Drill | What it builds | Checklist point | Easier version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Push-and-glide (arms forward, face down) | Body line, relaxation | 1 | Short glides from the wall, stand often |
| Side kick (one arm forward, on your side) | Balance, rotation, breathing position | 1, 2, 3 | Kickboard under the lead arm |
| Six-kick switch (side kick, then one stroke to the other side) | Rotation and timing | 3, 8 | Fewer kicks, more rest |
| Catch-up (one hand waits in front until the other arrives) | Entry, reach | 4, 5 | Hold a kickboard as the "waiting" hand |
| Fingertip drag | Relaxed, high-elbow recovery | 7 | Slow pace, short lengths |
| Front sculling (small figure-of-eight hand movements) | Feel for the water at the catch | 5 | Sculling while standing in shallow water |
| Single-arm swimming | Pull path and finish | 5, 6 | Other arm resting at the side or on a board |
| Breathing every 3 strokes (bilateral) | Symmetry, calm breathing | 2 | Every 2 strokes, alternate sides each length |
| Stroke-count lengths (count strokes per length, try to swim "quietly") | Efficiency, rhythm | all | Count only, no target |
| Head-up sighting (lift eyes forward every 6–8 strokes) | Open-water skill | 1, 2 | Every 10 strokes, then build |

### 3.5 Review flow

1. Watch every angle. Score the 8 points.
2. Pick **1–2 priorities**. Never more.
3. Give **2–3 drills** from the menu that match those priorities.
4. Client does the drills in 2–3 swims a week, for 2–3 weeks. Drills take about 10–15 min of each swim.
5. **Re-film** the same angles. Compare. Pick the next priority.

Feedback is short, plain and kind. Adult-onset swimmers are welcome. We never shame pace, body or ability.

### 3.6 Dry-land options

Anatomy-based swim strength ideas at a general level from McLeod's *Swimming Anatomy*. Strength Coach fits these into Strength A or B, or adds a 10–15 min block 2–3 times a week.

- Band pull-aparts (upper back).
- Band external rotation (rotator cuff).
- Prone Y-T-W raises (shoulder blades).
- Lat pulldown or assisted pull-up (pulling strength).
- Push-up plus (shoulder blade control).
- Dead bug and side plank (trunk control).
- Glute bridge (hips, for the kick and the bike).
- Thoracic rotation and ankle mobility.

### 3.7 Shoulder pain goes to Physio

- Any shoulder pain, clicking with pain, numbness or weakness: stop the drill or set and hand to **Physio**.
- Common triggers to watch for: a sudden jump in swim volume, lots of hard pull sets, or new equipment. We flag; we do not diagnose.
- Background reading for Physio only: Ellenbecker & Wilk, *Sport Therapy for the Shoulder*.

## 4. Open-water safety and acclimatisation checklist

> **FOR COACHING STANDARDS REVIEW.** Draft only. Not for clients until Coaching Standards and Susmit sign it off.
> We educate. We never supervise open-water swimming, in person or remotely.
> Background: Lynne Cox, *Open Water Swimming Manual* (general-level ideas only).

### 4.1 Non-negotiables

- **Never swim alone.** Swim at an organised, lifeguarded session or with a safety boat or kayak.
- **Follow lifeguard and race rules.** Always. They know the water that day.
- **Local conditions vary.** Tides, currents, wind, boats, water quality, wildlife and temperature change by place and by day. Check local advice before every swim.
- **Have an exit plan.** Know where you get out, and where help is.
- **Know the help signal** your venue or race uses (often one arm raised and waved). Float on your back while you wait.

### 4.2 Before you go

- [ ] Venue has lifeguards or a safety boat, and open-water swimming is allowed there.
- [ ] Water quality and weather checked with local sources.
- [ ] A buddy is with you, and someone on shore knows your plan.
- [ ] Bright swim cap and goggles; wetsuit if allowed and the water calls for it. A brightly coloured tow float where the venue allows.
- [ ] You can swim your planned distance continuously in a pool.
- [ ] Open-water nerves? Tell your coach. Mindset can help.

### 4.3 Acclimatisation steps (first swims)

1. Walk in slowly. Splash your face and neck.
2. Put your face in and blow bubbles until breathing is calm.
3. First swims are short: about 5–10 min, parallel to the shore, in standing depth where possible.
4. Build time slowly over several sessions, never alone.
5. Add skills one at a time: sighting, swimming straight, turning at a buoy, getting in and out.
6. Practise with other swimmers around you only in an organised group session.

### 4.4 Cold water

- The first minute can take your breath away (cold shock). Stay calm, float, breathe slowly, then swim.
- Many swimmers find water below about 15 °C (59 °F) cold. Colder water needs shorter swims and more caution.
- Get out if you shiver hard, feel clumsy, confused or numb.
- Rewarm gradually: dry off, warm layers, warm drink. Do not drive until fully warm.
- Race wetsuit rules are set by the race. Check them.
- Seasons differ: Northern Hemisphere lakes and seas (Europe, North America, Japan) are often coldest in spring and early summer swims; Southern Hemisphere waters (Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Chile, Argentina) warm up from around November.

### 4.5 Heat and warm water

- Tropical and Gulf waters (Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, the Arabian Gulf, northern Australia) can be warm year-round. Sun, heat and humidity add load.
- Some races ban wetsuits in warm water. Follow race rules.
- Swim early in the day. Cover up on shore. Hydration questions go to Nutrition.
- Heat illness signs (feeling faint, confused, nausea, headache, stopping sweating): **stop, get out, cool down and get help.** Exertional heat illness is a stop-and-get-help situation, never managed by a coach remotely (Coaching Standards rule; background *Sport and Physical Activity in the Heat*, Casa, ed.).

### 4.6 Other hazards to check locally

- Currents and tides; rip currents at beaches.
- Boats, jet skis, paddlers.
- Jellyfish, weed, rocks, wildlife.
- Poor water quality after heavy rain.
- Glare from low sun when sighting.

## 5. Bike FAQ

Neutral education only. No brands, apps, equipment recommendations or affiliate links. Traffic rules vary by country.

### 5.1 Indoor-trainer sessions

Indoor structure ideas at a general level from Friel & Rutberg, *Ride Inside*.

**Q: Can I do most of my bike training indoors?**
A: Yes, for many clients. Indoor riding is time-efficient and safe from traffic. It suits winter in the north (Canada, Northern Europe), summer heat (the Gulf, parts of Australia and the US South), dark evenings and hotel stays. Do some outdoor skills riding before race day if you can do it safely.

**Q: How do I keep indoor riding comfortable?**
A: A cool room, airflow, a towel, and drinks to hand. Stop if you feel unwell or dizzy.

**Indoor session menu**

| Session | Structure | Zone / RPE |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery spin | 20–40 min very easy, light gear | Z1, RPE 1–2 |
| Endurance | 45–90 min steady | Z2, RPE 3–4 |
| Cadence skills | 6 × 1 min fast, light pedalling, 2 min easy | Z2, RPE 4 |
| Tempo | 2–3 × 10–15 min, 5 min easy | Z3, RPE 5–6 |
| Threshold | 3–4 × 8–10 min, 3–5 min easy | Z4, RPE 7–8 |
| Short high intensity (build phase only) | 5–6 × 2–3 min, equal easy | Z5, RPE 9 |
| Race simulation | Race-length block at planned race effort | Z2–Z4 by distance |

**Q: I travel a lot. What then?**
A: Use a hotel gym bike with RPE only. Keep the session shape. Swap long rides for two shorter rides if needed.

### 5.2 Road safety and bike-handling basics

General ideas from Sumner and the Editors of Bicycling Magazine, *Bicycling Complete Book of Road Cycling Skills*. Citing the book implies no endorsement by Bicycling magazine.

**Q: What are the basics before every ride?**
- Check tyres, brakes, chain and quick releases or axles.
- Wear a helmet. In some countries it is the law.
- Be seen: lights and bright clothing, day and night.
- Tell someone your route.

**Q: What about traffic rules?**
- They vary by country. Learn yours.
- Some countries drive on the left (for example the UK, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Africa). Many drive on the right (for example the US, Canada, most of mainland Europe, much of South America and the Middle East).
- Hand signals and cycle-lane rules differ. Follow local law.

**Q: How do I ride safely around others?**
- Ride predictably. Hold your line.
- Look ahead, not at your front wheel.
- Shoulder-check before you move out.
- Group rides: call out hazards, no sudden braking, keep a safe gap.

**Q: Cornering and descending?**
- Brake before the corner, not in it.
- Outside pedal down, weight on it. Look through the turn.
- On descents, stay relaxed, hands near the brakes, speed you can control.

**Q: Drafting in races?**
- Many age-group triathlons ban drafting on the bike. Some allow it. Read your race rules.

**Q: My knee, back or neck hurts on the bike.**
- Stop and tell us. Pain goes to **Physio**. Position questions may need a qualified local bike fitter. We do not recommend products.

### 5.3 Field tests, explained inside plans

Bike periodisation and test ideas at a general level from Friel's *The Cyclist's Training Bible*.

**Why test?** To set your zones and check progress. Tests are scheduled in lighter weeks (weeks 4 and 8 in most plans).

| Test | How | What it sets |
|---|---|---|
| **Talk test (beginners)** | Note the effort where full sentences become short phrases | Rough Z2/Z3 line by RPE |
| **20-min bike test (indoors)** | Thorough warm-up, then 20 min at the best steady effort you can hold | Estimate of FTP and power zones |
| **30-min solo effort (bike or run)** | 30 min at a strong, even effort; the average HR of the last 20 min estimates threshold HR | HR zones |
| **Timed swim** | After warm-up, a steady timed 400 m (440 yd) swim | Swim pace guide alongside RPE |
| **Run zones** | Run Coach chooses the test | Run zones |

**Test safety**
- Not when ill, injured or poorly slept.
- Indoors or on a closed, quiet route. Never in traffic.
- Clients with any medical question get clinician clearance first.
- New clients and first-timers may use the talk test only.
- Power files and HR data are personal data. Privacy & Data Care sets how we collect and store them.

## 6. Transition, taper and race-day checklists

### 6.1 Transition practice

**T1 (swim to bike) set-up**
- [ ] Bike racked, in an easy gear for the start.
- [ ] Helmet open, sunglasses inside, on the bars or by the bike.
- [ ] Bike shoes and race number belt ready.
- [ ] Small towel to stand on.

**T2 (bike to run) set-up**
- [ ] Run shoes with easy laces, cap or visor.
- [ ] Number on the front for the run (if race rules require).

**Practice drills (once a week in build, 10–15 min)**
- Wetsuit off: unzip and pull to the waist while walking, then off at the rack.
- Helmet on and buckled **before** you touch the bike (a common race rule; check yours).
- Walk or run the bike to the mount line; practise mounting and dismounting at the line.
- Short bricks: bike 10 min, run 5 min, repeat 2–3 times.
- Walk the transition area the day before the race. Learn your way in and out.

### 6.2 Race-week taper

Taper principles at a general level from Mujika's *Tapering and Peaking for Optimal Performance*: cut volume, keep some intensity and keep training frequency.

| Race | Taper length (guide) |
|---|---|
| Sprint / Olympic / duathlon | about 7–10 days |
| 70.3 | about 10–14 days |
| Full distance | about 2–3 weeks |

**Rules**
- Volume down. A few short race-effort pieces stay in.
- Nothing new: no new kit, foods, sessions or shoes.
- No "test" long session in race week.
- Last heavy strength about 7–10 days out (Strength Coach).
- Sleep and travel come first. Crossing many time zones (for example Asia to Europe, or North America to Australia)? Arrive early if you can and adjust sleep gradually.

**Sample Olympic race week (race on Sunday)**

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Rest or easy swim 20 min |
| Tue | Bike 45 min with 3 × 3 min race effort (Z3–Z4) |
| Wed | Swim 30 min with 4 × 100 m (110 yd) race effort; easy run 20 min |
| Thu | Run 30 min with 3 × 2 min race effort |
| Fri | Rest. Pack kit (checklist below) |
| Sat | Short swim, bike and run, 10–15 min each, easy with a few short pick-ups. Rack bike if the race requires |
| Sun | Race |

### 6.3 Race-day checklist

**Kit**
- [ ] Race number, timing chip, photo ID, race-pack items
- [ ] Swim: cap, goggles (plus a spare), wetsuit if allowed
- [ ] Bike: helmet, shoes, bottles, tyres pumped, spare tube and tools you know how to use
- [ ] Run: shoes, cap or visor, number belt
- [ ] Sun protection, warm layers for before and after
- [ ] Fuel and drinks as planned with Nutrition

**Morning**
- [ ] Wake early enough for your planned breakfast (Nutrition sets the plan)
- [ ] Arrive early; set up transition; walk the in and out routes
- [ ] Easy warm-up; short swim warm-up if allowed
- [ ] Listen to the race briefing

**Pacing by effort**
- **Swim:** start controlled (RPE 5–6). Find your rhythm. Sight often.
- **Bike:** sprint and Olympic mostly Z3 (RPE 5–7); 70.3 and longer mostly Z2–Z3. Do not chase other riders.
- **Run:** first part easy-steady, then build if you feel good.
- If you feel unwell at any point: slow down, stop, and tell race staff.

### 6.4 Fueling: handed to Nutrition

- Race fueling, hydration, carbohydrate, electrolytes, caffeine, plant-based fueling and any supplement question go to **Nutrition**.
- Triathlon Coach never advises on supplements.
- Background for Nutrition: Larson-Meyer & Ruscigno, *Plant-Based Sports Nutrition*.

### 6.5 Red flags: handed to Physio and the client's clinician

Background for Physio and clinicians only: Migliorini (ed.), *Triathlon Medicine*. We do not diagnose or treat.

**Stop and get local emergency help now (call your local emergency number):**
- Chest pain, pressure or tightness
- Fainting or collapse
- Severe breathlessness, or breathlessness out of proportion to effort
- Racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat with dizziness
- Confusion, slurred speech, or not making sense
- Signs of heat illness (see 4.5) or severe cold (hard shivering stopping, confusion)
- Swim distress in open water: signal for help, float, wait for rescue

**Stop and hand to Physio / clinician before training continues:**
- Pain that changes how you walk, run, swim or pedal
- Pain that gets worse during a session or lasts into the next day
- Joint swelling, numbness, tingling or weakness
- Shoulder pain when swimming; knee pain when cycling
- Severe headache, nausea or vomiting during or after a long session
- Any return after injury, illness or surgery
- Pregnancy, postpartum or menstrual-cycle questions go to **Women's Health**
