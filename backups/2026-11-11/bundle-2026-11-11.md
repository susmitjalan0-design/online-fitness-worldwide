# Metabolic Health Coaching Guide v1

**OnlineFitnessWorldwide — DRAFT for Susmit’s review**  
**Status:** Coach-education draft only. Not for clients, publication, or use in live sessions until Susmit has reviewed it.  
**Date:** 11 November 2026 (Asia/Calcutta · IST)  
**Audience:** OnlineFitnessWorldwide coaches working with a worldwide live-online strength and conditioning and hybrid-race (HYROX, DEKA, ATHX) clientele (US, UK, Europe, Middle East, Asia, Australia and other regions). India is one market among many, not the default.

---

> **Read this first.** OnlineFitnessWorldwide coaches are coaches, not clinicians. This guide does not diagnose, treat, clear, cure or prescribe. It does not replace the client’s doctor, diabetes care team, or other qualified clinician. The clinician manages the condition and any medication, monitoring or dosing decisions. The coach adapts the session — load, exercise choice, rest, effort and length — within advice the client already has. Do not invent blood-sugar, blood-pressure or heart-rate cut-offs, do not advise on medication, and do not promise that training will reverse or cure a condition.
>
> This is a starting framework of principles, screening questions and sample sessions. It is not an individual training plan, a medical protocol, or a promise of any outcome. Citing ACSM, ADA, NSCA, WHO, AHA or any other body never implies their endorsement of OnlineFitnessWorldwide.

---

## 1. Who this is for, and the clinician–coach split

This guide is for OnlineFitnessWorldwide coaches who support **adults living with, or at risk of, type 2 diabetes, prediabetes, high blood pressure, raised cholesterol, or weight-related cardiometabolic risk**. Use it to ask better questions, adapt live online sessions conservatively, and hand the right issues to the right teammate or to the client’s own clinician.

It is not a diagnosis tool, a treatment plan, a diet, or a race plan. It is not written for coaching minors; youth coaching is outside this guide’s scope. Gestational diabetes, PCOS and menopause-related questions are handed off (see section 8). Type 1 diabetes may appear among active or hybrid-race clients; the same clinician–coach split applies, and any insulin, carbohydrate or race-day glucose plan stays with the care team and Nutrition — it is not designed here.

**The working rule is: the client’s doctor or diabetes care team manages the condition; the coach adapts the session.**

That is not a blanket “everyone must obtain medical clearance before they can train with us” rule, and it is not a blanket “no one needs clinical input” rule. Public screening models differ. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) 2016 position statement, opened on 11 November 2026 (Asia/Calcutta) at the official *Diabetes Care* page, states that pre-exercise medical clearance is generally unnecessary for asymptomatic people before low- or moderate-intensity activity that does not exceed brisk walking or everyday living, and it describes a stricter ACSM preparticipation model as more conservative than the ADA view. A WHO brief on physical activity for people living with type 2 diabetes, extracted the same day from the official IRIS file, uses similar “generally unnecessary for light or moderate activity without contraindications” language, and also notes that people may wish to consult a health professional or activity specialist about what is appropriate for their needs, complications, medicines and overall care plan.

OnlineFitnessWorldwide does not resolve that debate with a house medical protocol, and does not set individual clinical thresholds (for example a glucose, ketone, blood-pressure or heart-rate number at which everyone may or may not train). Instead:

- If the client’s clinician has already given limits, restrictions, a clearance, or a plan for sessions, **follow that advice** and adapt the session inside it.
- If advice is missing, unclear, or has been withdrawn, **do not invent a substitute**. Help the client prepare questions for their own care team, keep the session conservative or wait, and only progress when the client has guidance they can actually use.
- A coach’s screening form is not clearance. A teammate, including Physio, is not a licensed clinician and cannot provide medical clearance.
- Local care pathways differ around the world. Use the client’s own doctor, diabetes educator, nurse practitioner, family physician or specialist team. Do not assume one country’s screening pathway applies everywhere.

This split follows the coaching frame summarised in the knowledge-base takeaways for Colberg’s clinician guide: the clinician manages the condition (including medicines and monitoring); the coach adapts the work.

## 2. Opt-in onboarding screening questions

Ask these questions only with the client’s opt-in, in a private conversation or a written intake they have agreed to complete. Diagnosed conditions and medicines are health information. Explain why you are asking, let the client skip anything they do not want to share, and never press for a medical history they have not offered.

A warm way to open:

> “If you are comfortable, I would like to ask a few questions so I can coach you safely. You can skip any question. I am a coach, not a clinician, so I will not diagnose anything you tell me, and I will not advise on your medicines. Your doctor or diabetes care team manages your condition. I adapt the session around the guidance you already have. If something needs a clinician, I will say so.”

Use the answers to adapt the session, wait for clearer clinical advice, or stop and refer. Do not use them to give a diagnosis, a medical go-ahead, a medication change, or a guarantee that training is safe.

Re-ask when training is about to change (new intensity, longer sessions, running, or hybrid-race stations), after any care-team update, and whenever the client reports a change. Screening is a conversation, not a one-time pass.

### Distinguish two responses

Do not use a modified set to “see if it settles.” If in doubt, stop.

**1. Emergency response — get local emergency help now.**  
Use this when the client collapses, becomes unresponsive or is having a seizure; when they cannot speak in a coherent way or look severely unwell; or when they (or you) believe they are in immediate danger. New chest pain or pressure, suspected heart attack or stroke signs, and severe or sudden unexplained breathlessness also require emergency assessment even if the client is conscious and speaking; do not put these in the later-review category. Stop immediately. Stay on the live call if it is safe to do so, while they or someone with them contacts **local** emergency services. Do not wait for a coach, teammate or later clinical appointment. Do not give medication, do not give dosing instructions, and do not leave the call until help is in motion or another responsible person is clearly with them. Afterward, do not resume training until the client’s own clinician has reviewed what happened and advised that training may continue.

**2. Stop the session and arrange clinical review.**  
Use this for concerning symptoms that are not emergency warning signs. Being conscious and able to communicate does not rule out an emergency. If urgency is uncertain, seek urgent assessment rather than deferring to a later appointment. Stop the work. Do not re-test with another repetition. The client should contact their doctor, diabetes care team, or the urgent pathway that team has already given them. If they already have a clinician-given plan for a particular situation (for example a plan they were taught for low blood sugar), they follow **that** plan; the coach does not invent, override or complete it. Resume or progress only after the client has sought the appropriate review and been advised that it is appropriate to train.

If the picture is unclear, treat it as the more urgent of the two. A teammate’s educational note is not that clinical advice.

### Remote-session emergency readiness

Live online coaching is remote. The coach cannot provide hands-on care and cannot assume a single emergency number.

Before harder or new work, and as a regular habit:

- With consent and secure handling, confirm the current training location precisely enough for emergency responders to find the client, and agree a reachable local emergency contact where possible. Country and city alone are not enough to dispatch help. Reconfirm when the client travels; collect only what the emergency plan needs.
- Confirm the client knows **how to reach local emergency services** where they are. Numbers and pathways differ worldwide. Do not assume one country’s number.
- Prefer that a phone is charged and within reach, and that the camera shows the person, not only the equipment.
- If it is practical, ask whether someone else could be in the home or easily reachable. This is a readiness habit, not a rule that people who live alone cannot train.
- Have water to hand, a clear floor, and any supplies the client’s **own clinician has already told them to have ready** for training. Coaches do not specify doses, tablet counts, or target readings.
- Agree a lost-connection emergency plan before training. If the connection drops during suspected immediate danger, activate local emergency help and the agreed emergency contact without delaying for repeated reconnection attempts. Follow emergency-dispatcher instructions; reconnect if possible while help is being arranged.

This is coaching readiness, not a medical emergency protocol and not a substitute for local emergency services.

## 4. Session principles (principles, not a medical protocol)

These principles are for adapting live online sessions. They are not a prescription, a treatment dose, or a promise of better lab results.

### Why both strength and aerobic work matter

Public guidelines opened for this draft support including **both** aerobic activity and muscle-strengthening work, rather than treating one as a complete substitute for the other.

- The ADA 2016 position statement recommends that adults with type 2 diabetes ideally do both aerobic and resistance training for glycemic and health outcomes, and that most adults with diabetes do 150 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous activity a week, spread over at least three days, with no more than two days in a row without activity, plus two to three resistance sessions on non-consecutive days. It also notes that combined training has been superior to either type alone for glycemic control, citing trials including Sigal et al. (2007) and Church et al. (2010).
- The WHO 2020 guidelines, and the WHO type 2 diabetes brief extracted from the official IRIS file, recommend regular activity for adults living with type 2 diabetes, including 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity a week (or 75–150 minutes vigorous, or an equivalent mix) and muscle-strengthening work involving major muscle groups on two or more days a week, plus less sedentary time.

Speak about this as a reason to keep both kinds of work in the week, not as a claim that coaching will treat, reverse or cure diabetes, high blood pressure, raised cholesterol or weight-related risk. Individual clinical targets stay with the clinician.

The ADA statement also supports breaking up long sitting with brief light activity, at least for adults with type 2 diabetes, **in addition to** structured exercise, not instead of it. Short movement breaks (an easy walk, sit-to-stands) can be suggested as a daily habit.

### Time-efficient options for busy and travelling clients

Short sessions still count. WHO’s brief, in the text extracted for this draft, notes that when people cannot meet the full recommendation they should still do what they can, starting with small amounts and building frequency, intensity or duration over time. “Every move counts” is a fair public-health message; it is not a promise of a particular clinical result from a five-minute workout.

Useful options for busy or travelling clients, all of them adaptations rather than a special medical protocol:

- Keep the OnlineFitnessWorldwide short-session shape: a brief prepare, **one main lift**, **one related accessory in the rest between main-lift sets**, then a small amount of core and a check-in (see section 6).
- Use hotel- and living-room-friendly kit: body weight, one or two dumbbells, a backpack, a band, a stable chair.
- Brisk walking and easy cycling remain valid aerobic options when a gym is not available.
- Short interval work is **one option**, not a replacement for strength work and not a promised shortcut. The open-access editorial for the Little, Karstoft and Safdar collection (the readable content of that reading-list volume) discusses interval training, including walking intervals, as a time-efficient approach under study for people with or at risk of type 2 diabetes. Gibala and Shulgan’s *The One-Minute Workout* is on the reading list for the same practical reason; **full text was not reviewed**, so treat it as a reading lead, not as a protocol to copy. The ADA 2016 statement describes low-volume high-intensity interval training as an alternative to continuous aerobic work for **some** people, and also says safety and efficacy remain unclear for some adults with diabetes; people who want to do it should be clinically stable, already used to regular moderate exercise, and likely supervised at least at first. Do not start a previously inactive or recently unwell client on all-out intervals, and do not sell “one-minute workouts” as a cure.

### Effort guidance

Use plain coaching language, not a clinical exercise-test protocol.

- Most working sets should leave the client able to complete the planned repetitions with control and **one or more repetitions in reserve**. The last repetition should not be a grind that breaks form or forces a breath hold.
- A useful aerobic check is the talk test: at a moderate effort the client can still speak in short sentences. If they cannot, ease off. This is a coaching cue, not a diagnostic test.
- Breathe throughout. Do not hold the breath to force a lift (the Valsalva manoeuvre). This matters for any client, and is a conservative cue when blood pressure is a concern, without using a house blood-pressure cut-off.
- Progress one variable at a time — range, load, or repetitions — and only when recent sessions felt repeatable and recovery has been reasonable.
- Heat, poor sleep, a missed meal, a recent medicine change, or travel can all be reasons to reduce demand that day. That is coaching judgment, not a medical clearance decision.

### Warm-up and cool-down

Prepare for a few minutes before loading: easy marching or walking, comfortable joint movement, and one or two light rehearsal sets of the main lift. A band pass-through or an easy floor movement such as a cat-camel can fit this general warm-up style; those examples are **compatible with previously noted house warm-up habits**, not a verified whiteboard inventory (see section 6).

Cool down with easy walking or gentle range-of-motion work and a short check-in: how does the client feel now, and do they have what they need after the call? Delayed low blood sugar can occur hours after activity in some people who use certain medicines; the coach does not manage that with a protocol, but can remind the client to follow whatever after-session plan their clinician has already given them, and to get clinical advice if they do not have one and are worried.

### What to have to hand during sessions

- A charged phone and a known way to reach local emergency services.
- Water.
- A clear, uncluttered floor and a stable chair or support if needed.
- A camera angle that shows the whole movement.
- Any meter, device or supply the **clinician has already asked the client to have ready**. Coaches do not prescribe what to swallow, how much, or what a reading must be.

## 5. Hybrid-race note (HYROX / DEKA / ATHX)

Diabetes, high blood pressure, raised cholesterol or weight-related risk need not automatically rule out training toward a hybrid race, but a race is not a reason to override clinical advice or symptoms.

**Before entering or building toward a HYROX, DEKA or ATHX event, plan with the client’s care team.** Useful questions for the client to take to their clinician include whether the event format is appropriate for them, whether any stations or conditions should be limited, and what the client should already know about managing their condition around long or hard efforts. The coach can help the client write those questions. The coach does not answer them.

**The clinician owns the race-day blood-sugar plan, all medication and insulin decisions, and the response plan for lows or highs.** Nutrition supports food, carbohydrate and fluid planning within that clinical guidance; Nutrition does not prescribe or adjust insulin or other medicines. Hybrid Race Coach owns periodised race plans and station skill only after that clinical planning is in place and the client is training comfortably in ordinary sessions. Metabolic Health can stay involved for condition-aware session adaptations. Do not write a race-week glucose protocol in this guide, and do not invent event-day dosing.

## 6. Two sample sessions (Susmit’s format)

**Exercise choices in these tables are provisional.** They have not been taken from a verified Susmit whiteboard inventory.

A workspace search for an authoritative gym-whiteboard exercise list or image did not find one. Knowledge-base notes mention a general warm-up style (including pass-throughs and cat-camel) and short whiteboard-style sessions, and a prior protocol documents the **session format** used here (one main lift, one related accessory between those sets, then core). Other “whiteboard” matches were video scripts or unrelated cached pages, not an exercise inventory. These examples follow that documented format and stay conservative for a worldwide live-online setting. They are **not** represented as Susmit’s documented whiteboard programme. Please replace or confirm the exercise names in a later revision.

These are short, live-online examples, not a full programme and not a medical prescription. They assume the client has relevant clinician guidance in place where it exists, is not mid-red-flag, and can use a chair and a small amount of equipment. Allow about 25–30 minutes: a few minutes to prepare, one main lift with a related accessory done in the rest between main-lift sets, then a small amount of core and a check-in.

**How the main lift and accessory fit together.** Complete a set of the main lift, then do a set of the accessory during that rest, then take any extra rest needed before the next main-lift set. Core comes after, not as a burnout.

**Daily core can mean light practice, not daily hard training.** On non-strength days, one easy set of a comfortable drill is enough. Skip it when sleep, travel, symptoms or recovery say so.

Prepare with easy marching or walking, comfortable joint movement, and one or two light rehearsal sets. A band pass-through can be included if a band is available. Use a clear floor, stable furniture, and a camera angle that shows the whole movement. Breathe throughout. Stop for section 3 concerns, for sharp pain, or if the client feels unwell.

### Session 1 — Home strength (provisional example)

A lower-body strength session for a client who already does some training, with a dumbbell or a household weight and a stable chair. Effort stays moderate: short sentences are still possible, and repetitions are left in reserve. This is not an interval session and not a race-station session.

| Exercise | Reps | Description |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Main: goblet squat to a stable box or chair** | 2–3 sets × 6–8 | Hold a manageable weight close to the chest, or use body weight if that is enough. Sit back and down until you lightly touch the box or chair, then stand. Keep the heels down and keep breathing. Use a higher box if depth feels rushed or unstable. |
| **Accessory, done between main-lift sets: Romanian deadlift or hip hinge** | 2–3 sets × 6–8 | Soften the knees, push the hips back, and keep the weight close. Stop where the spine stays long and comfortable. Hold a chair or wall for balance if needed. This is the between-sets exercise, not a second workout. |
| **Core: standing band or wall press-out (daily-style core)** | 1–2 sets × 6–8 | Stand tall. Press a light band forward, or press your hands into a wall at chest height, without holding your breath or flaring the ribs. Slow and steady. If a band is not available, use the wall. |

A higher box, an unweighted squat-to-chair, or a shorter range are the first regressions. If no extra weight is available, slow the lowering and pause briefly on the chair.

### Session 2 — Travel or minimal equipment (provisional example)

For a client in a hotel room or a spare space with a stable chair and little or no kit. The main lift is a single-leg strength pattern; the accessory supports the hips. This is still not a running session and not a finisher.

| Exercise | Reps | Description |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Main: sit-to-stand from a stable chair, or a split squat holding a support** | 2–3 sets × 6–8 (each side if split squat) | Sit tall, then stand without using momentum; sit down slowly. Use the chair arms if you need them. If sit-to-stand is already easy and quiet, a supported split squat — back foot light, front heel down, holding a chair or wall — is the next option, not a jump or a walking lunge down a corridor. |
| **Accessory, done between main-lift sets: glute bridge** | 2 sets × 8–10 | Lie on your back with feet planted, or use a standing hip hinge if getting to the floor is unsuitable. Lift the hips a comfortable height without forcing a lower-back arch. Pause briefly and lower smoothly. Breathe out as you lift. |
| **Core: heel slides or an easy dead-bug with breathing (daily-style core)** | 1–2 sets × 4–6 each side | On your back with knees bent, or seated if lying is uncomfortable. Breathe in. As you exhale, slide one heel a short distance away, or lower one opposite arm and leg a small way, without the back arching. Reset and switch sides. |

If these patterns are already easy, the next step in a later session is a slightly slower tempo or a light goblet hold — not a devil press, burpee or race-station chipper. Keep each session’s main lift different across the week (for example a squat or sit-to-stand pattern one day and a hinge or upper-body press or row on another) rather than repeating this exact menu every time. Pair the week with walking or another aerobic activity the client already tolerates, on separate days or as an easy finish if time and recovery allow.

## Supplementary safety sources

**This section is deliberately separate. It does not satisfy the brief’s requirement that red-flag symptoms be sourced only from the reading list.** It must not be summarised as “the reading-list red flags” or as a substitute for actually seen book passages. It is offered so coaches are not left without any checkable public safety language while the gap in section 3 remains open, pending Susmit’s review.

Public pages opened (or, for CDC symptoms, opened via WebFetch) on 11 November 2026 (Asia/Calcutta · IST):

- **ADA 2016 position statement** (same official URL as item 10 above). In the “Managing physical activity with health complications” table, the statement says to **stop exercise immediately** if symptoms of myocardial infarction occur during activity — it names chest pain, radiating pain, shortness of breath and others — and seek medical attention; and to stop immediately if symptoms of a stroke (coming on suddenly and often affecting only one side of the body) happen during exercise. It also discusses exercise-related hypoglycemia as a concern mainly for people using insulin or insulin secretagogues, without this guide importing its carbohydrate or insulin tables.
- **CDC, Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia)** — official page opened via WebFetch: [Source](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/about/low-blood-sugar-hypoglycemia.html.) The page states that reactions vary, and lists common symptoms that may include a fast heartbeat, shaking, sweating, nervousness or anxiety, irritability or confusion, dizziness, and hunger. It also describes more serious signs (weakness, trouble walking or seeing clearly, acting strangely or feeling confused, seizures) and notes that some people have no warning symptoms (hypoglycemia unawareness). **Do not use the page’s numerical cut-offs or treatment (“15-15”) rules as coaching or publishing advice.** If a client has a clinician-given plan for lows, they follow that plan. Severe distress, confusion, seizure or unresponsiveness is an emergency-response situation (section 3), not a coaching experiment.

If this supplementary list is used at all before a reading-list-sourced revision exists, use it only as a conservative reason to **stop** and to choose emergency help versus later clinical review. Do not use it to diagnose the cause, to set a house threshold, or to treat the client.

#### 2. Exercise and Diabetes: A Clinician's Guide to Prescribing Physical Activity
- **Author:** Sheri Colberg
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=vIssDQAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Written for health professionals on prescribing activity for people with diabetes.
  2. Covers practical issues such as blood glucose around sessions and medication timing, which the client's own care team manages.
  3. Good model for 'coach adapts the session, clinician manages the condition' language.

#### 3. The Athlete's Guide to Diabetes
- **Author:** Sheri R. Colberg
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=hJSHDwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Aimed at active people and athletes with diabetes who want to keep training and competing.
  2. Shows that diabetes need not rule out hard training or events such as hybrid races, with planning.
  3. Source for client-facing FAQs, always paired with 'check with your diabetes care team'.

#### 4. Diabetes and Exercise: From Pathophysiology to Clinical Implementation
- **Author:** Jane E. B. Reusch, Judith G. Regensteiner, Kerry J. Stewart, Aristidis Veves (eds.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=C5s3DwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Academic multi-author book linking diabetes physiology to exercise practice.
  2. Explains why both aerobic and resistance training matter for blood sugar control.
  3. Deep reference for checking blog claims before they go live.

#### 5. Clinical Exercise Physiology, 4E
- **Author:** Jonathan K. Ehrman, Paul M. Gordon, Paul S. Visich, Steven J. Keteyian (eds.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=8CxbDwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Textbook covering exercise testing and training across cardiovascular, metabolic, pulmonary and other conditions.
  2. Helps coaches understand what a clinical exercise physiologist does and where a coach's role stops.
  3. Broad reference for hypertension, obesity and cholesterol-related education.

#### 6. Optimizing Exercise for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes
- **Author:** Jonathan Peter Little, Kristian Karstoft, Adeel Safdar (eds.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=5gFiDwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Research collection on which exercise types, timings and intensities help type 2 diabetes.
  2. Covers ideas such as interval training and short activity breaks that suit busy clients.
  3. Useful for evidence-based, non-hype content on blood sugar and exercise.

#### 7. NSCA's Essentials of Training Special Populations
- **Author:** NSCA – National Strength & Conditioning Association; Patrick L. Jacobs (ed.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=8DAtDwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Strength-and-conditioning view of training clients with medical conditions, disabilities and other special needs.
  2. Organised by condition, with programme considerations a coach can apply.
  3. Reference only; citing it never implies any NSCA endorsement.
