## Books (28)

### Sports supplements, anti-doping and inadvertent doping (education; Nutrition owns, Coaching Standards reviews)
#### 1. Sports Supplements: Which nutritional supplements really work
- **Author:** Anita Bean
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=DAVxBgAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Goes supplement by supplement (beetroot juice, creatine, caffeine, whey protein and more): what each is, how it is used and whether the evidence supports it.
  2. Starts from the problem that media reports and manufacturer claims are often confusing or wrong, and suggests alternatives to supplements.
  3. Good plain-English source for client FAQs; we never recommend specific brands or doses as personal advice.
#### 2. Nutritional Supplements in Sport, Exercise and Health: An A-Z Guide
- **Author:** Linda M. Castell, Samantha J. Stear, Louise M. Burke (eds.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=LoBKCAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Over 140 evidence-based A–Z reviews of supplements, ergogenic aids and sports foods, each with food sources, practical notes, interactions and known side effects.
  2. Addresses efficacy, safety, legality and ethics, with extra reviews on the WADA code, inadvertent doping and stacking.
  3. Reference text for checking any supplement question a client raises before a draft answer goes out.
#### 3. The Athlete's Guide to Sports Supplements
- **Author:** Kimberly Mueller, Josh Hingst
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=lO96DwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Concise entries for 120 popular supplements: what it is, how it works, research outcomes, dosage ranges discussed and possible health concerns.
  2. Includes notes for masters athletes, extreme environments and special dietary needs (food allergies, diabetes, vegetarian).
  3. Written against 'too-good-to-be-true' supplement marketing, which matches our no-hype claims rule.
#### 4. Drugs in Sport (7th ed.)
- **Author:** David R. Mottram, Neil Chester (eds.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=0uFLDwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Updated to the World Anti-Doping Code: prevalence, doping-control rules, and the science and side effects of each major drug class.
  2. Increased focus on inadvertent doping, the role of athlete-support personnel (coaches included) and legal substances in supplements.
  3. Background for a clean-sport note for clients who race in tested events; any specific substance question goes to the race organiser's/anti-doping body's own rules and a clinician.

### Calisthenics and bodyweight skill progressions (home and travel-friendly strength)
#### 5. Convict Conditioning: How to Bust Free of All Weakness Using the Lost Secrets of Supreme Survival Strength
- **Author:** Paul Wade
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=IYGEtgEACAAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Built on six movement families, each with ten progressions, so a client of almost any starting level has a next step.
  2. Progression ladders are useful for live online sessions where clients train at home with no equipment.
  3. The blurb's macho 'true man' / '3-percenter' framing is the publisher's tone and must never be copied into our inclusive, worldwide copy.
#### 6. Get Strong: The Ultimate 16-Week Transformation Program for Gaining Muscle and Strength Using the Power of Progressive Calisthenics
- **Author:** Al Kavadlo, Danny Kavadlo
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=GzuGAQAACAAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. A 16-week structured calisthenics programme that pairs exercise selection with programming.
  2. Frames progress as moving along a strength continuum from beginner to advanced skills (one-arm pull-up, human flag, back lever).
  3. Template for how to sequence a block of bodyweight work; 'transformation' wording stays theirs, not a promise of ours.
#### 7. Next Level Strength: The Ultimate Rings and Parallettes Program
- **Author:** Al Kavadlo, Danny Kavadlo
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=zAdSwwEACAAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Programme for using gymnastic rings and parallettes as calisthenics tools.
  2. Bridges floor bodyweight work to low-cost equipment many home clients can buy and set up.
  3. The joint-healing claims in the blurb's testimonial are not repeated; pain questions go to Physio.
#### 8. Explosive Calisthenics
- **Author:** Paul Wade
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=1joLrgEACAAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Bodyweight-only methods for power, speed, agility and quick reflexes, drawing on martial-arts conditioning.
  2. Treats resilience of joints, tendons and nervous system as part of explosive training.
  3. Idea bank for bodyweight power work in hybrid-race and field-sport blocks, scaled conservatively and Physio-checked for landing/plyometric volume.

### Fitness assessment, field testing and exercise prescription (coaching use, not a dashboard)
#### 9. Advanced Fitness Assessment and Exercise Prescription (7th ed.)
- **Author:** Vivian H. Heyward, Ann L. Gibson
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=PkdoAwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Organised around fitness components: how to assess each one, then design exercise programmes from that assessment.
  2. Bridges research and practice for exercise-science students and fitness professionals.
  3. Model for an assessment-led onboarding: test, then prescribe, then retest at sensible intervals.
#### 10. NSCA's Guide to Tests and Assessments
- **Author:** NSCA (National Strength and Conditioning Association), Todd A. Miller (ed.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=xPJ6DwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Research-supported measures for body composition, heart rate and blood pressure, metabolic rate, aerobic power, lactate threshold, strength, muscular endurance, power, speed and agility, mobility and balance.
  2. Opens with how to test, analyse data and draw conclusions accurately and simply.
  3. Citing it never implies NSCA endorsement of OnlineFitnessWorldwide; choose only tests that are safe to run remotely on video.
#### 11. Practical Fitness Testing: Analysis in Exercise and Sport
- **Author:** Morc Coulson, David Archer
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=lbZnCgAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Theory and practice of fitness testing in exercise and sport settings, with current population norms and illustrations.
  2. Framed around measuring current fitness and then monitoring whether a programme is working.
  3. Mapped to UK National Occupational Standards for instructors; useful for writing clear, plain-language test instructions.
#### 12. Fitness Testing 101: A Guide for Personal Trainers and Coaches
- **Author:** Patrick S. Hagerman
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=9KQxKOtbFT4C
- **Takeaways:**
  1. For coaches without lab equipment: quick, efficient tests across the major fitness areas.
  2. Each test lists objectives, equipment, procedures and scoring, most with a table of norms, plus copyable datasheets.
  3. Closest fit to online coaching, where clients test at home with minimal kit.
#### 13. Advanced Strength and Conditioning: An Evidence-based Approach (2nd ed.)
- **Author:** Anthony Turner, Paul Comfort (eds.)
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=pbxZEAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Applies current evidence to exercise selection and programming across strength and power, speed and agility, and aerobic conditioning.
  2. Aimed at advanced students and novice-to-expert practitioners building a professional skill set.
  3. Core programming reference for a general strength coach role.

#### 16. Radical Candor: How to Get What You Want by Saying What You Mean
- **Author:** Kim Scott
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=wP9NDgAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. The sweet spot between obnoxious aggression and ruinous empathy: care personally while challenging directly.
  2. Guidance mixes praise and criticism to help people develop; three principles: make it personal, get stuff done, understand why it matters.

#### 17. Leading From Anywhere: Unlock the Power and Performance of Remote Teams
- **Author:** David Burkus
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=jEQKEAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Field guide to leading remotely, built from research on virtual teams and case studies of companies that thrived remotely.
  2. Everyday examples for running a distributed team, which is the natural shape of a worldwide online coaching business.
  3. Background for coordinating many bots and any future human staff across time zones.

### Gamification and challenge design (ethical engagement for member challenges)
#### 18. Actionable Gamification: Beyond Points, Badges, and Leaderboards
- **Author:** Yu-kai Chou
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=9ZfBDwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Introduces the Octalysis Framework and eight Core Drives behind what makes games engaging.
  2. Argues good gamification combines game design, dynamics and user experience, not just points and badges.
  3. Lens for designing member challenges; any leaderboard idea goes past Coaching Standards for body-image and pressure risks.
#### 19. The Gamification Toolkit: Dynamics, Mechanics, and Components for the Win
- **Author:** Kevin Werbach, Dan Hunter
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=2PU1EAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Short companion to For the Win that goes deeper into the key game elements.
  2. Separates dynamics, mechanics and components so designers pick elements deliberately.
  3. Checklist vocabulary for Community and Retention challenge drafts.
#### 20. SuperBetter: The Power of Living Gamefully
- **Author:** Jane McGonigal
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=kaSMEAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Written after the author turned her own concussion recovery into a resilience-building game.
  2. Rules for approaching personal challenges 'gamefully', later developed into a digital game and research.
  3. Idea source for playful, self-paced habit quests; mental-health content stays education-only with Mindset's referral rules.
#### 21. You've Been Played: How Corporations, Governments and Schools Use Games to Control Us All
- **Author:** Adrian Hon
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=wH5vEAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. A game designer's critique of points, badges and leaderboards used for coercion and profit (warehouse work, gig driving, in-app purchases).
  2. Asks what we can do about exploitative gamification.
  3. The ethical counterweight: no manipulative streak pressure, no shaming leaderboards, no dark patterns in our member experience.

## 1. Programming principles — one page for live online S&C

**1. Assess first.**
- Every plan starts from the onboarding assessment (section 2).
- We prescribe from what we see, not from a template.
- Retest every 4–6 weeks, or at the end of each block.
- The assess-then-prescribe model follows Heyward & Gibson, *Advanced Fitness Assessment and Exercise Prescription*.

**2. Progress patiently.**
- Small steps, every week or two.
- One lever at a time: reps, then sets, then load or a harder variation.
- Technique holds first. Load follows.
- Overload principles draw on Zatsiorsky, Kraemer & Fry, *Science and Practice of Strength Training*, and Turner & Comfort, *Advanced Strength and Conditioning*.

**3. Autoregulate by effort.**
- We use effort, not fixed numbers, to set load.
- Plain-language tool: **reps in reserve (RIR)** — how many more good reps you could have done.
- Most working sets end with about 1–3 reps in reserve.
- New lifters and new movements: stay further from failure (3+ in reserve).
- Bad sleep, high stress, travel, jet lag, heat → lower the target for the day. That is part of the plan, not a missed day.
- Hypertrophy volume and effort ideas draw on Schoenfeld, *Science and Development of Muscle Hypertrophy*, and Israetel et al., *Scientific Principles of Hypertrophy Training*.

**4. Scale to the room.**
- Same movement pattern, different tool: barbell → dumbbell → kettlebell → band → bodyweight.
- Plan around the client's kit, space, ceiling, floor and noise limits (flats, hotels, shared homes).
- Plan around their time zone and their week, not ours.

**5. Move in patterns.**
- Every week covers: squat, hinge, push, pull, carry or core, and a single-leg pattern.
- Add conditioning and power only when the basics are steady.

**6. Plan in blocks.**
- 4-week blocks: 3 build weeks + 1 lighter week, or adjust to life.
- Block-and-phase structure draws generally on Bompa & Buzzichelli, *Periodization*.
- Sport clients: off-season builds, in-season maintains (section 5).

**7. Coach the rep on camera.**
- Short external cues. One cue at a time. (Idea drawn from Winkelman, *The Language of Coaching*.)
- Camera at hip height, side-on for squats and hinges, front-on for knees and pressing.
- If we can't see it, we don't load it.

**8. Safety beats completion.**
- Pain (not effort discomfort) ends the set. Physio gets the note.
- No max-effort lifts on video for new clients.
- No high-volume jumping or landing work without Physio's check.

## 2. Remote onboarding assessment (video-safe, minimal kit)

**How to run it**
- One live session of about 45–60 minutes, or split over two.
- Kit: a phone or laptop camera, a sturdy chair or bench, a wall, a timer, a mat or towel, a step or stair (optional), a measuring tape (optional).
- Warm up first: 5–8 minutes of easy movement.
- Camera: side-on for squats, hinges and push-ups; front-on for balance and knees.
- Record the result, how it looked, and how hard it felt. Storage of these notes is an open question for Privacy & Data Care (section 7).
- We do **not** compare clients to population norms in this draft. Each client is compared with their own retest. Whether to use published norms is an open question for Susmit.

**Sources for test selection and protocol style**
- Hagerman, *Fitness Testing 101* — low-equipment tests for coaches without a lab.
- Heyward & Gibson, *Advanced Fitness Assessment and Exercise Prescription* — assess each fitness component, then prescribe.
- NSCA (Miller, ed.), *NSCA's Guide to Tests and Assessments* — research-supported test menu; we pick only remote-safe options. Citing it implies no NSCA endorsement.
- Coulson & Archer, *Practical Fitness Testing* — plain-language test instructions and test → monitor framing.
- Protocols below are our coaching adaptations for video. They are not copied from any book. Check the source protocol before publishing a client version.

**Global stop rules (apply to every test)**
- Stop for: pain, chest discomfort, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, nausea, feeling faint, numbness or tingling.
- Stop if form breaks down and the client can't correct it with one cue.
- Stop if the camera loses sight of the client mid-test.
- After a stop: no retesting today. Note it. Hand pain to Physio.

### Test 1 — Movement check: bodyweight squat and hinge screen

- **Purpose:** see squat depth, knee and trunk control, and hip-hinge pattern. Not scored. A coaching look.
- **Setup:** feet roughly hip-to-shoulder width. Camera side-on, then front-on. Chair behind for confidence.
- **Instructions:**
  - 5 slow bodyweight squats to a comfortable depth.
  - 5 hip hinges with hands sliding down the thighs (or a broomstick along the spine: head, upper back, tailbone).
- **Stop rules:** global rules. Stop if any joint hurts.
- **What it changes:**
  - Sets the first squat variation (box squat, goblet squat, or free squat).
  - Decides whether we teach the hinge first with a dowel or start loading.

### Test 2 — Sit-to-stand (lower-body strength-endurance)

- **Purpose:** baseline leg strength-endurance with a chair. Low risk, easy to repeat.
- **Setup:** sturdy chair against a wall. Arms crossed on chest. Side-on camera.
- **Instructions:**
  - Stand fully, sit fully, as many times as you can with good control in a set time (for example 30 seconds; confirm the protocol from the source before publishing).
  - Count full reps only.
- **Stop rules:** global rules, plus stop if knees cave sharply or the chair moves.
- **What it changes:**
  - Low score or poor control → start with box squats and split-squat regressions.
  - Strong score with good control → start loaded squats or harder single-leg work.

### Test 3 — Push-up test (upper-body pushing)

- **Purpose:** upper-body pushing strength-endurance and trunk control.
- **Setup:** choose the level the client can do with good form: wall, bench/countertop incline, knees, or full floor push-up. Side-on camera.
- **Instructions:**
  - Steady tempo. Chest to a fist-height target, arms to straight.
  - Count reps with a straight line from head to knees or heels.
  - Stop at the first rep that breaks form.
  - Record the level and the reps.
- **Stop rules:** global rules, plus wrist or shoulder pain.
- **What it changes:**
  - Sets the starting rung on the push ladder (section 4).
  - Sets pressing load in the gym and DB blocks.

### Test 4 — Pulling check (row or hang)

- **Purpose:** baseline pulling. Most home clients have no bar, so we offer options.
- **Setup — pick one:**
  - Table or sturdy-bar inverted row (only on a table or bar that is clearly stable — coach checks on camera).
  - Band row anchored in a door with a proper door anchor.
  - Dead hang from a pull-up bar, if the client has one fitted.
- **Instructions:**
  - Rows: max good reps, chest to target, body straight.
  - Hang: hold with straight arms, shoulders gently engaged, time it. Feet near the floor so they can step down.
- **Stop rules:** global rules, plus any wobble of the anchor or table — stop at once. Shoulder or elbow pain.
- **What it changes:**
  - Sets the starting rung on the pull ladder.
  - Tells us if the client needs kit (a bar, rings or a band) before pull-ups make sense. Equipment advice only — no purchase links or prices.

### Test 5 — Front plank and side plank holds (trunk endurance)

- **Purpose:** trunk control and endurance.
- **Setup:** mat. Side-on camera for front plank; front-on for side plank.
- **Instructions:**
  - Front plank on forearms (or hands, or knees as a regression). Hold a straight line.
  - Side plank each side (from knees if needed).
  - Stop the clock when the line is lost.
- **Stop rules:** global rules, plus back pain, breath-holding that the client can't release.
- **What it changes:**
  - Sets the core ladder starting rung.
  - Big left/right difference → add more single-side core and carry work.

### Test 6 — Single-leg balance (eyes open, then closed)

- **Purpose:** balance and ankle/hip control. Important for sport and single-leg lifts.
- **Setup:** stand next to a wall or chair for safety. Clear floor. Front-on camera.
- **Instructions:**
  - Stand on one leg, hands on hips, eyes open. Time up to a set cap. Repeat other leg.
  - Only if steady: repeat eyes closed, hand hovering near the wall.
- **Stop rules:** global rules. Touch the wall at any wobble. Skip eyes-closed if the client feels unsteady or has a history of falls (flag Physio/Masters).
- **What it changes:**
  - Low or uneven → split squats with support, step-ups, balance drills in the warm-up.
  - Good → progress to unsupported single-leg work and lateral sport drills.

### Test 7 — Step test or talk test (aerobic baseline)

- **Purpose:** a gentle aerobic baseline without a lab.
- **Setup:** one stair step or a sturdy low step. Timer. Optional heart-rate watch.
- **Instructions:**
  - Step up and down at a steady rhythm for a set time (confirm cadence and duration from the source protocol before publishing).
  - Record how hard it felt (0–10) and whether the client could talk in full sentences.
  - If they have a heart-rate watch, note the recovery heart rate at one minute. Use it only to compare with their own retest.
- **Alternative:** a 6–10 minute brisk walk or easy cardio on their own machine, rated by effort and talk test.
- **Stop rules:** global rules, plus heat — skip or postpone in a hot room. Anyone with a heart condition does not do this test without clinician advice.
- **What it changes:**
  - Sets conditioning starting level: easy steady work vs intervals.
  - Tells us how much rest to build between sets in the first block.

### Test 8 — Mobility snapshot: overhead reach and ankle knee-to-wall

- **Purpose:** check overhead shoulder range and ankle range. Both change exercise choice.
- **Setup:** wall. Tape measure optional. Side-on camera.
- **Instructions:**
  - Overhead: back and head against the wall, ribs down, raise straight arms overhead. Can thumbs touch the wall without the back arching?
  - Ankle: in a half-kneel facing the wall, drive the knee forward over the toes to touch the wall, heel down. Note the furthest foot distance that works. Compare sides.
- **Stop rules:** global rules. Pain → stop and note for Physio.
- **What it changes:**
  - Limited overhead range → landmine or incline press instead of strict overhead press for now; add shoulder mobility.
  - Limited or uneven ankle → heel-elevated goblet squats, ankle mobility in the warm-up.

### Retest rhythm
- Retest Tests 2–5 at the end of each 4-week block.
- Retest the full battery every 8–12 weeks, or after a break, illness or travel.
- Use the same setup, same time of day if possible, same camera angle.
- Talk about the client's own change. Never compare clients with each other.

## 3. Sample 4-week strength blocks

**How to read these**
- 3 sessions a week. About 45–60 minutes.
- Each session: warm-up (8–10 min) → main lifts → accessories → short cool-down.
- Week 1: learn the moves. 2–3 reps in reserve.
- Week 2: same work, add a rep or a little load.
- Week 3: hardest week. About 1–2 reps in reserve.
- Week 4: lighter week. Cut sets by about a third. Retest at the end.
- These are sample drafts. A real client plan comes from their assessment.

### Block A — Full gym

**Day 1 — Squat and push**

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Regression | Progression |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back squat (or front squat) | 3–4 × 5–8 | Goblet squat to a box | Add load; pause squat |
| Bench press | 3–4 × 6–8 | Dumbbell floor press | Add load; close-grip variation |
| Rear-foot elevated split squat | 3 × 8–10 each | Supported split squat | Add dumbbells |
| Seated cable row | 3 × 10–12 | Band row | Single-arm row; slower lowering |
| Pallof press | 3 × 10 each side | Half-kneeling, lighter band | Standing, step out further |

## 4. Calisthenics progression ladders

**Where these come from**
- Idea of long, small-step ladders for each movement family: Paul Wade, *Convict Conditioning*.
- Block structure for progressive calisthenics: Al Kavadlo & Danny Kavadlo, *Get Strong*.
- Rings and parallettes options: Al Kavadlo & Danny Kavadlo, *Next Level Strength*.
- Further background already in the knowledge base: Steven Low, *Overcoming Gravity*; Mark Lauren & Joshua Clark, *You Are Your Own Gym*.
- These ladders are our own simplified versions. We borrow the structure, not the books' tone. Every rung is a good place to be.

**How to move up a rung**
- Own the current rung for clean sets (for example 3 × 8–12, or a target hold) with good form for two sessions in a row.
- Then try the next rung for the first set only. Finish with the old rung.
- Moving down a rung is normal. Bad week, travel, sore shoulder — step back.
- Wrist, shoulder or elbow pain → stop that ladder, tell Physio.

### Push ladder
1. Wall push-up
2. High incline push-up (countertop)
3. Low incline push-up (chair or bench)
4. Knee push-up
5. Full push-up
6. Close-grip / diamond push-up
7. Feet-elevated push-up
8. Archer push-up (or ring push-up)
9. Assisted one-arm push-up (hand on a ball or block)
10. One-arm push-up (incline first, then floor)

Rings/parallettes option: parallette push-up → ring push-up → ring dip support hold → assisted ring dip.

### Pull ladder
1. Door-frame or band row (body nearly upright)
2. Table or bar row, body at a high angle
3. Table or bar row, body lower
4. Row with feet on the floor, body horizontal
5. Feet-elevated row
6. Dead hang and scapular pulls (shoulder blades down and back)
7. Jumping or band-assisted pull-up with slow lowering
8. Negative pull-up (slow lowering only)
9. Full pull-up / chin-up
10. Archer pull-up or assisted one-arm work (advanced; Physio check for elbows and shoulders)

### Squat ladder
1. Sit-to-stand from a chair
2. Box squat to a lower box
3. Assisted squat (holding a door frame or table)
4. Bodyweight full squat
5. Split squat
6. Rear-foot elevated split squat
7. Pistol to a box (single-leg sit to box)
8. Assisted pistol or assisted shrimp squat
9. Shrimp squat
10. Pistol squat

### Hinge / bridge ladder
1. Glute bridge (two legs)
2. Glute bridge, slow lowering and pause
3. Single-leg glute bridge
4. Shoulders-elevated bridge on a chair
5. Single-leg shoulders-elevated bridge
6. Bodyweight single-leg RDL (hand on wall)
7. Single-leg RDL, free
8. Table bridge / reverse tabletop
9. Straight-arm back bridge prep at a wall (only with good shoulder range — Test 8)
10. Full back bridge (advanced; mobility and Physio check first)

Note: the full back-bend bridge is optional. Many clients never need it. The hinge strength rungs (1–7) matter more for most people.

### Core ladder
1. Dead bug (arms or legs only)
2. Full dead bug
3. Forearm plank / side plank from knees
4. Full plank / full side plank
5. Hollow body tuck hold
6. Hollow body hold
7. Lying leg raise (lower back stays in contact)
8. Hanging knee raise
9. Hanging leg raise (bent then straight)
10. L-sit on parallettes or chairs (tuck first)

### Skill ladder — the first pull-up (default skill)

Chosen because it suits most home clients with a doorway bar and builds the pull ladder.

1. Band or table rows, 3 × 12 clean
2. Dead hang, building time
3. Scapular pulls from a hang
4. Flexed-arm hang at the top (chin over bar; step up from a chair)
5. Slow negatives (lower under control; build lowering time)
6. Band-assisted pull-ups, lighter band over time
7. First full pull-up
8. Sets of 2–3 pull-ups, then build reps

**Alternative skill — handstand (for clients with good overhead range and wrist comfort):**
1. Wrist prep and loaded wrist rocks
2. Pike hold with hands on floor
3. Plank to wall walk (partial)
4. Chest-to-wall handstand, short holds
5. Longer chest-to-wall holds
6. Kick-up practice to a wall, with a safe bail-out (cartwheel out) taught first
7. Free balance attempts

Handstand safety: clear the space. Soft floor. No furniture behind. Teach the bail-out before kick-ups. Skip for clients with blood pressure concerns, eye conditions, or neck, wrist or shoulder pain — flag Physio and Metabolic Health.

## 5. Sport-specific S&C outlines (needs-analysis format)

**Format**
- Follows the structure of Turner (ed.), *Routledge Handbook of Strength and Conditioning*: sport demands (physiological and biomechanical) → injury considerations → testing battery → exercise programming.
- We keep the structure. We do not copy figures from the book. Demands below are written in general terms.
- For adult amateur and veteran club players worldwide. Not elite programming. Not rehab.
- Injury history and return-to-play → **Physio** always.
- Plyometric and landing volume → **Physio** checks before we load it.
- Veteran players roughly 40+ → co-plan with **Masters**.

**Season structure (all sports)**
- **Off-season:** build strength and fitness. 2–3 S&C sessions a week.
- **Pre-season:** add power, speed and sport-like conditioning. Keep strength.
- **In-season:** maintain. 1–2 short sessions. Keep away from match day.
- **Week after a match:** lighter. Recovery first.

### 5a. Football (soccer)

**Needs analysis**
- **Demands:** intermittent running with repeated sprints, changes of direction, jumps, tackles and kicks. Long matches with a high aerobic base.
- **Key qualities:** repeated-sprint ability, acceleration, deceleration, single-leg strength, hamstring and groin strength, trunk control.
- **Common injury areas (general):** hamstrings, groin, ankles and knees. We do not assess or treat these. History → Physio.
- **Position notes:** goalkeepers need more jump, dive and lateral power; outfield players more repeated running.

**Testing battery (remote-friendly)**
- Onboarding tests 1, 2, 5, 6, 8.
- Optional field tests on the client's own pitch: timed short sprint or repeated sprints (filmed side-on), only after a proper warm-up and only for clients with no hamstring history.

**Programming**
- Strength: split squat, RDL, trap-bar deadlift, Nordic hamstring curl (assisted, low volume, build slowly), Copenhagen plank regressions for the groin.
- Power: low-level hops and bounds → jumps (Physio-cleared volume).
- Conditioning: mostly on the pitch with the team; off-field intervals only if club training is light.
- In-season: 2 short sessions, at least 48 hours from a match.

**Sources:** Turner (ed.), *Routledge Handbook of Strength and Conditioning*; Verheijen, *Conditioning for Soccer* (attribution per the Google Books listing); Gamble, *Strength and Conditioning for Team Sports*; small-sided game ideas from Clemente, *Conditioned Games for Team Sports Training*.

### 5b. Tennis and padel

**Needs analysis**
- **Demands:** short, repeated high-intensity rallies with rest between points. Fast first steps, lateral movement, split steps, rotation and overhead hitting.
- **Key qualities:** first-step speed, lateral agility, rotational power, shoulder and forearm endurance, single-leg strength and balance.
- **Padel notes:** more time at the net and in tighter space, more low volleys and walls. Movement is shorter and lateral. We apply tennis principles by analogy — no dedicated padel source in our reading list yet.
- **Common injury areas (general):** shoulder, elbow, wrist, lower back, knees, calves. History → Physio.

**Testing battery (remote-friendly)**
- Onboarding tests 3, 5, 6, 8.
- Optional on-court: lateral shuffle or spider-style agility drill filmed front-on (coaching look only, compared with their own retest).

**Programming**
- Strength: split squat, lateral lunge, RDL, single-arm row, landmine press.
- Rotational power: medicine-ball or band rotations (scoop throw against a wall if space allows).
- Shoulder health: band external rotations, face pulls, scapular work — Physio reviews for anyone with shoulder history.
- Footwork: split step, lateral shuffles, crossovers at low intensity.
- Conditioning: short work and rest intervals that look like points.

**Sources:** Turner (ed.), *Routledge Handbook of Strength and Conditioning*; USTA, Kovacs, Roetert & Ellenbecker, *Complete Conditioning for Tennis*. USTA's endorsement is of that book only. It does not endorse OnlineFitnessWorldwide.

### 5c. Golf

**Needs analysis**
- **Demands:** repeated high-speed rotation from a stable base. Long walks or periods of standing. Low aerobic demand but long duration.
- **Key qualities:** hip and upper-back rotation, hip and glute strength, single-leg stability, trunk control, grip and forearm endurance, walking endurance.
- **Common issues (general):** lower back, hips, shoulders, elbows. Pain questions → Physio. (The book's "play without pain" phrasing is the book's, not a promise of ours.)
- **Population note:** many golfers are 40+ → co-plan with Masters.

**Testing battery (remote-friendly)**
- Onboarding tests 1, 5, 6, 8.
- Add a seated trunk-rotation check and a hip-rotation look on camera (coaching observation, not scored).

**Programming**
- Strength: goblet squat, RDL, split squat, single-arm row, half-kneeling press.
- Rotation and anti-rotation: Pallof press, cable or band chops and lifts, medicine-ball rotations.
- Mobility: thoracic rotations, hip rotations, part of a pre-round warm-up.
- Conditioning: brisk walking and easy aerobic work.
- In-season: 1–2 short sessions plus a 5–10 minute pre-round warm-up.

**Sources:** Turner (ed.), *Routledge Handbook of Strength and Conditioning*; Palacios-Jansen, *Golf Fitness*.

### 5d. Basketball (chosen as the fourth sport)

Chosen over cricket and rugby because the Routledge Handbook covers it (cricket needs a separate source we don't yet have), it has worldwide amateur reach, and it carries less contact load than rugby for remote coaching. Susmit to confirm (section 7).

**Needs analysis**
- **Demands:** intermittent sprints, repeated jumps and landings, quick changes of direction, backpedals and defensive slides. Physical contact for space.
- **Key qualities:** jump and landing control, acceleration and deceleration, lateral speed, single-leg strength, trunk strength, aerobic base for repeated efforts.
- **Common injury areas (general):** ankles, knees, fingers, lower back. History → Physio. Landing volume is the biggest load to watch — Physio checks it.

**Testing battery (remote-friendly)**
- Onboarding tests 1, 2, 6, 8.
- Optional: landing check — step off a low step and stick the landing, filmed front-on (coaching look at knee and trunk control). Only after Physio is happy with any knee or ankle history.

**Programming**
- Strength: front or goblet squat, trap-bar deadlift, split squat, step-up, calf raises, single-leg RDL.
- Landing and power: snap-downs and stick landings first → low box jumps → hops. Build slowly. Physio-cleared volume.
- Change of direction: deceleration drills, lateral shuffles, closeouts at low intensity.
- Trunk: Pallof press, side plank, carries.
- In-season: 1–2 short sessions, away from game days.

**Sources:** Turner (ed.), *Routledge Handbook of Strength and Conditioning*; Gamble, *Strength and Conditioning for Team Sports*; landing and power ideas drawn generally from McGuigan / NSCA, *Developing Power*, and Wade, *Explosive Calisthenics* (scaled conservatively). Citing NSCA titles implies no NSCA endorsement.

## 6. Exercise-library notes — 15 key exercises

For Video and Social to turn into content. Each note: cues, common faults, regressions, progressions.
Cue style follows the short, external-focus approach in Winkelman, *The Language of Coaching*. Technique background: NSCA, *Exercise Technique Manual for Resistance Training*, and Rippetoe, *Starting Strength*. Citing them implies no endorsement by NSCA or any body.
Filming note for Video: side-on for squats, hinges, push-ups and rows; front-on for knees and single-leg work. No client faces or names without written consent and Susmit's approval.

### 1. Goblet squat
- **Cues:** hold the weight at your chest like a cup. Sit between your heels. Knees follow your toes. Stand tall.
- **Faults:** heels lift; knees cave in; chest drops; weight drifts forward.
- **Regressions:** box squat to a chair; counterweight squat; heels raised on a plate.
- **Progressions:** pause at the bottom; slower lowering; heavier bell; front squat.

### 2. Back squat
- **Cues:** bar on your upper back, not your neck. Big breath. Sit down and back. Drive the floor away.
- **Faults:** depth changes rep to rep; hips shoot up first; knees cave; heels lift.
- **Regressions:** goblet squat; box squat; safety-bar or front squat if shoulders limit position.
- **Progressions:** add load; pause squat; tempo squat.

### 3. Romanian deadlift (RDL)
- **Cues:** soft knees. Push your hips back to the wall behind you. Bar slides down your thighs. Stand up by squeezing your glutes.
- **Faults:** rounding the back; squatting it down; bar drifting away from the legs; leaning back at the top.
- **Regressions:** dowel hinge drill; kettlebell RDL; reduced range.
- **Progressions:** add load; single-leg RDL; pause below the knee.

### 4. Trap-bar or kettlebell deadlift
- **Cues:** stand in the middle. Hips back, chest proud. Push the floor away. Hips and shoulders rise together.
- **Faults:** jerking the weight off the floor; back rounds; hips rise first; lockout by leaning back.
- **Regressions:** kettlebell deadlift from blocks or a step; hinge drill.
- **Progressions:** add load; lower start position; barbell deadlift.

### 5. Hip thrust / glute bridge
- **Cues:** chin tucked. Ribs down. Drive through your heels. Lift until hips are level with your knees and shoulders.
- **Faults:** arching the lower back at the top; pushing through toes; rushing the top.
- **Regressions:** floor glute bridge; shorter range.
- **Progressions:** pause at the top; single-leg; add load across the hips.

### 6. Split squat
- **Cues:** long stance. Back knee straight down to the floor. Front foot stays flat. Stay tall.
- **Faults:** stance too short; front heel lifts; knee caves; torso pitches forward.
- **Regressions:** hold a support; shorter range.
- **Progressions:** add dumbbells; rear-foot elevated; front-foot elevated.

### 7. Reverse lunge
- **Cues:** step back softly. Drop the back knee. Push through the front foot to stand.
- **Faults:** wobbly balance; front knee caves; pushing off the back foot only.
- **Regressions:** split squat; hand on a wall.
- **Progressions:** add load; deficit from a low step; walking lunge.

### 8. Step-up
- **Cues:** whole foot on the step. Push through the top leg. Don't spring off the bottom foot.
- **Faults:** pushing off the bottom leg; knee caving; dropping down fast.
- **Regressions:** lower step; hand support.
- **Progressions:** higher step; add dumbbells; slow lowering.

### 9. Push-up
- **Cues:** hands under shoulders. Body in one line. Lower your chest to the floor. Push the floor away.
- **Faults:** hips sag; head pokes forward; elbows flare wide; half reps.
- **Regressions:** wall, countertop, bench incline; knees.
- **Progressions:** feet elevated; pause at the bottom; archer push-up; ring push-up.

### 10. Bench press / dumbbell floor press
- **Cues:** shoulder blades back and down. Feet planted. Lower to mid-chest. Press up and slightly back.
- **Faults:** bouncing off the chest; elbows flared to 90 degrees; feet floating; hips lifting.
- **Regressions:** floor press; lighter dumbbells; incline push-up.
- **Progressions:** add load; pause press; single-arm dumbbell press.

### 11. Overhead press (dumbbell or barbell)
- **Cues:** squeeze glutes. Ribs down. Press up and slightly back so the weight ends over your mid-foot.
- **Faults:** big back arch; pressing forward; shrugging early.
- **Regressions:** half-kneeling press; landmine press; seated press.
- **Progressions:** standing barbell press; single-arm press; add load.

### 12. One-arm dumbbell row
- **Cues:** flat back. Pull the elbow toward your back pocket. Pause. Lower slowly.
- **Faults:** twisting the torso; shrugging; yanking the weight.
- **Regressions:** chest-supported row; band row.
- **Progressions:** add load; pause at the top; slower lowering.

### 13. Inverted row (table, bar or rings)
- **Cues:** body in one line. Pull your chest to the bar. Squeeze your shoulder blades together.
- **Faults:** hips sag; chin reaches for the bar; partial range. Unstable table or bar — stop.
- **Regressions:** more upright body; bend knees.
- **Progressions:** feet elevated; pause at the top; single-arm.

### 14. Pull-up / chin-up
- **Cues:** start from a full hang. Shoulders down first. Pull your elbows to your ribs. Chin over the bar. Lower slowly.
- **Faults:** kipping or swinging; half reps; shrugging at the top.
- **Regressions:** band-assisted; negatives; flexed-arm hang; lat pulldown.
- **Progressions:** add reps; pause at top; weighted (gym); archer pull-up.

### 15. Plank and Pallof press (core pair)
- **Plank cues:** elbows under shoulders. Squeeze glutes. Ribs down. Breathe.
- **Plank faults:** hips sag or pike; holding breath; neck dropping.
- **Plank regressions:** knee plank; incline plank on a bench.
- **Plank progressions:** longer hold; shoulder taps; hard "full-tension" plank for short holds; body saw.
- **Pallof cues:** stand side-on to the band. Press straight out. Don't let it turn you.
- **Pallof faults:** rotating; leaning away; shrugging.
- **Pallof regressions:** half-kneeling; lighter band.
- **Pallof progressions:** step further out; add an overhead press; split stance.

**Optional next additions (v2):** kettlebell swing, farmer carry, Copenhagen plank, Nordic curl. Swing and carry notes are already in Blocks A and B.
