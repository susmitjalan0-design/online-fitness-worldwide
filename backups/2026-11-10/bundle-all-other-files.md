# Online Fitness Worldwide outreach v2: plan (DRAFT; nothing has been sent)

Prepared 2 Nov 2026 (IST) for Susmit Jalan. Files: `prospects.csv`, `templates.md`, `v1_followup.md`, `v1_followup_recipients.csv`, `v1_queue_check.csv`, `v1_queue_cleaned.csv`, `v1_queue_check_summary.md`.

## 1. Diagnosis: why v1 got 0 replies

Facts from `sent_log.csv`: 124 rows, 122 sent, 2 logged as bounced (7 hard bounces in total once the 5 early ones are included); 80 sent on Saturday 3 Oct; 46 of 124 recipients on free mail (37%) and 222 of the 273 still queued (81%); every recipient an individual.

Top 3 reasons
1. **Wrong buyer and no clear offer.** v1 mailed individual desk workers about "energy" and "heart health and blood sugar", with no budget, no authority and no reason to answer a stranger. The ask ("Would that be useful for you? Just hit reply.") is open-ended and invites nothing specific. The poster newsletter added fear-framed health claims, which is also a compliance risk in SG and the UAE. Individuals were targeted from their personal sites, and the "personal line" inferred their screen habits, which reads as presumptuous.
2. **Weak sender and deliverability.** A 1-day-old free Gmail persona sent 40 emails in 11 minutes on a Saturday at 09:14 SGT / 05:14 UAE, with 5 bounces on day 1, an image-heavy HTML poster (21 images), a burst of 8 emails in one second on 16 Oct, and some addresses taken from harvesting tools. There was no domain authentication and no follow-up.
3. **Copy gave no reason to reply.** About 170 words, the sender opens with herself, subjects like "{{name}}, quick question" or health-fear headlines, no specific offer, no deadline, no low-effort next step, and no follow-up sequence. Also be honest about the sample: with about 100 delivered emails, zero replies is partly just a small sample (at a 1.5% reply rate you would see none about a quarter of the time). Fixing the three items above matters more than tweaking wording.

## 2. v2 strategy

**ICP (roles only, never personal health):** HR heads, people and culture managers, wellness/benefits leads at companies of about 50 to 1,000 staff, mostly desk-based (audit and advisory, legal, software, real estate), in Singapore, the UAE and India (Pune first). Only publicly listed business emails, taken from the company's own page. 10 pilot prospects are in `prospects.csv` (SG 4, UAE 3, India 3); MX for every domain verified on 2 Nov 2026 (this proves the domain takes mail, not that the mailbox exists; no SMTP probing was done). Three India or UAE leads use a public company inbox rather than a personal address and are marked confidence B.

**Offer:** one free 30-minute live virtual desk-strength session for the team: guided strength and mobility at the desk, no equipment, no sales pitch, up to 20 people (Susmit to confirm) on Zoom or Teams. The call to action is "reply yes plus a day", or "point me to the right person". Optional paid follow-on: a 4-week small-group programme, discussed only after the free session.

**Positioning and compliance:** talk about the team's working day, never an individual's body or health. No medical or outcome claims. Identify Sydney as writing on behalf of Susmit, include an opt-out line, and add every opt-out to do_not_send.txt the same day.

**Sender fix:** move to a mailbox on onlinefitnessworldwide.com with SPF, DKIM and DMARC, and warm up with a few real emails first. Susmit signing as himself will likely work better than the "Sydney" persona.

## 3. Cadence

- Sequence per prospect: email 1 on day 0, follow-up 1 after 4 working days, follow-up 2 after 9 working days, then stop. Two subject variants each, rotated A/B across prospects.
- Hard cap: **8 emails a day in total** (main, follow-ups and v1 follow-ups). Weekdays only, **Tue to Thu** preferred, sent between 09:00 and 11:00 in the recipient's local time (SGT 09:30 = 07:00 IST; GST 09:30 = 11:00 IST; India 10:00 IST). Randomise by 20 to 40 minutes; never send two in the same minute.
- Skip local public holidays. Deepavali/Diwali falls around 8 to 9 Nov 2026 (please confirm the SG and India dates), so wave 1 goes out before it and follow-ups restart afterwards.
- Pause for 2 days and re-check the list if there are 2 bounces in 7 days.

## 4. What to measure
- Delivered, hard bounces (target 0, pause above 2%), and spam or "unsolicited" rejections.
- Replies of any kind, and **positive replies** (yes, later, or referral to the right person). Track reply rate per variant (subject A vs B), per country, and per touch (main, FU1, FU2).
- Free sessions booked, attendees, and a one-line feedback question after the session.
- Conversion from free session to a paid pilot or programme.
- Opt-outs (target under 5%).
- Don't read much into 10 prospects. Judge the pilot as: bounces at 0; at least 1 positive reply or referral from 10. If that holds, extend to 40 to 60 prospects built the same way, then compare variants.
- Open tracking is off (plain text, no pixels), so reply and booking are the measures that count.

It does not replace midwifery, obstetric, medical or pelvic-health physiotherapy care. Every pregnant or postpartum client needs clearance from their midwife, doctor or pelvic health physiotherapist before starting or changing training. If a care team has set restrictions, follow those restrictions. New or worsening red-flag symptoms mean stop the session and refer — do not troubleshoot them with a harder or easier workout.

This is a starting framework of principles, screening questions and sample sessions. It is not an individual training plan, a medical protocol or a promise of any outcome.

## 1. Who this is for, and the clearance rule

This guide is for OnlineFitnessWorldwide coaches who support women who are pregnant, recently postpartum, or returning toward running and hybrid-race stations after birth. Use it to ask better questions, adapt sessions conservatively and hand off the right issues to the right teammate or clinician.

It is not for diagnosing pregnancy complications, pelvic-floor disorders, abdominal-wall conditions, or mental-health conditions. It is not a race plan. It is not a nutrition plan.

**The clearance rule is non-negotiable.** Before a pregnant or postpartum client starts with us, or before we change the demand of their training (new impact, heavier loading, longer sessions, or a return to running or race stations), they need clearance from their own midwife, doctor or pelvic health physiotherapist. “I feel fine” is not clearance. A coach’s screening form is not clearance. An OnlineFitnessWorldwide teammate, including Physio, is not a licensed clinician and cannot provide medical clearance.

A useful split, drawn from the coaching frame in Clapp and Cram, is:

- the **clinician** decides whether exercise is appropriate and whether any restrictions apply
- the **coach** then adapts load, intensity, position, rest and session length within that advice

If clearance is missing, delayed, unclear, or withdrawn, do not start or progress the plan. Offer to wait, help the client prepare questions for their care team, and only resume when written or clearly stated clinical advice is in place. Local care pathways differ around the world; use the client’s own midwife, obstetrician, general practitioner, family physician or pelvic health physiotherapist, and do not assume one country’s postnatal check timing applies everywhere.

## 2. Screening questions for onboarding

Ask these questions only with the client’s opt-in, in a private conversation or written intake they have agreed to complete. Pregnancy and postpartum details are health information. Explain why you are asking, let the client skip anything they do not want to share, and never press for birth-story detail they have not offered.

A warm way to open:

> “If you are comfortable, I would like to ask a few questions so I can coach you safely. You can skip any question. I am a coach, not a clinician, so I will not diagnose anything you tell me. If something needs a midwife, doctor or pelvic health physiotherapist, I will say so and we will wait for their advice before we train or change the plan.”

Use the answers to decide whether to wait for clearance, adapt the session, or stop and refer. Do not use them to give a diagnosis, a due-to-return date, or a guarantee that training is safe.

## 3. Red-flag symptoms that mean stop and refer

These lists are a conservative coaching stop-and-refer aid. They are not a complete medical checklist and not a tool for diagnosing the cause. If in doubt, stop and refer. Do not use a modified set to “see if it settles.”

**If the situation seems like an emergency** — including heavy bleeding, fainting, chest pain, severe breathlessness, severe headache, a sudden change in baby’s movements, or a client who looks very unwell — stop immediately, stay with them if you are on a live call while they (or someone with them) contacts their local emergency service or maternity unit, and do not wait for a coach or teammate to reply.

### During pregnancy: stop the session and contact the care team

The pregnancy signs below follow the widely used warning list in ACOG Committee Opinion 804, *Physical Activity and Exercise During Pregnancy and the Postpartum Period* (2020). The opinion was opened and the official page resolved on 10 November 2026 (Asia/Calcutta) at [this ACOG URL](https://www.acog.org/clinical/guidance/committee-opinion/2020/04/physical-activity-and-exercise-during-pregnancy-and-the-postpartum-period/). Coaches should treat the list as a reason to stop and refer, not as a diagnosis.

| Symptom | Coach action |
|:--|:--|
| Vaginal bleeding | Stop. Do not resume. The client should contact their midwife, doctor or maternity service promptly; heavy bleeding or feeling unwell is an emergency. |
| Fluid leakage (suspected waters breaking or amniotic fluid) | Stop. The client should contact their maternity care team the same day, urgently. |
| Regular painful contractions or regular painful tightenings | Stop. The client should contact their midwife or maternity service. Do not stretch, keep walking it off, or re-test with another set. |
| Chest pain | Stop and treat as an emergency if it is new or severe. Do not wait to “cool down and see.” |
| Dizziness or fainting | Stop in a safe position (seated or lying as comfort and pregnancy position allow). Fainting or ongoing dizziness needs urgent medical advice; do not continue lifting or standing work. |
| Headache | Stop. A new, severe or worsening headache, especially with visual changes, swelling or feeling unwell, needs prompt medical or maternity advice, not a shorter session. |
| Calf pain or swelling, especially if it is one-sided, red or warm | Stop. The client should seek prompt medical advice. Do not massage the calf, use a compression experiment, or continue lower-body loading. |
| Breathlessness before exertion (getting short of breath before they have even started meaningful work) | Stop. This is different from getting winded during hard work. Seek medical or maternity advice before any return. |
| Muscle weakness that affects balance | Stop any standing, lifting or stepping work where a fall is possible. Seek medical advice. |
| Reduced fetal movement, or a change from the baby’s usual movement pattern | Stop immediately. The client should contact their midwife or maternity unit the same day, without waiting for the next session. Coaches do not interpret movement charts. |
| Abdominal pain (also listed among ACOG 804 warning signs) | Stop. The client should contact their care team. Do not treat this as ordinary training soreness. |

WHO’s 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour also recommend regular activity in pregnancy and after birth when there is no contraindication, and they sit alongside — they do not replace — this stop-and-refer approach. The official publication page resolved on 10 November 2026 (Asia/Calcutta) at [WHO ISBN 9789240015128](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128).

Santos-Rocha’s edited collection is listed in the reading list as a further international-guideline stack for keeping screening and red-flag practice consistent across countries, not as a substitute for the client’s own clinician.

### After birth: stop and refer

Postpartum warning signs are not a second copy of the pregnancy table. Use the conservative list below, then involve the client’s midwife, doctor or pelvic health physiotherapist. Urgent medical signs should not wait for a coaching adaptation.

| Symptom | Coach action |
|:--|:--|
| Heavy bleeding, soaking through pads, passing large clots, or a sudden return of heavy bleeding | Stop. Seek urgent or emergency maternity or medical care. This is not a reason to “take it easier next time.” |
| Wound problems after a caesarean or perineal repair: opening, increasing redness, swelling, leaking fluid, or fever | Stop work that stresses the area. The client should contact their care team promptly. Coaches do not inspect, clean or judge healing on camera. |
| Pelvic heaviness, dragging, pressure or a sense of a bulge during or after training | Stop or immediately reduce load and impact. Refer to a pelvic health physiotherapist and hand off to Physio. Do not add running, jumps or harder “core” work. |
| Leaking of urine, gas or stool with training or daily tasks | Reduce or remove the aggravating task. Refer to a pelvic health physiotherapist. Leaking is a change-the-plan sign, not a badge of effort. |
| Pain that is severe, worsening, changing walking or lifting, or located at a wound, the pelvis, chest or calf | Stop the aggravating work. Severe, chest, or calf symptoms need medical advice on an urgent basis; other persistent pain is a Physio hand-off plus a clinician, not a mobility challenge. |
| Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, fainting, one-sided calf pain or swelling, or feeling severely unwell | Treat as urgent or emergency medical care, as in pregnancy. Postpartum clients can still present with these signs. |

Once symptoms have been reviewed, only resume or progress when the client’s own clinician has advised that it is appropriate. A teammate’s educational note is not that advice.

## 4. Training in pregnancy by trimester: principles, not a protocol

In the absence of complications or contraindications, regular physical activity is generally encouraged in pregnancy, with adjustments rather than an automatic stop. That is the shared direction of ACOG Committee Opinion 804, the WHO 2020 physical activity guidelines, and the coaching frame in Clapp and Cram: most healthy pregnancies can continue regular exercise, the clinician clears, and the coach adapts load, intensity and position.

These are principles for cleared clients. They are not a week-by-week medical protocol, and they do not promise particular labour, recovery or fitness outcomes.

**What usually continues**

- Strength work the client already knows, at a manageable effort, with steady breathing and enough rest
- Aerobic work they already tolerate, often judged with a simple talk test: they can still speak in short sentences
- A mix of muscle-strengthening and aerobic activity across the week, in line with the WHO 2020 public-health recommendation of at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity across the week when there is no contraindication
- Clients who were already doing vigorous work may be able to continue some of it if their care team agrees and symptoms stay quiet — this is not a reason to introduce vigorous or high-impact work for the first time in pregnancy

**What usually changes**

- **Positions.** After the first trimester, avoid prolonged lying flat on the back (motionless supine work). Prone work usually becomes impractical as the bump grows. Favour upright, inclined, side-lying or all-fours options. Stance and squat depth often need to widen or shorten so the bump is cleared.
- **Intensity.** Drop the aim of training to failure, heavy breath-holding, or chasing personal bests. Use more rest. Shorten the session when nausea, fatigue or heat is high. Avoid getting overheated and keep fluid intake sensible; this matters in warm climates and hot indoor rooms worldwide.
- **Impact and risk.** Reduce or remove tasks with a high risk of falling, contact or abdominal trauma. Running and jumping are not automatically banned for a previously active, cleared client, and they are not automatically continued either. Let symptoms, balance, pelvic comfort and clinical advice decide. Race-floor chaos, heavy sleds and burpee broad jumps are examples of work that often need an earlier conversation, not a later apology.
- **Equipment and loading.** Front-loaded or goblet positions often feel better than a bar against the abdomen. Change range before forcing the old range. There is no prize for keeping a pre-pregnancy load.

### First trimester

Energy, nausea, breast tenderness and a naturally lower tolerance for heat or long sessions are common, not universal. Many cleared clients can continue familiar training with shorter sessions, more rest and a lower ceiling on hard efforts. This is a poor time to start a brand-new high-impact or high-volume plan. Stop for any sign in section 3, including bleeding or reduced fetal movement once movements are being felt. Do not treat first-trimester tiredness as something to be trained through for sport.

### Second trimester

Some clients feel more comfortable in this window; some do not. Assume nothing from the trimester label alone. This is usually when position changes become essential: avoid prolonged supine work, adjust squat and hinge range around the bump, and offer a stable support for balance. Watch for new pelvic-girdle, pubic or back pain — reduce the aggravating stance or load and involve Physio rather than stretching into the pain. Keep strength regular and moderate. Revisit impact only if it remains comfortable, cleared and technically controlled.

### Third trimester

Range, balance and abdominal-wall pressure often change again. Session length, rest and exercise choice should get more generous, not more heroic. Stay upright where possible, keep breathing quiet and steady, and treat heaviness, leaking or midline doming as a reason to reduce load and refer, not to add more core. Some cleared clients still train close to birth; others need mostly walking, gentle strength and rest. There is no fixed “stop date” a coach should invent. Preparation for postpartum movement quality matters more than a final hard block.

Across all trimesters, avoid activities with a high risk of falling, contact or abdominal trauma, and follow any activity limits the care team has set (commonly including scuba and hard work at altitudes the client is not used to). Hybrid-race practice is still training: it is not a loophole around those limits.

## 5. Phased postpartum return

Return after birth is staged, symptom-led and clearance-dependent. It is not a countdown to a race. DiFiore’s postnatal-fitness work supports rebuilding in stages that start with breathing, core and posture, and handing clinical questions to a physiotherapist. Lindholm’s running-focused work supports a gradual return to running with symptom checks rather than a fixed date — directly relevant for clients who want the running legs of HYROX, DEKA or ATHX. Neither book is treated here as a protocol we have reproduced.

Caesarean birth, assisted birth, tears, twins, significant blood loss or any complication can reasonably slow the early stages. Follow the care team, not a generic week number.

### Stage A — Breathing, pelvic floor basics and posture

After clearance, early work is mostly about how the client breathes, stands, sits, walks and gets on and off a chair. Cue easy nasal or quiet breathing, a gentle exhale on effort, and a pelvic floor that can settle as well as lift — a light coordination cue, not a treatment programme of endless holds. Leave formal pelvic-floor assessment, internal examination and rehab dosing to a pelvic health physiotherapist. Physio can help with educational material; they cannot examine or clear the client.

Walking, posture changes for feeding and holding, and short movement snacks are usually more useful than a workout at this stage. No running, no jumps, no raced station work.

### Stage B — Strength rebuild

When clearance allows, use short sessions with one main lift, one related accessory between sets, and a small amount of daily-style core (see section 6). Choose ranges that do not provoke leaking, heaviness, pain or abdominal doming. Increase only one variable at a time — range, load, or repetitions — and only when the last sessions were comfortable and recovery (including sleep and feeding) can support it.

Treat the abdominal wall as a whole-body load and alignment issue, not a sit-up problem. That education frame comes from Bowman; the assessment still belongs with a pelvic health or women’s health physiotherapist. Avoid crunch-style work and forceful breath-holding while the wall is still finding capacity.

### Stage C — Impact readiness checks

These are coaching observations, not a pelvic-health assessment and not a pass/fail medical test. If any check is symptomatic or poorly controlled, do not “try a short jog anyway.” Reduce the task and refer to a pelvic health physiotherapist.

Before running or jumping, look for, over a few sessions:

- a quiet abdomen and pelvic floor during strength work (no new leaking, heaviness, dragging or painful pressure)
- comfortable walking at a decent everyday pace without later heaviness or pain
- single-leg strength and balance that looks controlled (for example a supported step-up or split squat)
- a small series of well-rested marches, skips or gentle hops that do not cause leaking, heaviness, pain, or a sudden change in technique

Failed or symptomatic checks mean stay in stage B and involve Physio plus the client’s clinician. Successful checks are not a race entry.

### Stage D — Gradual return to running and hybrid-race stations

Return to running with walk–jog fragments, full recovery between efforts, and a planned stop if symptoms appear during or in the hours after the session. Increase running time slowly. Do not add a station block and a new running progression in the same week if you can help it.

Bring stations back as skills, not as a fatigue test:

- **Sled (push or pull).** Start light, over a short distance, with an upright, comfortable posture and steady breathing. Stop if the client bears down, leaks, or feels heaviness or pain. Distance and load come later; racing a heavy sled does not.
- **Wall balls.** Start with a lighter ball, fewer repetitions and a depth the bump-free, rebuilt squat can own. Exhale on the throw. Watch the midline and pelvic floor. A thruster to a lower target, or a goblet squat plus an easy overhead pass, is an acceptable regression.
- **Burpee broad jumps.** These combine impact, a get-up and a jump, and should come last. Regress to an elevated or step-back burpee without the jump, or to a separate squat and hip hinge, until impact checks are quiet. Do not use this station as a conditioning finisher on the way back.

HYROX, DEKA and ATHX have different formats, loads and standards. Confirm current rules with the organiser. This guide is not affiliated with those events and does not set a race date. Once the client is cleared, symptom-quiet and moving well, Hybrid Race Coach owns periodised race planning; Women’s Health stays involved if pelvic-health or pregnancy-recovery questions remain.

## 6. Two sample sessions (Susmit’s format)

These are short, live-online examples for **cleared** clients. They are not a full programme and not a medical prescription. Allow about 25–30 minutes: a few minutes to prepare, one main lift with a related accessory done in the rest between main-lift sets, then a small amount of core and a check-in.

Prepare with easy marching or walking, comfortable joint movement, and one or two light rehearsal sets. Use a clear floor, stable furniture and a camera angle that shows the whole movement. Breathe throughout; do not hold the breath to force a repetition. Stop for any sign in section 3, or for leaking, heaviness, pain or midline doming.

**How the main lift and accessory fit together.** Complete a set of the main lift, then do a set of the accessory during that rest, then take any extra rest needed before the next main-lift set. Core comes after, not as a burnout.

**Daily core can mean light practice, not daily hard training.** On non-strength days, one easy set of a comfortable drill is enough. Skip it when sleep, feeding, symptoms or recovery say so.

### Session 1 — Pregnancy (cleared, example: second trimester)

For a client whose midwife, doctor or pelvic health physiotherapist has cleared continued strength training, and who has no red-flag symptoms. Positions stay off a prolonged flat back. Effort stays moderate: short sentences are still possible.

| Exercise | Reps | Description |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Main: goblet squat to a stable box or chair** | 2–3 sets × 6–8 | Hold a manageable weight close to the chest. Take a stance that clears the bump. Sit back and down until you lightly touch the box, then stand. Keep breathing. Use a higher box if depth feels cramped. |
| **Accessory, done between main-lift sets: supported Romanian deadlift** | 2–3 sets × 6–8 | Soften the knees, push the hips back, and keep the weight close. Stop where the spine stays long and comfortable. Hold a chair or rack for balance if needed. This is the between-sets exercise, not a second workout. |
| **Core: standing band or wall press-out** | 1–2 sets × 6–8 | Stand tall. Press a light band forward, or press your hands into a wall at chest height, without holding your breath or flaring the ribs. Slow and steady. If a band is not available, use the wall. |

A wider stance, a higher box, or an unweighted squat-to-box are the first regressions. This session is not a running session and not a station session.

### Session 2 — Postpartum (cleared, early strength rebuild)

For a client who has been cleared to begin easy strength work in the rebuild stage, with quiet symptoms and no wound, bleeding or pelvic-health red flags. This is not a return-to-running session. After a caesarean or while any wound is still settling, prefer chair-based work and only use the floor if the care team is happy and getting down and up is comfortable.

| Exercise | Reps | Description |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Main: sit-to-stand from a stable chair** | 2 sets × 6–8 | Sit tall. Exhale as you stand; sit down slowly. Use the chair arms if you need them. Add a light goblet hold only if the movement is already easy, the abdomen and pelvic floor stay quiet, and the agreed plan allows it. |
| **Accessory, done between main-lift sets: glute bridge or incline bridge** | 2 sets × 6–8 | Lie on your back only if that is comfortable; use an incline or stay with a standing hip hinge if getting to the floor is difficult. Feet planted. Exhale and lift the hips a comfortable height without forcing a lower-back arch. Stop for doming, heaviness or pain. |
| **Core: heel slides with easy breathing** | 1–2 sets × 4–6 each side | On your back with knees bent, or seated if lying is uncomfortable. Breathe in. As you exhale, slide one heel a short distance away without letting the abdomen dome or the back arch. Reset and switch sides. |

If sit-to-stand is already easy and quiet, the next main-lift option in a later session is a goblet squat to the same chair — not a jump, sled or wall-ball set. Keep each session’s main lift different across the week (for example a squat pattern one day and a hinge or upper-body pattern on another) rather than repeating this exact menu every time.

## 7. Menstrual-cycle note: flexible and symptom-led

Outside pregnancy, the menstrual cycle can change how training feels. Energy, comfort, sleep, cramps and mood may shift. Some clients want to train as usual; some want a lighter day. Both can be reasonable.

**Plan from the person in front of you, not from a colour-coded phase chart.** Track symptoms and adjust session length, intensity or rest when the client asks for it or clearly needs it. Do not require every woman to periodise her training by cycle phase.

The evidence for strict “cycle-syncing” or fixed phase-based programming (for example, always lifting heavy in one phase and only doing easy work in another) is weak and mixed. It is not a reliable rule for every woman, every cycle, or every sport. Kolić and Morse’s recent book on menstruation in sport and exercise is useful further reading for separating that hype from what we can actually support: individual symptom tracking and flexible planning, rather than one rule for every client. Full book text was not reviewed for this draft; that sentence follows the knowledge-base takeaway, not a page-level claim.

A missed, newly irregular or stopped cycle, especially with fatigue, stress, a large drop in eating, or recurrent bone or injury problems, is not a coaching puzzle and is not automatically “just stress” or “just sport.” Coaches recognise warning signs and refer. They do not diagnose low energy availability, the female athlete triad, or relative energy deficiency. Hand fueling concerns to Nutrition, bone-stress or injury concerns to Physio, and medical review to the client’s doctor (see section 8).

This cycle note does not apply as a training-periodisation tool during pregnancy. In the postpartum months, bleeding patterns vary, especially with feeding; do not interpret them. Unusual or heavy bleeding belongs with the care team (section 3).

Menopause and the midlife transition belong with Masters, not with a pregnancy-cycle chart.

## 8. Hand-offs

Stay in your lane, and say so clearly to the client. A hand-off is a support, not a diagnosis.

| Teammate | Hand off when | What Women’s Health keeps / what it does not do |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Physio** | Pain, pelvic-health symptoms (leaking, heaviness, dragging, bulge), abdominal-wall concerns including doming, impact-readiness questions once symptoms are present, or a suspected running-related problem. | We can reduce load and stop aggravating work. We do not examine, diagnose, treat, or clear the pelvic floor or abdominal wall. Formal assessment sits with a pelvic health physiotherapist. Physio offers educational review, not a clinical appointment. |
| **Nutrition** | Fueling for training, eating enough to support pregnancy, postpartum recovery or feeding, and any concern about low energy availability, restrictive eating, or pressure to “get the body back.” | We do not prescribe diets, supplements or weight-loss plans, and we do not set a postpartum body target. Suspected under-fuelling needs a qualified clinician and a qualified nutrition professional, not a tighter meal rule. |
| **Masters** | Clients around 40+, including those in perimenopause or menopause, when age-aware programming or menopause-related training questions are the main need. Pregnancy or postpartum and midlife can overlap. | Do not treat menopause as a postnatal issue, or every 40+ woman as postpartum. Share the client rather than inventing a blended protocol here. |
| **Hybrid Race Coach** | Periodised HYROX, DEKA or ATHX race plans, station skill under fatigue, and race-week structure — only after clearance, a quiet pelvic floor and abdominal wall, and a sensible strength and impact foundation. | We do not write race plans or honour an entry date that outruns readiness. We stay involved if pre- or postnatal questions remain during the plan. |
| **Mindset** | Mental skills for returning to training, confidence, and working with broken sleep or race nerves. | Mindset is not therapy and not clinical mental-health care. If a client is distressed, unsafe, or may have depression or anxiety that needs care — including postnatal mood concerns — encourage them to speak to their midwife, doctor or a qualified mental-health professional. Do not coach through a crisis. |
| **Social / Video** | Anything that might be published: educational explainers, session demos, or general women’s-health education. | Drafts can be offered for review. Do not send client stories, testimonials, images, or health details to publishing teammates without the client’s explicit, specific consent. Do not invent results. No prices and no implied endorsement by any professional body. |

If more than one teammate is involved, say who owns the next decision so the client is not given three plans at once.
