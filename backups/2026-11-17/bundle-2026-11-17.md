## Books (28)


### Running technique, gait and footwear coaching (education; Physio reviews anything painful)
#### 1. Running Mechanics and Gait Analysis
- **Author:** Reed Ferber, Shari Macdonald
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=qPB6DwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Evidence review (250+ peer-reviewed papers) of running biomechanics, gait analysis and overuse-injury prevention, written for clinicians, coaches and trainers.
  2. Connects gait to muscular strength, flexibility and alignment, and explains kinetic-chain interactions behind common overuse injuries.
  3. Basis for a simple remote video-gait checklist; assessing or treating injuries stays with Physio and the client's own clinician.
#### 2. Running Form: How to Run Faster and Prevent Injury
- **Author:** Owen Anderson
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=DcNKDwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Focused on making key improvements in running form for better performance with less injury risk.
  2. Gives a coaching vocabulary for form changes that can be cued in short, simple drills.
  3. Form changes are introduced gradually; no promise of faster times or zero injuries in our drafts.
#### 3. Injury-Free Running: Your Illustrated Guide to Biomechanics, Gait Analysis, and Injury Prevention
- **Author:** Tom C. Michaud
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=EM6cEQAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Illustrated guide linking anatomy and biomechanics to strengthening exercises and rehab protocols for runners.
  2. Explains impact forces travelling through the body at each foot strike and why tissue load matters.
  3. Strength exercises suit Run Coach and Strength Coach plans; rehab protocols are Physio-reviewed references only.
#### 4. Tread Lightly: Form, Footwear, and the Quest for Injury-Free Running
- **Author:** Peter Larson, Bill Katovsky
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=8uRDCgAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Balanced look at running form, footwear and barefoot/minimalist running, drawing on footwear scientists, biomechanists, coaches, podiatrists and physical therapists.
  2. Argues repetitive stress drives most running injuries and moves beyond the pronation-control model of shoe choice.
  3. Supports neutral, non-salesy footwear FAQs: no brand recommendations, and any shoe transition is gradual.
#### 5. Fixing Your Feet: Injury Prevention and Treatments for Athletes (6th ed.)
- **Author:** John Vonhof
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=OIMWDgAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Covers how anatomy, biomechanics and footwear interact to keep feet healthy, for individual and team care.
  2. New chapter reframes blister prevention around shear forces.
  3. Practical foot-care checklist for long runs and race days; anything beyond minor blister care goes to a clinician.

### Road-race programming from 5K to marathon for recreational runners
#### 6. Faster Road Racing: 5K to Half Marathon
- **Author:** Pete Pfitzinger, Philip Latter
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=xxQxBQAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Training plans for the most popular road distances, 5K to half marathon.
  2. Adds advice on recovery, cross-training, nutrition, tapering and masters running.
  3. Plan structures are references; every plan is scaled to the client's history, schedule and time zone.
#### 7. Hansons Half-Marathon Method: Run Your Best Half-Marathon the Hansons Way
- **Author:** Luke Humphrey, Kevin Hanson
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=J6CIBwAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Half-marathon plans for intermediate and advanced runners plus a 'Just Finish' programme for newer runners.
  2. Explains SOS ('something of substance') workouts (speed, strength, tempo, long) alongside easy runs, and pacing-based goal setting.
  3. Shows how to adapt plans to busy schedules and missed workouts, which suits online clients.
#### 8. Marathon: The Ultimate Training Guide
- **Author:** Hal Higdon
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=J2q8mxzw6IsC
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Updated edition focused on first marathoners, women runners and people new to the sport.
  2. Covers building mileage, long runs, speedwork for distance runners and planning for a peak race.
  3. Plain-language source for first-marathon education emails and FAQs.
#### 9. Runner's World Run Less, Run Faster (revised ed.)
- **Author:** Bill Pierce, Scott Murr, Ray Moss
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=8R9OAQAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. The Furman Institute (FIRST) three-runs-a-week programme with a quality-over-quantity approach.
  2. Adds novice sections and sets out to limit overtraining and burnout by pairing fewer runs with cross-training.
  3. Useful model for clients who also lift; the publisher's 'train less, run faster' promise is never repeated as a guarantee.
#### 10. Personal Best Running: Coach Coogan's Strategies for the Mile to the Marathon
- **Author:** Mark Coogan, Scott Douglas
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=NySBEAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. Training and racing strategies from the mile to the marathon, from a coach of Olympic and champion runners.
  2. Stresses that running should never cost overall physical or mental health and shouldn't take over life.
  3. Values-led tone that matches our coaching: performance inside a healthy, balanced life.
#### 11. Running Your First Marathon: The Complete 20-Week Marathon Training Plan
- **Author:** Andrew Kastor
- **Google Books:** https://books.google.com/books?id=TqucEAAAQBAJ
- **Takeaways:**
  1. A 20-week plan for a full or half marathon from a coach of elite runners.
  2. Focuses on setting achievable goals, building fitness and staying motivated through the build.
  3. Good structure for a first-marathon client onboarding sequence (no promised finish times).

## 1. Running-programming principles — one-pager for clients who also lift

**DRAFT · Proposed**

Run to keep running.
Health first, times second.
In the spirit of Coach Coogan's view that running should never cost your physical or mental health (source note: Coogan & Douglas, *Personal Best Running*, KB entry 13 takeaway — paraphrase, not a quote).

### 1.1 The effort scale we use

No jargon. One scale for every plan.

| Effort | How it feels | Talk test | RPE (0–10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Easy** | Relaxed. You could keep going. | Full sentences. | 3–4 |
| **Steady** | Working, but in control. | Short sentences. | 5–6 |
| **Tempo** ("comfortably hard") | Strong, focused, sustainable for a while. | A few words at a time. | 7 |
| **Intervals** | Hard, but repeatable. Not a sprint. | One or two words. | 8 |
| **Strides** | Quick, light, relaxed. 15–20 seconds. Full walk-back rest. | — | Fast, not strained |

RPE = rate of perceived exertion: how hard it feels to you, 0 (rest) to 10 (max).
Heat, hills, altitude, poor sleep and fasting all make the same pace feel harder. **Run the effort, not the pace.**

Clients who like numbers can add pace zones later (source note: Daniels' *Running Formula* prescribes paces from a fitness score rather than vague "easy/hard", KB entry 1). Pace targets stay inside the plan. No dashboards.

### 1.2 Easy most of the time

- Most weekly running is **easy**. A smaller share is genuinely hard (source note: Fitzgerald, *80/20 Running*, KB entry 5).
- Avoid the grey middle: every run "kind of hard" adds fatigue without a clear purpose.
- Walk breaks are allowed in any easy run. Walk-run is real running.

### 1.3 A few purposeful quality sessions

- **1 quality session** a week for new runners (often strides or short tempo).
- **2 quality sessions** a week at most for experienced runners, one of which can be the long run.
- Three-run weeks: each run has a job (quality over quantity — source note: Pierce, Murr & Moss, *Run Less, Run Faster*, KB entry 13). The book's "train less, run faster" line is the publisher's claim. We never promise it.

### 1.4 Weekly progression limits (proposed house rules)

These are cautious coaching rules, not research figures.

- Raise **one** thing at a time: frequency, or duration, or intensity. Never all three in one week.
- Grow total weekly running time in small steps. If a week felt hard, repeat it.
- Every 3rd or 4th week: a **lighter week** (roughly a third less running, no new hard sessions).
- New to running, coming back after a break, or 40+: smaller steps, more repeats (co-planned with Masters for clients roughly 40+).
- Form or footwear changes are introduced gradually, never in the same week as a big volume jump.

### 1.5 Missed-session rules

Life happens. The plan bends.

| Missed | What we do |
|---|---|
| 1 easy run | Let it go. Don't double up. |
| 1 quality session | Move it only if there are 48 hours before the next hard run or heavy leg day. Otherwise skip it. |
| The long run | Move it within the week if you can. If not, do the next scheduled long run, shortened a little. |
| 1 full week (busy, travel, mild illness) | Repeat the last completed week. |
| 2 weeks | Go back two weeks in the plan. |
| More than 2 weeks, or any illness that needed a doctor | Coach rebuilds the plan. Illness or pain: Physio or the client's clinician first. |

Source note: Humphrey & Hanson, *Hansons Half-Marathon Method*, covers adapting plans to busy schedules and missed workouts (KB entry 13). The table above is our own proposed version, not the book's rules.

### 1.6 Combining with 2 or 3 strength sessions

Co-owned with Strength Coach. Rule of thumb: **hard days hard, easy days easy.**

- Don't put a heavy lower-body session the day **before** the long run or an interval session.
- If a run and a lift share a day, put the more important one first, with as many hours between them as the day allows.
- Easy runs can sit the day after a heavy leg day.
- Strength stays in the plan through race build-ups. Load and volume ease off in the final 1–2 weeks before a race (Strength Coach sets the detail).
- Source note: Anderson, *Running Science* (KB entry 5), on running economy being trainable through strength and technique, not only more miles. No outcome is promised.

**Sample week — 3 runs + 2 strength**

| Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat | Sun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strength (full body) | Quality run | Rest or walk | Easy run | Strength (upper-led) | Long run | Rest |

**Sample week — 4 runs + 3 strength**

| Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat | Sun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strength (lower-led) | Easy run + strides | Quality run | Strength (upper-led) | Easy run | Long run | Strength (light, full body) or rest |

Swap days to fit the client's week, time zone and weekend. In the Gulf and elsewhere, the weekend may fall on different days — the shape matters, not the day names.

### 1.7 Climate and environment

- Hot or humid weather (Gulf summers, South-East Asia, northern Australia, US summers): run earlier or later, shorten, slow down by effort, and follow Coaching Standards heat rules. Feeling unwell in the heat means **stop and seek help**, not push through.
- Cold, dark or icy winters (northern Europe, Canada, northern US): treadmill or indoor options are fine. Safety and visibility first.
- Altitude, travel and jet lag: treat as reasons to ease off, not to test fitness.

## 2. Four plan templates

**DRAFT · Proposed starting templates.** Every plan is scaled to the client's running history, schedule, climate, time zone and strength block. Nothing here is a promised result or finish time.

**Before any plan starts (all templates):**
- Adults only (18+). Under-18s are not programmed — flag to Susmit and Coaching Standards.
- Pre-session screening per Coaching Standards. Any current pain or injury history → Physio first.
- Pregnancy, postpartum, medical conditions or medication → Women's Health / Metabolic Health and the client's clinician first.
- Every run starts with 5–10 minutes of brisk walking or very easy jogging, and ends with 5 minutes of walking.

**Distance key:** 5K = 5 km (3.1 mi) · 10K = 10 km (6.2 mi) · half marathon = 21.1 km (13.1 mi) · marathon = 42.2 km (26.2 mi).

**How to read progressions and regressions (all templates):**
- **Regress** = repeat the week, go back a week, swap a run for a brisk walk, or add walk breaks.
- **Progress** = only when the current week felt manageable and recovery was good. Add strides, or a few minutes to one run. Never skip ahead more than one step.

### 2A. Walk-run to 5K — 8 weeks

Source note: plan structure is our own. Faster Road Racing (Pfitzinger & Latter) is a reference for 5K-to-half plan structures (KB entry 13); this beginner template is not taken from it.

**Who:** new runners, returning runners (no current pain), anyone who wants to start with walking.
**Standard:** 3 walk-run sessions a week, a rest or strength day between them. (This template is already a 3-run week.)
**Minimum variant:** 2 sessions a week + 1 brisk 30-minute walk. Take 12 weeks instead of 8: repeat weeks 2, 4, 6 and 7.

| Week | Each session (after warm-up) | Total run-walk time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 × (run 1 min / walk 2 min) | ~24 min |
| 2 | 8 × (run 1.5 min / walk 1.5 min) | ~24 min |
| 3 | 6 × (run 2 min / walk 1.5 min) | ~21 min |
| 4 (lighter) | 6 × (run 2 min / walk 2 min) | ~24 min |
| 5 | 5 × (run 3 min / walk 1.5 min) | ~23 min |
| 6 | 4 × (run 5 min / walk 1.5 min) | ~26 min |
| 7 | 3 × (run 8 min / walk 1.5 min) | ~28 min |
| 8 | 2 × (run 12 min / walk 1 min), then optional 5 km (3.1 mi) walk-run at your own pace | ~25–45 min |

- "Run" = easy effort. Slow enough to talk.
- Finishing 5K with walk breaks is a full finish.
- **Regression:** repeat any week. Go back a week after a missed week.
- **Progression (only if week 8 felt easy):** add 4 × 15-second strides to one session a week, then move to 2B.

### 2B. 10K — 8 to 10 weeks

Source note: Pfitzinger & Latter, *Faster Road Racing* (KB entry 13), reference for 5K–half marathon plan structures. The table is our own.

**Who:** can run about 30 minutes continuously at easy effort (with or without short walk breaks), or has finished 2A.
**Standard:** 4 runs a week. **3-run variant:** drop the Easy run 2 column.
**8-week version:** skip weeks 3 and 7.

| Week | Easy run 1 | Quality run | Easy run 2 | Long run (easy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 30 min | 25 min easy + 4 × 20 s strides | 25 min | 40 min |
| 2 | 30 min | 6 × (2 min steady / 2 min easy jog) | 25 min | 45 min |
| 3 | 30 min | 3 × 5 min tempo, 2 min easy between | 30 min | 50 min |
| 4 (lighter) | 25 min | 25 min easy + 6 × 20 s strides | 20 min | 40 min |
| 5 | 35 min | 6 × 3 min intervals, 2 min easy jog | 30 min | 55 min |
| 6 | 35 min | 2 × 10 min tempo, 3 min easy between | 30 min | 60 min |
| 7 | 35 min | 5 × 4 min intervals, 2.5 min easy jog | 30 min | 65 min |
| 8 (lighter) | 30 min | 20 min tempo or steady, broken if needed | 25 min | 50 min |
| 9 | 35 min | 3 × 8 min tempo, 2 min easy between | 30 min | 60 min |
| 10 (race) | 25 min + 4 strides | 20 min easy + 3 × 2 min steady | Rest or 15 min easy | **10K race or solo 10 km (6.2 mi)** |

- **3-run variant:** quality run + long run + one easy run. Swap one easy run for 30–40 min of low-impact cross-training (bike, rower, elliptical) if wanted.
- **Regression:** replace intervals with steady efforts; cap the long run at the previous week's time; walk breaks always allowed.
- **Progression:** add 5 minutes to Easy run 1 in weeks 5–9; add one rep to the quality run. Only one change per week.
- **Race day:** start easier than you feel. Pace by effort. Finish strong if you can.

### 2C. Half marathon — 12 weeks (with a Hansons-style "Just Finish" option)

Source notes: Humphrey & Hanson, *Hansons Half-Marathon Method* (KB entry 13) describes plans for intermediate/advanced runners, a "Just Finish" programme for newer runners, and SOS ("something of substance") workouts — speed, strength, tempo, long — alongside easy runs. Pierce, Murr & Moss, *Run Less, Run Faster* (KB entry 13) is the model for a three-run, quality-over-quantity week with cross-training. The tables below are our own drafts "in the style of" these approaches, not reproductions of either book's plans.

**Who (standard):** comfortable running 45–60 minutes easy; has run 10K or similar.
**Who ("Just Finish"):** newer runners whose goal is to get round healthy. Walk-run is welcome.

#### Standard — 4 runs a week

| Week | Easy run | Quality run (SOS) | Easy run | Long run (easy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 35 min | 30 min easy + 6 × 20 s strides | 30 min | 60 min |
| 2 | 35 min | 6 × 3 min intervals, 2 min jog | 30 min | 65 min |
| 3 | 40 min | 2 × 10 min tempo, 3 min jog | 30 min | 70 min |
| 4 (lighter) | 30 min | 30 min easy + strides | 25 min | 55 min |
| 5 | 40 min | 5 × 5 min steady-to-tempo, 2 min jog | 35 min | 80 min |
| 6 | 40 min | 3 × 10 min tempo, 3 min jog | 35 min | 85 min |
| 7 | 45 min | 8 × 2 min intervals, 2 min jog | 35 min | 95 min |
| 8 (lighter) | 35 min | 20 min tempo | 30 min | 70 min |
| 9 | 45 min | 2 × 15 min tempo, 3 min jog | 35 min | 100 min |
| 10 | 45 min | 6 × 4 min intervals, 2 min jog | 35 min | 105–110 min (about 16–19 km / 10–12 mi, depending on pace) |
| 11 (taper) | 35 min | 3 × 8 min tempo | 30 min | 70 min |
| 12 (race) | 25 min + strides | 20 min easy + 3 × 3 min at goal effort | Rest or 15 min easy | **Half marathon 21.1 km (13.1 mi)** |

#### 3-run variant (Run Less–style)

Keep the Quality run and Long run columns. Replace both easy runs with one easy run **or** with 30–45 minutes of low-impact cross-training. Strength sessions stay as planned with Strength Coach.

#### "Just Finish" option — 3 or 4 sessions a week, 12 weeks

- Every run is easy. Walk-run is the default (e.g. run 4 min / walk 1 min, adjusted to the client).
- **One SOS-style session a week** stays gentle: strides, or 3–5 × 3 min steady.
- Long run (walk-run counts) builds: 50 → 60 → 70 → 55 (lighter) → 80 → 90 → 100 → 75 (lighter) → 110 → 120–135 min peak (week 10) → 75 (taper) → race.
- On the day: walk breaks planned from the start. Finishing is the goal.

- **Regression (both):** repeat a week; drop the quality run to strides; cap the long run.
- **Progression (standard only):** add 5 min to one easy run; add a rep to intervals. Never both in one week.
- Race fueling and hydration plan → **Nutrition**.

### 2D. First marathon — 16 to 20 weeks

Source notes: Higdon, *Marathon: The Ultimate Training Guide* (KB entry 13) for first-marathon mileage build, long runs and peak-race planning; Kastor, *Running Your First Marathon* (KB entry 13) for a 20-week structure with achievable goals. The phases below are our own draft, not either author's plan. No finish times are set or promised.

**Who:** has run a half marathon or comfortably runs 60+ minutes easy, with no current pain. Clients roughly 40+ are co-planned with Masters.
**Standard:** 4 runs a week. **3-run variant:** quality run + mid-week easy run + long run, plus optional cross-training.
**16-week version:** drop weeks 1–4 (start at Base 2) if the client already runs 60+ minutes easy regularly.

| Phase | Weeks (20-wk plan) | Focus | Long run (easy, walk breaks allowed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base 1 | 1–4 | Easy running, strides, habit | 60 → 70 → 75 → 60 min (lighter) |
| Base 2 | 5–8 | Add steady and short tempo | 80 → 90 → 95 → 75 min (lighter) |
| Build | 9–12 | Tempo + one longer interval set every other week | 105 → 120 → 130 → 90 min (lighter) |
| Specific | 13–17 | Goal-effort segments inside long runs | 140 → 150 → 120 (lighter) → 165 → 180 min peak (about 29–32 km / 18–20 mi, whichever comes first) |
| Taper | 18–19 | Less volume, keep a little intensity | 120 → 90 min |
| Race | 20 | Rest, short easy runs, race | **Marathon 42.2 km (26.2 mi)** |

**Typical week (Build / Specific):**
- Easy run 40–50 min
- Quality run: tempo (e.g. 2 × 15–20 min) or goal-effort segments
- Easy run 30–45 min (dropped in the 3-run variant)
- Long run as above. Some long runs include 20–40 min at goal effort near the end.

- **Peak long run is capped by time** so slower runners aren't on their feet for far longer than faster ones. Walk-run marathoners are welcome.
- **Regression:** repeat any block week; shorten the long run to the last completed one; drop goal-effort segments.
- **Progression:** add 5–10 min to the mid-week easy run in Build. One change per week.
- Race fueling, gut practice on long runs and hydration → **Nutrition**. Heat on race day → Coaching Standards heat rules.
- Race-week checklist (proposed): sleep, familiar kit, nothing new on race day, warm-up of easy jogging and a few strides, start by effort, foot-care prep (section 4).

## 3. Remote run-form review protocol

**DRAFT · Proposed.** Coaching observations only. Never diagnosis. Any pain → Physio.

Source notes (KB entry 13 takeaways):
- Ferber & Macdonald, *Running Mechanics and Gait Analysis* — evidence-review framework linking gait to strength, flexibility and alignment; basis for a simple remote checklist. Assessing or treating injuries stays with Physio.
- Anderson, *Running Form* — a coaching vocabulary for gradual form changes, cued in short, simple drills. No promise of faster times or fewer injuries.
- Michaud, *Injury-Free Running* — links biomechanics and impact forces to strength exercises for runners; rehab protocols are Physio-reviewed references only.

### 3.1 Before filming: consent and privacy

- Written consent to record, store and review the video (Privacy & Data Care owns the wording and storage).
- Faces don't need to be visible. Avoid filming house numbers, street names or anything that shows where the client lives.
- Strip location data from the file where the phone allows it.
- Videos are used for this client's review only. Never for content, unless separate written consent and Susmit's approval.

### 3.2 Filming instructions

- **Phone:** landscape, steady (tripod, wall, or a helper). Hip height.
- **Distance:** about 3–5 m (10–16 ft) from the runner, so the whole body is in frame, head to feet.
- **Where:** a flat path, track, or treadmill. Good light. Plain background if possible.
- **Clothing:** fitted enough to see knees and hips. Usual running shoes.
- **Clips (10–20 seconds each, after a 10-minute easy warm-up):**
  1. **Side view** at easy effort (runner passes the phone, or treadmill side-on).
  2. **Rear view** at easy effort (running away from the phone, or treadmill from behind).
  3. **Side view** at a faster, steady-to-tempo effort.
  4. Optional: **front view** at easy effort.
- **Slow motion:** helpful if the phone has it. Not required.
- Treadmill and outdoor running can look a little different. Note which was used, and re-film the same way next time.

### 3.3 8-point gait checklist (observation only)

| # | Look at | View | What we note (plain language) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Posture** | Side | Tall, slight forward lean from the ankles, or bent at the waist / leaning back? |
| 2 | **Head and gaze** | Side / rear | Eyes ahead, neck relaxed, or looking down / chin poking? |
| 3 | **Arm action** | Side / rear | Relaxed swing, elbows bent, or crossing the body / tense shoulders? |
| 4 | **Where the foot lands** | Side | Lands roughly under the body, or well out in front (overstriding)? |
| 5 | **Foot strike** | Side | Heel, midfoot or forefoot — **noted neutrally**. No style is "right" for everyone; we only look at it alongside #4. |
| 6 | **Cadence** | Side | Steps per minute (count steps in 30 seconds × 2). A baseline only. Any change is small and gradual. |
| 7 | **Knee and hip control** | Rear / front | Knee path stays fairly in line, or drifts in? Does one side of the pelvis drop a lot? |
| 8 | **Rhythm and symmetry** | All | Do both sides look and sound similar? Heavy or noisy landing? Any visible limp? |

**Red flags — stop the review and refer to Physio:** visible limping, guarding a body part, the client reports pain during or after the run, or a big left-right difference that came on recently.

### 3.4 Cue and drill menu — pick 2–3 per review, never more

| Observation | Simple cue | Drill or exercise (with Strength Coach) |
|---|---|---|
| Overstriding (#4) | "Land under you." "Quick, light feet." | Strides focusing on quick feet; a small, gradual cadence nudge on easy runs |
| Leaning at the waist (#1) | "Run tall. Lean from the ankles." | Wall lean drill; A-march |
| Tense or crossing arms (#3) | "Elbows back, hands relaxed." | Seated or standing arm-swing drill |
| Looking down (#2) | "Eyes on the horizon." | Easy run with a far-off focus point |
| Heavy landing (#8) | "Run quiet." | Pogo hops (low and easy), skipping |
| Knee drift / pelvic drop (#7) | "Knee over toes." | Single-leg strength: step-ups, split squats, side-lying hip work, single-leg balance (Strength Coach programmes; Michaud as reference) |
| Low cadence vs. easy rhythm (#6) | "Shorter, quicker steps." | Short strides; metronome on part of one easy run |

Rules:
- One main cue per run. Most of the run is still just running.
- Change slowly. Form changes are added over weeks, not days (Anderson, as above).
- No promise that a change makes anyone faster or injury-free.

### 3.5 Re-film schedule

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Week 0 | Baseline film. Choose 2–3 cues/drills. |
| Week 2–3 | Re-film same angles, same surface. Keep, adjust, or drop each cue. |
| Week 6 | Re-film. Decide whether the change is now automatic. Add a new focus only then. |
| Every 8–12 weeks, or a new race block | Optional check-in film. |

Any new pain at any point → stop form work, refer to Physio.

## 4. Neutral footwear and foot-care FAQ

**DRAFT · Proposed. For Physio review before use.** No brand recommendations. No affiliate links. Education only.

Source notes (KB entry 13 takeaways):
- Larson & Katovsky, *Tread Lightly* — balanced look at form, footwear and barefoot/minimalist running; argues repetitive stress drives most running injuries and moves beyond the pronation-control model of shoe choice.
- Vonhof, *Fixing Your Feet* (6th ed.) — how anatomy, biomechanics and footwear interact; reframes blister prevention around shear (rubbing and sliding) forces; anything beyond minor blister care goes to a clinician.

**Q1. Which running shoe should I buy?**
We don't recommend brands or models.
Pick a shoe that feels comfortable from the first steps, fits well, and suits where you run.
Try a few. Comfort is a good guide.

**Q2. How should a running shoe fit?**
Snug at the heel. Room to wiggle your toes.
Roughly a thumb's width of space in front of your longest toe.
Try shoes later in the day, with the socks you run in.

**Q3. Do I need a "stability" or "motion control" shoe?**
Not automatically.
Thinking has moved on from choosing shoes only by how much the foot rolls in (Larson & Katovsky).
Comfort and gradual change matter more. If you have pain, see Physio — not a new shoe first.

**Q4. Should I switch to minimalist or barefoot-style shoes?**
You can. Go slowly.
Start with short, easy runs or walking in them. Keep your usual shoes for the rest.
Increase over weeks to months. Calf or foot soreness that lingers means slow down — any pain goes to Physio.

**Q5. When should I replace my shoes?**
There's no single number.
Signs: the cushioning feels flat, the upper or sole is worn unevenly, or your legs feel more beaten up than usual.
Rotating two pairs is fine if you want to.

**Q6. How do I prevent blisters?**
Blisters come from rubbing and sliding (shear) (Vonhof).
- Well-fitting shoes and socks that wick moisture. Avoid thick cotton on long runs.
- Lace firmly enough that your heel doesn't slip.
- Keep feet dry. Trim toenails straight across.
- Test any anti-rub product or tape on training runs, never first on race day.

**Q7. I've got a blister. What now?**
Small and intact: keep it clean and protect it from more rubbing.
Large, very painful, red, hot, swollen, or showing signs of infection: see a clinician.
If you have diabetes or poor sensation in your feet: see a clinician for any foot wound. Don't self-treat.

**Q8. What about black toenails, calluses and hot spots?**
A "hot spot" is the warning sign before a blister. Stop and fix it (sock, lace, tape) if you can.
Black toenails often mean the shoe is too short or the foot slides on downhills. Check fit and lacing.
Painful or thick calluses, or nail problems that don't settle: see a clinician or podiatrist.

**Q9. What should I do with my feet before a long run or race?**
- Nothing new on race day: same shoes, socks and tape you trained in.
- Nails trimmed a few days before, not the night before.
- Hot or wet weather: pack a spare pair of socks for long efforts if practical.
- After: check your feet. Clean, dry, look for blisters or marks.

**Q10. Does running on hard surfaces or treadmills hurt my feet?**
Your body adapts to the surfaces you train on, gradually.
Mix surfaces if you can. Change surfaces slowly, the same as shoes.
Running on a treadmill is fine, including in heat, cold or darkness.

> **Physio review note (for Physio):** Please check Q3, Q4, Q7 and Q8 for scope and safe-referral wording, and confirm whether to add a line on numbness, tingling or persistent foot pain going straight to a clinician. Run Coach has not added any treatment advice beyond "protect and refer".

## 5. Return-to-run progression outline

> ### ⚠️ USE ONLY AFTER PHYSIO OR CLINICIAN CLEARANCE
> **DRAFT · Proposed.** Run Coach does not decide when someone is ready to run again. Physio or the client's own clinician clears the client, sets the start stage and sets the criteria for moving on. This outline is a coaching framework for them to edit, not a medical protocol. Bone-stress injuries, surgery and red-flag symptoms follow the clinician's own plan only.

Source note: Harrast (ed.), *Clinical Care of the Runner* (KB entry 6, 10 November 2026) supports Physio in deciding when a running issue needs a clinician rather than a coaching tweak; Michaud (KB entry 13) for strength exercises tied to biomechanics. No stage below is taken from either book.

**Before stage 1, confirm in writing (Physio / clinician):**
- Clearance to start running, and the start stage.
- Any limits (surface, distance, days between runs).
- The symptom rule to use between stages (Physio sets it; Run Coach doesn't invent one).

| Stage | Proposed content (3 sessions a week, a rest day between) | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Brisk walking 20–30 min + Physio / Strength Coach exercises | Physio says so |
| 1 | Walk-run: 1 min run / 2 min walk × 5–8 | Physio's criteria are met across 2–3 sessions |
| 2 | Walk-run: 2 min run / 1 min walk × 6–8 | As above |
| 3 | Walk-run: 4–5 min run / 1 min walk, total 25–30 min | As above |
| 4 | Continuous easy running 20–30 min, flat and soft-ish surface | As above |
| 5 | Easy running 30–40 min; add a 4th run only if Physio agrees | As above |
| 6 | Add strides, then gentle steady efforts; hills last | As above |
| 7 | Rejoin the regular plan (section 2) one step below where the client left it | Physio and coach agree |

**At any stage:**
- Pain, swelling, limping or symptoms returning → stop running that day, back one stage, and tell Physio.
- Never two progressions in one week (e.g. longer **and** faster).
- Strength work continues throughout, programmed with Strength Coach and Physio.

## 6. Time-of-day and fasting-period adjustments (incl. Ramadan)

**DRAFT · Proposed.** Education only — not medical, nutrition or religious advice. Fasting-period fueling and hydration → **Nutrition**. Medical conditions, medication, pregnancy → **Metabolic Health / Women's Health** and the client's clinician. Respectful, non-judgemental religious framing and heat rules → **Coaching Standards**.

Source notes (KB entry 13 takeaways):
- Rowland, *The Athlete's Clock* — how the body clock and time affect performance, including whether there's an optimal time of day to train.
- Reilly, Atkinson & Waterhouse, *Biological Rhythms and Exercise* — rhythms of different cycle lengths and what they mean for practitioners, incl. shift workers and travellers.
- Bouhlel & Shephard, *Optimizing Physical Performance During Fasting and Dietary Restriction* — how sustained fasting affects metabolism and performance; covers religious and non-religious fasting.
- Chtourou, *Effects of Ramadan Fasting on Health and Athletic Performance* (KB entry 13, #20) — Ramadan fasting and performance, training adaptations, sleep and nutrition; supports session timing around iftar and suhoor.
No research numbers are quoted; none were read.

### 6.1 Time of day

- **Consistency first, ideal timing second.** The best time to run is the one the client can keep.
- Plans are written in the **client's** time zone, whatever and wherever that is.
- **Morning runners:** longer warm-up; save the hardest sessions for when the body feels awake, or accept that early sessions feel harder.
- **Evening runners:** keep hard sessions away from bedtime if sleep suffers.
- **Hot climates:** early morning or after sunset. Heat beats any "best time" — follow Coaching Standards heat rules.
- **Shift workers:** anchor runs to the client's own wake time, not the clock. Hard sessions after good sleep, not after a night shift.
- **Travel and jet lag:** easy runs only for the first days; no key session or race-effort test right after a long flight.
- **Race prep:** if a race starts early, do some key sessions at that time of day in the final weeks.

### 6.2 Ramadan and other fasting periods

Ask, don't assume. Not every client fasts. Each client chooses how they observe.

**First conversation (coach prompts):**
- "Are you fasting this month? Would you like us to adjust your running?"
- "When are suhoor and iftar where you are?" (times vary by location and season)
- "How is your sleep looking this month?"
- "Any medical condition or medication?" → if yes, clinician and Metabolic Health first.

**Proposed load adjustments:**
- Lower the target: maintain, don't build. Race build-ups pause or slow down where possible.
- Cut volume and intensity. Fewer hard sessions; shorter long runs.
- Easy runs and walk-run are fine. Effort, not pace.
- Strength stays, lighter and shorter (with Strength Coach).

**Session timing options (client chooses):**

| Option | Suits | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Short easy run before iftar** | Easy runs only | Keep it short and easy; food and drink come soon after. Not for hard sessions. Avoid in heat. |
| **After iftar (evening)** | Quality sessions, strength | Leave time to eat and digest first (Nutrition advises timing and content). Watch sleep. |
| **Before suhoor** | Clients who wake early anyway | Only if it doesn't cut into sleep. |
| **Rest or walk** | Tough days, hot days, poor sleep | Always a valid choice. |

**Stop rules:** dizziness, feeling faint, unusual breathlessness, or feeling unwell → stop. Follow Coaching Standards stop-and-seek-help guidance. Don't push through.

**After the fasting period:** one or two easier weeks, then pick the plan back up one step back.

The same framework, adjusted, applies to other religious or personal fasting periods and dietary restrictions — always with the client's lead and Nutrition's input.
