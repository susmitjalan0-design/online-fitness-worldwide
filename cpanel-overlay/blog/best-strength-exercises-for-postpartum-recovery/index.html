Best strength exercises for postpartum recovery: 7 moves and a sample week

After a baby, start with your pelvic floor and walking. Add strength slowly: short sessions, light loads, 1 to 3 reps in reserve. No running or jumping for at least the first 3 months.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 16 November 2026

A baby changes your body. Then you lift, bend, feed and carry all day, on broken sleep. The NHS lists back pain, a leaky bladder and separated tummy muscles among the physical problems that can follow pregnancy and birth.[2] Gentle strength work helps with the lifting. It starts smaller than you think.

WHO's advice for women after birth, with no medical reason not to: be active regularly, work up to at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, and include muscle-strengthening work. Start with small amounts and build up slowly. Gentle stretching may help too.[1]

When to start depends on you. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) says exercise can be resumed gradually as soon as it's medically safe, depending on how you gave birth and any complications. Pelvic floor exercises can start straight after the birth.[3] Your postnatal check, around 6 to 8 weeks after the birth, is a good time to talk to your doctor about how you're recovering.[2]

Pelvic floor first. NICE, the UK guideline body, says to encourage women who have recently given birth to do pelvic floor muscle training, because it helps prevent problems such as leaking. If you leak when you cough, sneeze or lift, it recommends at least 3 months of training supervised by a physio or another trained professional.[4]

Honest note: a Cochrane review of 46 trials found the clearest benefit for pelvic floor training started early in pregnancy. For training started after birth, the effect on leaking later on was uncertain.[5] It's low-risk, and the guideline still recommends it. So we do it, and we don't oversell it.

The tummy gap. It's common for the 2 long tummy muscles to separate in pregnancy. This is called diastasis recti. The NHS says it usually goes back to normal by the time your baby is 8 weeks old, and to see a GP if the gap is still obvious after that.[2] In a Norwegian study of 300 first-time mothers, a gap of 2 finger widths or more was found in 60% at 6 weeks after birth, 45% at 6 months and 33% at 12 months. Women with and without a gap reported the same amount of low back and pelvic pain at 12 months.[6]

Can exercise close it? The evidence is mixed. ACOG cites studies where tummy exercises reduced the gap.[3] In a randomised trial of 175 first-time mothers, a weekly class of pelvic floor and tummy strength work, plus daily pelvic floor training at home, from 6 weeks after birth for 4 months, did not change how many had a gap at 6 or 12 months.[7] So we train the tummy for everyday strength, not to chase a number.

This is coaching, not medical care. Had a caesarean, a bad tear, heavy bleeding, high blood pressure in pregnancy or any other complication? Check with your doctor or midwife before you start. Read "Before you start".

The 7 best strength exercises for postpartum recovery

  1. 1Pelvic floor squeeze
  2. 2Side-lying tummy draw-in
  3. 3Glute bridge
  4. 4Goblet squat to a chair
  5. 5One-arm row
  6. 6Incline push-up
  7. 7Dumbbell Romanian deadlift

  1. 1Pelvic floor squeeze

    Why
    The muscles around your bladder, vagina and back passage. They carried 9 months of load. Train them first, every day.
    How
    Sit or lie comfortably. Squeeze and lift as if stopping wind and the flow of urine at the same time. Keep breathing; don't squeeze your buttocks or hold your breath. Let go fully between squeezes. Add some quick squeezes too.
    Dose
    Build up to 10 long squeezes, each held for up to 10 seconds, and 10 quick ones, at least 3 times a day. Breathe normally. Don't pull your tummy in hard.

  2. 2Side-lying tummy draw-in

    Why
    Your deep tummy muscles, the ones that wrap round like a corset. A gentle way back in.
    How
    Lie on your side, knees slightly bent. Let your tummy relax and breathe in gently. As you breathe out, gently draw in the lower part of your tummy, like a corset narrowing your waist, and squeeze your pelvic floor at the same time. Hold for a count of 10, breathing normally, then let go. Too much? A shorter hold. Easy? Do it sitting, then standing, then as you pick up your baby.
    Dose
    Up to 10 holds of 10 seconds, once or twice a day. Keep breathing.

  3. 3Glute bridge

    Why
    Buttocks and the back of the legs, lying down. Kind to a tired back and a healing tummy.
    How
    Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Squeeze your buttocks and lift your hips until your body makes a line from shoulders to knees. Lower slowly. Too much? A smaller lift. Easy? A 2-second hold at the top, a weight on your hips, or one leg at a time.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Breathe out as you lift. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4Goblet squat to a chair

    Why
    Down to the floor and back up, often holding a baby. Thighs and buttocks do the work, not your back.
    How
    Hold a dumbbell, a water bottle or a filled backpack at your chest. Feet a little wider than your hips. Sit back and down until you touch the chair, then stand. Too much? No weight, a higher chair. Easy? Pause just above the seat.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5One-arm row

    Why
    Upper back and arms. Feeding and carrying pull your shoulders forward; this pulls them back.
    How
    One hand and knee on a bench or sturdy chair, back flat. Pull a dumbbell or backpack up towards your hip, elbow close. Lower slowly. Too much? A lighter load, or a band. Easy? Heavier, with a two-second pause at the top.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Incline push-up

    Why
    Chest, shoulders and arms, with your tummy holding a straight line. The higher your hands, the easier it is on both.
    How
    Hands on a counter or a sturdy table, body in one line from head to heels. Lower your chest to the edge, then push away. Too much? A higher surface, or the wall. Easy? A lower surface, then the floor.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 6 to 12. See your tummy dome or bulge down the middle? Raise your hands higher. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  7. 7Dumbbell Romanian deadlift

    Why
    Lifting the car seat, the baby bath, the washing basket. This is that hinge, trained light.
    How
    Hold two dumbbells or bags in front of your thighs, knees soft. Push your hips back and slide the weights down your thighs, back long, until you feel a stretch behind your legs. Stand up by pushing your hips forward. Too much? Lighter, or a shorter range, stopping at the knees.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 10, light. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

When to start, how much, how hard

WHO, for women after birth: at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, with muscle-strengthening work, built up gradually. Brisk walking with the pram counts. Daily pelvic floor training may lower the risk of leaking.[1]

Early weeks: pelvic floor squeezes, the tummy draw-in and short walks. One physio guideline for returning to running suggests squats, lunges and bridges from around weeks 2 to 4, in line with daily life, and light deadlifts from weeks 6 to 8. It suggests starting at no more than the weight of your baby in the car seat, and building up gradually.[8] That guideline is based on the evidence available and the authors' clinical experience. It is not a rule for every body.

Strength 2 or 3 days a week, 15 to 25 minutes. Nap time counts. Missed a day? Do the next one. Broken sleep is real: on a bad night, do half.

How hard: sets should feel steady, not heroic. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal. Breathe out on the effort; don't hold your breath.

Traffic-light pain check, 0 to 10, during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or worse the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. Leaking, heaviness or dragging in the vagina, or tummy doming: count it as amber and make the move easier. A coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

Progression: when the top of the rep range feels easy with clean reps for a week, add a little load, about 2 to 10%, or a harder version.[9]

Impact: no running, jumping or skipping for at least the first 3 months. The same guideline suggests a return to running between 3 and 6 months after birth at the earliest, after a check that you can walk, balance, squat on one leg, hop and jog on the spot without pain, heaviness, dragging or leaking.[8] The sample week below has no impact at all.

Breastfeeding? ACOG says regular aerobic exercise does not affect milk supply, milk composition or your baby's growth. Feed or express before you train, and drink enough water.[3]

A sample week of postpartum strength training

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it with your doctor to your body, your medicines and your history.

  • For a woman at least 6 to 8 weeks after an uncomplicated birth, with her doctor's OK, no leaking or heaviness, and no strength training yet. Pelvic floor squeezes every day, 3 times a day.
  • Monday, about 20 minutes: tummy draw-in, glute bridge, squat to a chair, one-arm row. 2 sets each. A 20-minute pram walk later.
  • Tuesday: a 30-minute brisk walk. You can talk, but not sing.
  • Wednesday, about 20 minutes: tummy draw-in, incline push-up, light Romanian deadlift, glute bridge. 2 sets each.
  • Thursday: a 30-minute walk, or rest if the night was bad.
  • Friday, about 20 minutes: repeat Monday. Feeling good for 2 weeks? Make it 3 sets.
  • Saturday: a longer walk, 40 to 50 minutes, or a swim once your bleeding has stopped and any wound has healed.
  • Sunday: rest, or an easy walk.
  • Load cap for this sample week: your own bodyweight, then light dumbbells you can move for 10 smooth reps. No sit-ups, no front planks yet, no maximal lifts, no training to failure, no breath-holding, no running, no jumping. Very tired day? Pelvic floor and a walk. It still counts.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[10] Strength training does not replace medical care. The NHS says to ask a GP or health visitor for help at any time if a physical problem is bothering you, and to contact a GP if the tummy gap is still obvious 8 weeks after the birth. With a tummy gap, it says to avoid sit-ups, planks and high-impact exercise to start with, and to avoid straining on the toilet and heavy lifting.[2] Leaking, or heaviness or a bulge in the vagina? NICE recommends supervised pelvic floor training with a physio; ask your doctor for a referral.[4] After a caesarean, your wound and your doctor set the pace. Low mood, anxiety or thoughts that scare you: tell your doctor or health visitor now. Stop and get help for heavy bleeding, chest pain, a painful swollen leg, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.
