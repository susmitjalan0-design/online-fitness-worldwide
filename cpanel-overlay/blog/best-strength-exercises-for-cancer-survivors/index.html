Best strength exercises for cancer survivors: 7 moves and a sample week

During and after cancer treatment, strength work is generally safe and helps you stay strong. Start low, go slow, keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Your cancer team sets the limits.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 17 November 2026

Cancer and its treatment take a lot out of you. Surgery, chemo, radiotherapy, hormone therapy, weeks on the sofa. Muscles shrink. Stairs get harder. Tiredness hangs on. Strength work doesn't treat the cancer. It helps you keep doing the things your day needs.

The old advice was to rest. Not any more. An international roundtable convened by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) concluded that exercise is generally safe for cancer survivors, during and after treatment, and that every survivor should "avoid inactivity".[3] Macmillan Cancer Support says it is usually safe to start some physical activity before, during or after treatment.[2]

What it helps. The same roundtable found enough evidence that aerobic training, aerobic plus resistance training, and for some outcomes resistance training alone, can improve fatigue, anxiety, depressive symptoms, physical function and quality of life. For nerve damage from chemo and for thinking problems, the effect is still uncertain.[3]

The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guideline says cancer doctors should recommend regular aerobic and resistance exercise during treatment given with curative intent. In the evidence it reviewed, exercise during treatment improved fitness, strength and fatigue.[4] The Clinical Oncology Society of Australia wants exercise to be part of standard cancer care, with referral to an exercise physiologist or physio who has cancer experience.[5]

Honest note: a Cochrane review of 56 trials, with 4,826 people, looked at exercise during treatment. Exercise may improve quality of life, physical function and fatigue. The authors also said to read the results cautiously: the programmes differed a lot, and many trials were at high risk of bias.[6] So we train because it helps most people feel and function better. Not because it is a cure.

WHO reviewed the evidence for people living with chronic conditions, including cancer. Physical activity is considered safe for them when there is no contraindication, with the same targets as other adults: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, and muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days. In breast and colorectal cancer survivors, being active after diagnosis is linked with a lower risk of dying from cancer or from any cause.[1] That's a link across many people, not a promise for one.

Lifting with lymphoedema? For years women were told not to lift with the swollen arm. In a trial of 141 breast cancer survivors with stable arm lymphoedema, slowly progressive weight lifting twice a week, wearing a well-fitted compression garment, did not increase swelling. Flare-ups were less common (14% vs 29%), and symptoms and strength improved.[7] The roundtable says a start-low, progress-slow programme is safe when supervised; there isn't enough evidence yet on starting one without supervision.[3]

This is coaching, not cancer care. Your cancer team sets the limits. Read "Before you start" first.

The 7 best strength exercises for cancer survivors

  1. 1Sit-to-stand
  2. 2Wall push-up
  3. 3One-arm row
  4. 4Glute bridge
  5. 5Step-up
  6. 6Calf raise
  7. 7Farmer's carry

  1. 1Sit-to-stand

    Why
    Getting out of a chair, off the toilet, out of the car. Time in bed and on the sofa drains the legs first. This brings them back.
    How
    Sit near the front of a sturdy chair, feet flat, hip-width. Arms forward. Lean in and stand up tall. Sit back down slowly, don't drop. Breathe out as you stand. Don't hold your breath. Too easy? Hold a bag of rice at your chest.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. One set on a hard week. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Wall push-up

    Why
    Chest, shoulders and arms, at a load you control. After breast or chest surgery, wait for your surgeon's or physio's OK, and work in the range you have.
    How
    Hands on the wall at shoulder height, a little wider than your shoulders. Step your feet back. Body straight from head to heels. Bend your elbows and bring your chest towards the wall. Push away. Breathe out as you push.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Central line or PICC line? Ask your team first: Macmillan advises avoiding vigorous upper-body exercise with one.[2] Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3One-arm row

    Why
    Upper back. Long days lying down round the shoulders forward. Rowing pulls them back.
    How
    One hand on a table, feet a step apart, back long. Hold a water bottle, a bag or a dumbbell in the other hand. Pull it up towards your hip. Lower slowly. All reps one side, then the other.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 each arm. Lymphoedema in that arm? Wear your compression garment if you have one, start lighter on that side and add load slowly.[2][7] Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4Glute bridge

    Why
    Hips and the backs of the thighs, lying down, with no load on the spine. Good on a low-energy day.
    How
    Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat. Press through your heels and lift your hips until knees, hips and shoulders line up. Squeeze. Lower slowly. Breathe out as you lift. Easy? Do it on one leg, or with a weight on your hips. One leg only after two legs is easy, and only if you can feel your feet well.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Stoma or recent tummy surgery? Ask your surgeon or stoma nurse first, and keep the load low.[2][3] Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Step-up

    Why
    Stairs, kerbs, the bus step. Leg strength and balance in one move.
    How
    Start on the bottom stair. Whole foot on the step. Push through it and stand tall. Lower the other foot slowly. Hold the rail. Go higher only when it stays smooth. Good shoes on, not bare feet. Nerve damage in the feet, or balance worries? Always use the rail and a low step.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 6 to 10 each leg, holding the rail. Numb feet or unsteady? Skip it for now and do sit-to-stands: Macmillan suggests care with steps when nerve damage affects your feet or balance.[2] Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Calf raise

    Why
    Ankles and calves, for walking and balance, with a counter in reach. Useful when treatment has left your feet numb or tingly.
    How
    Stand tall, hands on a counter. Rise up onto the balls of your feet. Pause. Lower slowly. Easy? One foot at a time.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 10 to 15. Both hands on the counter if your balance is off. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  7. 7Farmer's carry

    Why
    Shopping bags, the laundry basket, a suitcase. Grip and posture for real life.
    How
    Hold a heavy bag or water bottle in each hand. Stand tall, shoulders down. Walk slowly across the room and back. Breathe normally. Put them down before your grip fails. Check your feet and shoes afterwards.
    Dose
    2 to 3 walks of 20 to 30 seconds, light. Numb hands? Weightlifting gloves can help you feel the grip.[2] Lymphoedema arm: lighter on that side, build slowly.

How much, how hard, how often

The roundtable's general dose: moderate aerobic exercise at least 3 times a week, at least 30 minutes, for at least 8 to 12 weeks. Adding resistance training at least twice a week, at least 2 sets of 8 to 15 reps at 60% or more of your one-rep max, appears to give similar benefits.[3] You don't need a max test. We use reps in reserve instead.

Build towards at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic exercise and 2 to 3 moderate resistance sessions a week.[5] Build towards. Not week one.

Start low. Week one might be 1 set of each move and 10-minute walks. Some soreness or stiffness for 2 to 3 days is common. Very sore or very tired? Do a bit less next time.[2] During chemo, plan around your cycles: lighter days straight after a dose.

How hard: sets should feel steady, not heroic. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal. Breathe out on the effort; don't hold your breath.

Traffic-light check, 0 to 10, for pain and for tiredness, during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or worse the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. New or unusual bone, back or muscle pain is never amber: stop and tell your doctor.[2] The colours are a coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

Progression: when the top of the rep range feels easy with clean reps for a week, add a little load, about 2 to 10%, or a harder version.[9] With lymphoedema, take smaller steps, more slowly.

Impact: none in this plan. Macmillan says to avoid high-impact activity such as running if you have bone problems, and your doctor may advise gentle exercise with no high-impact activity while your platelets are low.[2] Want to build bone later? The bone-health trials the roundtable describes used about a year of supervised resistance plus impact training.[3] Plan that with your team, not from a blog.

Cancer in the bones? An international expert group says exercise can still be part of care. It should be planned with your health care team and delivered by exercise professionals with cancer training, with the focus on posture, controlled movement and good technique.[8] That needs a specialist plan, not this sample week.

A sample week of strength training after cancer

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it with your cancer team to your body, your treatment and your history.

  • For someone cleared by their cancer team, with no cancer in the bones, no new symptoms and no lymphoedema flare. During chemo, move the strength days away from treatment days.
  • Monday, about 20 minutes: sit-to-stand, wall push-up, one-arm row, calf raise. 1 to 2 sets of each.
  • Tuesday: a 10- to 20-minute walk, at a pace where you can still talk.
  • Wednesday: rest, or a gentle walk.
  • Thursday, about 20 minutes: glute bridge, step-up (or sit-to-stand), one-arm row, farmer's carry. 1 to 2 sets of each.
  • Friday: a 10- to 20-minute walk.
  • Saturday: an easy walk, or rest in a treatment week. Sunday: rest.
  • Load cap for this sample week: your bodyweight, then light weights you can move for 12 smooth reps. No maximal lifts, no training to failure, no breath-holding, no running or jumping, no sit-ups. Bad day? A short walk counts. Feeling well for 2 weeks? Add a set before you add weight.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. Talk to your cancer doctor or specialist nurse before you start: they know your treatment. The ACSM roundtable uses a triage adapted from the US National Comprehensive Cancer Network. No other health problems: no extra medical check is needed. Nerve damage, arthritis or joint problems, weak bones (osteopenia or osteoporosis), or lymphoedema: a medical check is recommended first. Lung or tummy surgery, a stoma, heart or lung disease, poor coordination, extreme tiredness, severe nutrition problems, a condition that is getting worse, or cancer in the bones: get medical evaluation and clearance before you exercise.[3] ACSM's general screening adds: known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, means medical clearance first.[10] Macmillan: don't exercise if you feel unwell. With low white blood cells, your doctor may advise avoiding busy places such as gyms and pools. With low platelets, exercise gently and avoid high-impact activity. With a central or PICC line, no swimming and no vigorous upper-body exercise. Stop, and speak to a doctor straight away, for dizziness, chest pain, a racing heart, breathing problems, feeling sick, unusual back or bone pain, unusual muscle pain, or a headache that won't go away.[2] A temperature or signs of infection during treatment: call your hospital's emergency contact number now, not your coach.
