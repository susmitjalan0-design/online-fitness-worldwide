Fitness during a lockdown: what the research shows

What studies from the COVID-19 lockdowns report on activity, sitting time, mood and live-streamed home exercise, and what WHO states about staying active at home.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 21 November 2026

Studies from the COVID-19 lockdowns mostly report less physical activity and more sitting. In a randomised trial in nine countries, live-streamed home exercise increased self-reported moderate and vigorous activity, with effects that decreased over time. WHO's COVID-19 guidance on staying active at home lists short activity breaks, online exercise classes and bodyweight exercises.[1][5][7]

A systematic review of 66 studies (86,981 participants) found that most studies reported decreases in physical activity and increases in sedentary behaviour during COVID-19 lockdowns, across several populations including children and patients with a variety of medical conditions.[1]

Step-count data from 455,404 users of a smartphone app in 187 countries showed a 5.5% fall in mean daily steps within 10 days of WHO's pandemic declaration on 11 March 2020, and a 27.3% fall (1,432 steps) within 30 days. Italy, which declared a nationwide lockdown on 9 March 2020, showed a 48.7% maximal decrease; Sweden showed 6.9%. The authors describe the data as a non-representative convenience sample.[2] In an international online survey of 1,047 people in April 2020, home confinement had a negative effect on all physical-activity intensity levels, and daily sitting time rose from 5 to 8 hours.[3]

A rapid review of 21 observational studies (42,293 participants) found that people who were physically active regularly, with higher volume and frequency and stable routines, showed fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety during the pandemic. For example, more time in moderate-to-vigorous activity was associated with 12 to 32% lower chances of depressive symptoms and 15 to 34% lower chances of anxiety. These are associations, mostly from cross-sectional studies.[4]

A multicentre randomised trial in 763 healthy adults from nine countries compared 4 weeks of live-streamed multicomponent home exercise with an inactive control during government lockdowns. The live-streamed group did more moderate activity (week 1: 1.65 times more minutes a week) and vigorous activity (1.31 times), with effects decreasing over time. Exercise motivation, sleep quality and anxiety improved slightly; mental well-being improved during the live-streaming weeks but showed an inverted trend once pre-recorded workouts replaced the live sessions.[5] An analysis of 760 participants from the same trial reported that 53.8% dropped out over 8 weeks.[6]

What WHO states on amounts and routine at home

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days, that bouts of any duration count, and that some physical activity is better than none.[8]

WHO's COVID-19 guidance states that regular physical activity can help give the day a routine and be a way of staying in contact with family and friends. It lists planning a daily activity break alone, in an online class, or online with friends or colleagues. For people who are not regularly active, it describes beginning with 5 to 10 minutes and building up to 30 minutes or more over a few weeks. WHO marks the page as no longer updated and kept for reference.[7]

A narrative review of minimal-dose resistance training states that short sessions, or short, frequent bouts with little or no equipment, can improve strength and functional ability in younger and older adults.[9]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

Illness: WHO's COVID-19 guidance states that people with a fever, cough and difficulty breathing do not exercise, and stay home and rest.[7]

Warning symptoms: ACSM's 2015 screening recommendations list signs and symptoms that include pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion, dizziness or fainting, palpitations, and unusual fatigue. They state that physically active people who develop such signs or symptoms discontinue exercise and seek medical clearance before resuming exercise of any intensity.[14]

Carbon monoxide indoors: the CDC states that charcoal is never burned indoors, a portable gas camp stove is never used indoors, a house is never heated with a gas oven, and a generator is never used inside a home or garage, even with doors and windows open. It lists the most common symptoms as headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion.[10]

Space, heat and cold: WHO lists unsafe stairs, lighting or walking surfaces among the risk factors for falls.[11] WHO's heat guidance lists avoiding strenuous activity during the hottest time of day and keeping the home cool with night air and covered windows.[12] The NHS states that homes are heated, if possible, to at least 18°C in rooms used regularly.[13]

Frequently asked questions

How did COVID-19 lockdowns change physical activity?

A systematic review of 66 studies found that most reported less physical activity and more sedentary behaviour during lockdowns.[1] App data from 187 countries showed mean daily steps 27.3% lower within 30 days of the pandemic declaration.[2]

Does live-streamed home exercise make a difference?

In a randomised trial of 763 adults in nine countries, 4 weeks of live-streamed home exercise increased self-reported moderate and vigorous activity compared with an inactive control, with effects that decreased over time.[5] In the same trial, 53.8% of participants dropped out over 8 weeks.[6]

What did WHO list for staying active at home during COVID-19?

WHO listed short 3 to 5 minute breaks from sitting every 20 to 30 minutes, walking around the home and on stairs, online exercise classes, dancing, household chores, and muscle strengthening with body weight (press-ups, sit-ups, squats) or full water bottles.[7]

Is physical activity linked to mood during lockdowns?

A rapid review of 21 observational studies found that more moderate-to-vigorous activity was associated with 12 to 32% lower chances of depressive symptoms and 15 to 34% lower chances of anxiety during the pandemic. These are associations, not proof of cause.[4]
