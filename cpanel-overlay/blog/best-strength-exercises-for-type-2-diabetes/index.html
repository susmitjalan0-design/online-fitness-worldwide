Strength training and type 2 diabetes: what the research shows

What the ADA, WHO, the NHS and trials found on resistance training in type 2 diabetes: HbA1c, combined training, sitting breaks and hypoglycaemia.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

The ICMR-INDIAB survey of 113,043 people in India found a weighted prevalence of 11.4% for diabetes and 15.3% for prediabetes.[7]

A meta-analysis of 47 randomised trials (8,538 patients) found structured resistance training associated with an HbA1c decline of 0.57 percentage points versus control, aerobic training with 0.73 and both combined with 0.51. Structured exercise of more than 150 minutes a week was associated with a reduction of 0.89, and 150 minutes or less with 0.36. Physical activity advice alone was not associated with HbA1c changes.[3]

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) 2016 position statement lists resistance training benefits in type 2 diabetes as improvements in glycaemic control, insulin resistance, fat mass, blood pressure, strength and lean body mass. It states that combined training is superior to either type alone for glycaemic control, and that adults with type 2 diabetes should ideally do both aerobic and resistance training.[2]

In one trial, combined training changed HbA1c by a further −0.46 percentage points compared with aerobic training alone and −0.59 compared with resistance training alone.[4] In another, only the combined group improved HbA1c versus control (−0.34%); the changes with resistance training alone (−0.16%) and aerobic training alone were not statistically significant.[5]

In a randomised crossover trial, 24 inactive adults with type 2 diabetes sat for 8 hours on three days: uninterrupted, or with 3-minute bouts of light walking or of simple resistance activities (half-squats, calf raises, gluteal contractions and knee raises) every 30 minutes. Both break conditions reduced the glucose area under the curve (from 24.2 to 14.8 and 14.7 mmol·h/L).[6]

Doses and protocols the studies used

The ADA states that adults with diabetes should do 2 to 3 sessions a week of resistance exercise on non-consecutive days, and that most adults with diabetes should do 150 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous activity weekly over at least 3 days, with no more than 2 consecutive days without activity.[2]

The ADA table lists at least 8 to 10 exercises with 1 to 3 sets of 10 to 15 repetitions to near fatigue early in training, intensity from moderate (about 15 repetitions) to vigorous (6 to 8 repetitions), on a minimum of 2 non-consecutive days a week, preferably 3.[2]

The ADA states that prolonged sitting should be interrupted with bouts of light activity every 30 minutes for blood glucose benefits, at least in adults with type 2 diabetes, and that the longer-term efficacy of this remains to be determined.[2]

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous) a week, and muscle-strengthening activities involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.[1]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The ADA states that pre-exercise medical clearance is generally unnecessary for asymptomatic individuals before low- or moderate-intensity activity not exceeding brisk walking or everyday living. It notes that ACSM recommends prior medical clearance for anyone with diabetes who is sedentary and wants to begin activity at any intensity, which the ADA calls excessively conservative.[2][10]

The ADA states that exercise-induced hypoglycaemia occurs, to a lesser extent than in type 1 diabetes, in people with type 2 diabetes using insulin or insulin secretagogues.[2] The NHS lists symptoms of low blood sugar including feeling hungry, dizzy, anxious or irritable, sweating, shaking, heart palpitations and blurred vision.[8]
