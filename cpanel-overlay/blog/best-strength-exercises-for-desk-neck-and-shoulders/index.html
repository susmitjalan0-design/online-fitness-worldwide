Best strength exercises for desk neck and shoulders: 7 moves and a sample week

Long desk days load the neck and shoulders. Light, progressive strength beats endless stretching alone. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Pain is a traffic light.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 7 November 2026

Your neck aches by mid-afternoon. Shoulders creep up toward your ears. You stretch, then sit again. Stretching helps some people feel looser. It is not the whole plan.

WHO advises adults to do muscle-strengthening work for all the major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week, and to limit long stretches of sitting.[1]

A Cochrane review of exercise for mechanical neck pain found moderate-quality evidence that strength work for the neck, shoulder blade and upper arm region can cut pain.[6] A workplace meta-analysis in office workers with neck pain found medium effects when strengthening targeted the neck and shoulder, and bigger effects when people actually showed up.[7]

In a one-year trial of office workers, specific dumbbell work for the shoulder girdle (front raise, lateral raise, shrug and related moves), about 20 minutes, three times a week, cut neck pain in people who already had it, and helped prevent new shoulder pain compared with advice alone.[4] In women with trapezius myalgia, similar specific strength work three times a week for 10 weeks cut pain far more than general fitness cycling with relaxed shoulders.[5]

Scapular-focused work matters too. A 10-week trial of supervised lower-trapezius and serratus training (press-up and push-up plus patterns) cut neck/shoulder pain versus usual activity in office workers with chronic non-specific pain.[8]

This list is coaching, not treatment. Sharp arm pain, numbness, weakness after a fall, or a hot swollen joint? Read "Before you start" first.

The 7 best strength exercises for desk neck and shoulders

  1. 1Chin tuck
  2. 2Scapular squeeze (band W)
  3. 3One-arm row
  4. 4Reverse fly
  5. 5Front raise to about 90°
  6. 6Lateral raise to about 90°
  7. 7Wall push-up plus

  1. 1Chin tuck

    Why
    Long screen time often parks the head forward. A gentle chin tuck trains the deep neck flexors that help hold a taller head position. It is small. It is not a hard yank.
    How
    Sit or stand tall. Soften the jaw. Glide the chin straight back as if making a double chin, eyes level. Hold 5 seconds. Release. No looking up or down. Too much? Do fewer holds, or less travel.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 5 to 8 holds of about 5 seconds. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Scapular squeeze (band W)

    Why
    The mid-back and lower trapezius help set the shoulder blades. Office trials that trained scapular muscles without hammering the upper trapezius cut neck/shoulder pain.[8]
    How
    Hold a light band in both hands, elbows bent, palms up, as if making a W against your ribs. Draw the shoulder blades down and together. Pause. Return. Keep the neck soft. No band? Squeeze the blades without arms first.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3One-arm row

    Why
    Rows load the mid-back and the muscles that pull the shoulder blade toward the spine. They showed up in the specific strength plans that cut trapezius pain.[5]
    How
    Hinge at the hips, one hand on a chair or bench. Pull a dumbbell or bag toward your hip. Elbow close. Shoulder blade slides in. Lower with control. Swap sides. Too much? Use a lighter bag or a seated band row.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4Reverse fly

    Why
    A reverse fly targets the rear shoulder and mid-back. It sat in the progressive dumbbell menus used in office-worker neck trials.[4][5]
    How
    Hinge with a soft knee bend, light dumbbells or a band in both hands, palms facing in. Raise the arms out to the sides to about shoulder height, thumbs slightly up. Squeeze the blades. Lower slow. Keep the neck long; do not shrug up.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Front raise to about 90°

    Why
    Front raises to roughly shoulder height were part of the specific resistance menus that reduced neck pain in office workers over a year.[4] Cap the height. You do not need an overhead slam.
    How
    Stand tall, light dumbbells or water bottles at your thighs, palms down or thumbs up. Raise one or both arms forward to about shoulder height. Lower in 2 to 3 seconds. Soft neck. Too much? Raise one arm at a time, or shorten the arc.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Lateral raise to about 90°

    Why
    Side raises to about 90° were also in those desk-worker menus, including a thumb-down variant for the supraspinatus in the original protocol.[4] We keep thumbs neutral-to-up and stop near shoulder height.
    How
    Stand tall, light weights at your sides. Raise the arms out to the sides until about level with the shoulders. Soft elbows. Lower slow. Stop short of pain. Regression: smaller arc, or raise one arm at a time.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  7. 7Wall push-up plus

    Why
    A push-up plus finishes with a small reach that loads the serratus anterior — a scapular muscle highlighted in scapular-function trials for neck/shoulder pain.[8]
    How
    Hands on a wall at chest height, body in a straight line. Bend the elbows for a small push-up, then push the wall away until the shoulder blades wrap forward around the ribs. Return. Floor version only if the wall feels easy and calm.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

How much, how hard, how often

WHO: muscle-strengthening for major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week, and less unbroken sitting.[1]

The office trials that moved pain often used about 20 minutes, two to three times a week, with light-to-moderate dumbbells progressed when 12 to 15 good reps felt easy.[4][5] Scapular-function work ran three supervised 20-minute sessions a week for 10 weeks.[8]

A workplace review found medium pain reductions from neck/shoulder strengthening in symptomatic office workers, with larger effects when attendance was higher.[7] Showing up beats a perfect program you skip.

Cochrane's mechanical-neck review: strengthening for the cervico-scapulothoracic and upper-extremity region can help chronic neck pain; stretching alone looked weaker.[6]

Progression: when you beat 8 to 12 good reps by 1 or 2, add a little load — about 2 to 10% — or slow the lowering.[9] Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal.

This is training, not a diagnosis. Neck or shoulder pain that is getting worse, or that does not settle, needs a clinician — not a heavier dumbbell.

A sample week for desk neck and shoulder strength

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it with your doctor or physio to your body and your history.

  • Monday, about 20 to 25 minutes: chin tuck, scapular squeeze, one-arm row, reverse fly. 1 to 3 sets each.
  • Tuesday: desk breaks — stand or walk 2 to 3 minutes each half hour if you can. No extra lifting required.
  • Wednesday, about 20 to 25 minutes: chin tuck, front raise, lateral raise, wall push-up plus. 1 to 3 sets each.
  • Thursday: easy walk or rest. Soft neck rolls only if they feel calm.
  • Friday: repeat Monday's session.
  • Saturday: optional light scapular squeezes and chin tucks, or a walk.
  • Sunday: rest, or an easy walk.
  • Load cap for this sample week: light dumbbells or a band you can control for 8 to 12 smooth reps. No overhead presses, no kipping, no max shrugs. Raises stop near shoulder height.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[10] NHS neck advice: see a GP if neck pain or stiffness does not go away after a few weeks, painkillers have not helped, you are worried, or you have pins and needles or a cold arm.[2] NHS shoulder advice: see a GP if shoulder pain is getting worse or has not improved after 2 weeks, or it is very difficult to move the arm; get urgent help for sudden severe pain, a changed shape, ongoing pins and needles, loss of feeling, a hot or cold limb, pain after a fall, severe pain in both shoulders, or if you feel feverish or unwell.[3] Outside the UK, that means your doctor or local urgent care. Recent neck or shoulder surgery, known disc issues with arm weakness, or unexplained weight loss with night pain? Get cleared first. Train on a floor that does not slip. Stop for sharp pain, chest pain or dizziness.
