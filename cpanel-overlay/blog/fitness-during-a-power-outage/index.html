Fitness during a short or planned power outage: what the research and official guidance show

What activity guidelines, short-bout and no-equipment training studies, and official outage guidance report for a few hours without electricity, such as scheduled load-shedding.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 21 November 2026

None of the cited sources studies exercise during a power outage. What they do report: WHO's 2020 guidelines count moderate-to-vigorous activity in bouts of any length towards the weekly recommendations; trials have tested training with no equipment or very little; and official outage guidance from the CDC and Ready.gov covers lighting, carbon monoxide, food storage, heat and cold.[1][6][7]

WHO's 2020 guidelines, published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, state that adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days. Bouts of any duration now count towards these amounts, which differs from the minimum of 10-minute bouts in the 2010 guidelines, and the guidelines restate that some physical activity is better than none.[1]

A narrative review of minimal-dose resistance training states that short sessions, or short, frequent bouts with little or no equipment (resistance "exercise snacking"), can improve strength and functional ability in younger and older adults.[2]

In an 8-week trial in 18 young men (9 per group, twice a week), push-ups adjusted to a load similar to 40% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) bench press, and bench press at 40% 1RM, produced comparable gains in bench-press 1RM and in triceps and chest muscle thickness.[3] A meta-analysis of 21 studies, with all sets taken to momentary muscular failure, found greater 1RM strength gains with high loads (over 60% of 1RM) than low loads, and similar changes in muscle size.[5]

In sedentary young adults (12 per group), vigorously climbing a 60-step stairwell 3 times a day, 1 to 4 hours apart, 3 days a week for 6 weeks raised peak oxygen uptake compared with a non-training group. The authors describe the absolute increase as modest.[4]

What official outage guidance states

Ready.gov, the US government's preparedness site, states that a power outage may disrupt communications, water and transport, close shops and services, cause food spoilage and water contamination, and prevent the use of medical devices. Its preparation steps include flashlights for every household member and batteries or other power sources such as a power bank.[6]

Ready.gov states that a closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours and a full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours.[6] The CDC gives 24 hours for a half-full freezer that stays closed, and lists perishable refrigerated food to be thrown out once the power has been off for 4 hours or more.[7]

Ready.gov lists alternate plans for refrigerated medicines and power-dependent medical devices, and checking with local officials about heating and cooling locations.[6]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

Carbon monoxide: the CDC states that a generator, pressure washer or other petrol-powered engine should never be used inside a home, basement or garage, or less than 20 feet (about 6 metres) from any window, door or vent, and that a charcoal grill, hibachi, lantern or portable camping stove should never be used inside a home, tent or camper. It lists the most common symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning as headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain and confusion.[7] Ready.gov states that a gas stove or oven is not to be used to heat a home, because it increases the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning.[6]

Low light and falls: the CDC states that battery-powered flashlights and lanterns are used rather than candles or gas lanterns, to minimise the risk of fire.[7] WHO lists unsafe stairs, lighting or walking surfaces among the risk factors for falls, which it estimates caused about 842,000 deaths worldwide in 2023.[8]

Heat and cold: the CDC states that electric fans may give some comfort but will not prevent heat-related illness when it is really hot, that in heat stroke body temperature can rise to 106°F (about 41°C) or higher within 10 to 15 minutes, and that hypothermia, a body temperature below 95°F (35°C), is a medical emergency.[7]

Warning symptoms: ACSM's 2015 screening recommendations list signs and symptoms that include pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion, dizziness or fainting, palpitations, and unusual fatigue. They state that physically active people who develop such signs or symptoms discontinue exercise and seek medical clearance before resuming exercise of any intensity.[9]

Frequently asked questions

Do short bouts of exercise count towards activity guidelines?

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that moderate-to-vigorous activity in bouts of any duration counts towards the weekly 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity, and that some physical activity is better than none.[1]

What has been studied for training without gym equipment?

In an 8-week trial in 18 young men, push-ups and bench press at a similar load produced comparable gains in strength and in chest and triceps thickness.[3] In sedentary young adults, 3 short stair climbs a day, 3 days a week for 6 weeks, raised peak oxygen uptake compared with no training, by an amount the authors call modest.[4]

How long does food stay cold in a fridge during a power cut?

Ready.gov gives about four hours for a closed refrigerator and about 48 hours for a full freezer.[6] The CDC gives 24 hours for a half-full freezer that stays closed.[7]

Can a generator be run indoors during an outage?

The CDC states that a generator should never be used inside a home, basement or garage, or less than 20 feet from any window, door or vent, because carbon monoxide can build up and poison the people and animals inside.[7]

How is this page different from the blackout page?

This page covers short or scheduled outages of a few hours. The blackout page covers longer or unplanned outages: low light at home, carbon monoxide from alternative heating and cooking, heat, cold and fluids.
