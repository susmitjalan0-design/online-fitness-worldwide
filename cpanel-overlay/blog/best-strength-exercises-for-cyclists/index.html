Best strength exercises for cyclists: 7 moves and a sample week

Solid leg work, a strong trunk and a little impact. Two short sessions a week around your rides. 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Never to failure.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 19 November 2026

Riders ask me two things. Will lifting make my legs heavy? Will it cost me watts? The research says: done right, it tends to help.

A review of runners and cyclists says heavy strength training is the one recommended for improving cycling economy, and the evidence of an extra gain in cycling performance is most convincing when the strength work is heavy.[2] A systematic review of 26 studies in trained endurance athletes found strength training improved time-trial performance, economy and muscle power.[3]

Two trials. Well-trained cyclists added four lower-body exercises, twice a week for 12 weeks. Their mean power in a 5-minute all-out effort, done after 185 minutes of steady riding, went from 371 to 400 watts. The riding-only group didn't change.[5] In 19 well-trained female cyclists, 11 weeks of added heavy strength training improved mean power in a 40-minute all-out trial, and cycling economy. Again, no change in the riding-only group.[6] Small groups of trained riders. Not a promise for you.

Not every study agrees. A review of 5 randomised trials in highly trained road cyclists found 2 with no gain in performance. Both added lifting on top of the usual riding. The 3 that improved replaced part of the riding with lifting.[4] So it's a swap, not an extra. And a review of the field says a maintenance dose in race season is essential to keep the strength.[7]

The body. Of 109 professional road cyclists, 58% had lower back pain in the previous 12 months and 36% had pain at the front of the knee. Knee injuries were the most likely to cost time off the bike.[8] A review of 31 studies found that adult road cyclists in regular training tend to have low bone density in key areas, such as the lower spine. Road cycling did not appear to help bone. Long hours, seated, weight supported.[9]

Injuries in sport in general: a meta-analysis of 6 randomised trials, 7,738 athletes aged 12 to 40 (not cyclists), found strength training programmes cut the risk of sports injuries to about a third (relative risk 0.338). More strength training went with a lower risk.[10] Different sports. Same direction.

The baseline for every adult: WHO asks for 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous, plus muscle-strengthening work for all the major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.[1] Most riders tick the first box. Many skip the second.

This is coaching, not medical care. Read "Before you start" first.

The 7 best strength exercises for cyclists

  1. 1Goblet squat, then back squat
  2. 2Romanian deadlift
  3. 3Rear-foot-elevated split squat
  4. 4Step-up
  5. 5One-arm row
  6. 6Side plank
  7. 7Calf raises, then small hops

  1. 1Goblet squat, then back squat

    Why
    Quads and glutes push the pedal down. The trials tested strength with half squats and leg presses.[5][6] The first lift to own.
    How
    Feet about shoulder width. Dumbbell or kettlebell held at your chest. Sit down between your heels, chest up, knees following your toes. Stand up with intent. 10 clean, easy reps? Add load. A barbell comes later, with coaching.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 5 to 8. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Romanian deadlift

    Why
    Glutes, hamstrings, and a back that holds its shape. Lower back pain was the most common overuse complaint in professional riders.[8]
    How
    Dumbbells in front of your thighs. Knees soft. Push your hips back, dumbbells close to your legs, back long. Stop when you feel the backs of your thighs. Stand up by squeezing your glutes.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 6 to 8. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3Rear-foot-elevated split squat

    Why
    Pedalling is one leg at a time. This shows you your weaker side, and loads it on its own.
    How
    Back foot on a bench or sofa behind you, laces down. Front foot far enough forward that the front heel stays down. Lower the back knee towards the floor. Drive up through the front foot. Hold a wall at first. Too much? Back foot on the floor: a plain split squat.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 6 to 10 each leg. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4Step-up

    Why
    Single-leg drive, easy on touchy knees, easy to load. Knee pain is common on the bike.[8]
    How
    A sturdy step or box, knee height or lower. Whole foot on the step. Push through that foot and stand tall. Don't bounce off the back foot. Lower slowly. Knee grumbling? Use a lower step.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 10 each leg. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5One-arm row

    Why
    Hours bent over the bars. The upper back holds you there, and pulls on the bars when you climb or sprint.
    How
    One hand and one knee on a bench, back flat. Dumbbell in the other hand. Pull it to your hip. Lower slowly.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Side plank

    Why
    The trunk is the base the legs push from. Side planks train it without bending the spine, which already spends hours bent on the bike.
    How
    Lie on your side, elbow under your shoulder, knees bent. Lift your hips until you are straight from head to knees. Breathe. Easy? Straighten your legs.
    Dose
    2 to 3 holds of 20 to 30 seconds each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  7. 7Calf raises, then small hops

    Why
    Riding is weight supported, and road cycling did not appear to help bone.[9] Loaded calf work and a small dose of impact give the bones, the calves and the Achilles something to adapt to.
    How
    Calf raises first: balls of the feet on a step, hold a rail. Rise up, then lower slowly below the step. Then small bounces on the spot, landings quiet, shoes on, on a non-slip floor.
    Dose
    Calf raises: 2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15. Hops: 2 to 3 sets of 10, rest fully between sets. No jumping down from a height. Sore heel, Achilles or shin, or told you have low bone density? Leave the hops out and keep the calf raises until a doctor or physio says otherwise.

How much, how hard, how often

How often: 2 sessions a week in the off-season or base phase. The 12-week trial lifted twice a week.[5] In race season, 1 shorter session a week to keep what you built.[7]

How much: 3 or 4 lower-body exercises, 2 to 3 sets each. The 12-week trial used 3 sets of 4 to 10 repetition maximum: the most you can lift that many times.[5] You don't start there. Start lighter, own the technique, add load in small steps over the weeks.

How hard: keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal. Heavy means few, clean reps. Not grinding.

Rest: 2 to 3 minutes between the heavy leg sets. Less for the trunk and the calves.

Swap, don't stack. In the review of highly trained riders, the trials that worked replaced some riding with lifting.[4] On lifting weeks, trim an easy ride. Keep your key sessions.

Timing: lift on a hard day, after the ride, or keep a full day between heavy legs and your hardest ride. Heavy legs in the first week or two are common. Still heavy after that? Cut a set. A coaching rule, not a research finding.

Traffic-light check, 0 to 10, for pain during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or worse the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. Pain at the front of the knee that builds on the bike, back pain that spreads down a leg, or any pain that changes how you move, is never amber: stop and get it checked. The colours are a coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

A sample week of strength training for a cyclist

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it to your riding, your season, your health and your injury history.

  • For a healthy adult rider with no current injury and no medical condition that needs a doctor's input. Base season, about 6 to 8 hours of riding a week.
  • Monday: rest or an easy spin.
  • Tuesday: intervals in the morning. Later, about 40 minutes: warm-up, goblet squat, Romanian deadlift, one-arm row, side plank. 2 to 3 sets of each.
  • Wednesday: easy ride.
  • Thursday, about 40 minutes: warm-up, calf raises and small hops, split squat, step-up, one-arm row, side plank. 2 sets of each.
  • Friday: rest or an easy spin.
  • Saturday: long ride. Sunday: easy ride or rest.
  • Load cap for this sample week: weights you could lift for 1 to 3 more clean reps at the end of every set. No max lifts or max tests, no training to failure, no jumping down from a height. Hops capped at 3 sets of 10. Big race or hard block coming? Drop to 1 session: squat, hinge and side plank only.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. A heart condition, high blood pressure, diabetes or another health condition, a recent crash, fracture or concussion, or a diagnosis of low bone density? Talk to your doctor or physio first, and train inside the limits they set. Back after a crash or a long break? Start with the easiest version of every exercise. The trial results above come from small groups of well-trained riders.[5][6] They show what can happen, not what will happen to you. No heavy lifting alone without coaching, no maxing out, collars on any bar. Chest pain, fainting, a racing heart, or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort: stop and get medical help now.
