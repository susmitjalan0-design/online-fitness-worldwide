Strength training for cyclists: what the research shows

What trials and reviews found on strength training, cycling performance and economy, plus injury and bone data in road cyclists.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

A review of strength training for running and cycling states that heavy strength training is recommended for improving cycling economy, and that the most compelling evidence of an additive effect on cycling performance is with heavy strength training.[2] A systematic review of 26 studies in competitive endurance athletes found that strength training improved time-trial performance, economy and muscle power.[3]

In well-trained cyclists, adding four lower-body exercises (3 × 4 to 10 repetition maximum), twice a week for 12 weeks, increased mean power in a 5-minute all-out trial after 185 minutes of cycling from 371 to 400 W; no change occurred in the endurance-only group.[5] In 19 well-trained female cyclists, 11 weeks of added heavy strength training improved mean power in a 40-minute all-out trial, fractional utilisation of VO2max and cycling economy.[6]

A systematic review of 5 randomised trials in highly trained road cyclists found that 2 studies showed no performance improvement; both added resistance training on top of existing endurance training. The 3 studies with improved performance replaced part of endurance training with resistance training.[4] A review of the field states that strength-maintenance training is essential to retain strength gains during the competition season.[7]

Of 109 professional road cyclists, 58% had experienced lower back pain and 36% anterior knee pain in the previous 12 months; 57% of time-loss injuries were in the knee.[8] A review of 31 studies found that adult road cyclists in regular training have low bone mineral density in key regions such as the lumbar spine.[9]

A meta-analysis of 6 trials (7,738 participants aged 12 to 40, not cyclist-specific) found strength training programmes reduced sports injuries (relative risk 0.338).[10]

Doses and protocols the studies used

In the 12-week trial, the strength group performed four lower-body exercises at 3 × 4 to 10 repetition maximum, twice a week.[5]

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous) a week, and muscle-strengthening activities involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.[1]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The trials studied small groups of well-trained riders.[5][6] The injury meta-analysis did not study cyclists.[10]

This page reports what the cited sources found and state. It gives no advice and recommends no course of action. It is not medical advice.
