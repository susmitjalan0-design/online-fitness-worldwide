Achilles and patellar tendon pain (tendinopathy): what the research shows

What a pathology model, a clinical practice guideline, randomised trials and network meta-analyses report on how common tendon pain is and on exercise-based treatment.

By Susmit Jalan, strength and conditioning coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 21 November 2026

In 2009, Cook and Purdam proposed a continuum model of load-induced tendon pathology with three stages: reactive tendinopathy, tendon dysrepair (failed healing) and degenerative tendinopathy. The authors describe the stages as continuous, state that adding or removing load is the primary stimulus that moves a tendon forward or back along the continuum, especially in the early stages, and state that reversibility of pathology is unlikely in the degenerative stage. They presented the model for evaluation by clinicians and researchers.[1]

The same paper describes reactive tendinopathy as a non-inflammatory response to acute overload, usually a burst of unaccustomed physical activity, seen more often in younger people, and describes the classic degenerative presentation as a middle-aged recreational athlete with focal Achilles tendon swelling and pain.[1] A 2016 follow-up by the same group notes that classifying tendinopathy by structure, in what is primarily a pain condition, has been challenged, and that the interplay between structure, pain and function is not yet fully understood.[2]

The 2024 clinical practice guideline of the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy (American Physical Therapy Association) reports an overall prevalence of midportion Achilles tendinopathy of 4% to 7% in a recent meta-analysis, higher with increasing age and athletic involvement, and a reported 40% to 52% lifetime chance of an Achilles tendon injury in runners.[3] In a Dutch general-practice cohort of 57,725 registered patients, the incidence of GP visits for midportion Achilles tendinopathy in 2009 was 1.85 per 1,000 registered patients (2.35 per 1,000 in adults aged 21 to 60), and a relationship with sports activity was recorded in 35% of cases; the authors state that the actual incidence might be higher because of study limitations.[4]

In a cross-sectional study of 613 Norwegian elite athletes from 9 sports, 14.2% had current jumper's knee (patellar tendinopathy). Prevalence was 44.6% in volleyball and 31.9% in basketball, with no cases in cycling or orienteering, and lower in women (5.6%) than in men (13.5%).[8]

A living network meta-analysis of 29 randomised trials (42 treatments) of Achilles tendinopathy found that every active treatment class seemed superior to wait-and-see for midportion tendinopathy at 3 months, and that there seemed to be no clinically relevant difference between active treatments at 3 or 12 months. 22 trials (76%) were at high risk of bias, and the certainty of evidence was very low to low.[5] A 2023 living network meta-analysis of 68 trials in Achilles tendinopathy, patellar tendinopathy and greater trochanteric pain syndrome found no convincing evidence that any adjunct, alone or added to exercise, was more effective than exercise alone.[13]

Exercise approaches compared in the cited trials

  1. 1Heavy slow resistance (Achilles)
  2. 2Continued running and jumping with pain monitoring (Achilles)
  3. 3Heavy slow resistance and eccentric decline squats (patellar)
  4. 4Progressive tendon-loading exercise (patellar)
  5. 5Isometric holds (patellar)

  1. 1Heavy slow resistance (Achilles)

    In the sources
    In 58 people with chronic midportion Achilles tendinopathy, 12 weeks of heavy slow resistance or eccentric training gave equally good improvements that were maintained at 52 weeks; session compliance was 92% and 78% respectively.[6]

  2. 2Continued running and jumping with pain monitoring (Achilles)

    In the sources
    In 38 people who all followed the same tendon-loading exercise programme, the group allowed to continue running and jumping using a pain-monitoring model did not differ in rate of improvement from the group that stopped those activities for the first 6 weeks; both groups improved at every follow-up to 12 months, and the authors reported that no negative effects could be demonstrated from continuing.[7]

  3. 3Heavy slow resistance and eccentric decline squats (patellar)

    In the sources
    In 39 men, improvements after 12 weeks were maintained at the half-year follow-up with eccentric decline squats and heavy slow resistance but deteriorated after corticosteroid injections; at the half-year follow-up, treatment satisfaction was highest with heavy slow resistance.[9]

  4. 4Progressive tendon-loading exercise (patellar)

    In the sources
    In an investigator-blinded randomised trial of 76 patients, the VISA-P score improved more with progressive tendon-loading exercise than with eccentric exercise therapy after 24 weeks (28 vs 18 points; adjusted between-group difference 9 points, 95% CI 1 to 16).[10]

  5. 5Isometric holds (patellar)

    In the sources
    In a single-blinded randomised crossover study of 6 volleyball players, one bout of isometric contractions reduced pain during a single-leg decline squat more than isotonic contractions, and the reduction was sustained at 45 minutes after the isometric bout only.[11] A later pre-registered randomised crossover trial in 21 people with patellar tendinopathy found small pain reductions immediately after a single session of either isometric or dynamic exercise, not sustained at 45 minutes, with no difference between the two.[15] A pairwise meta-analysis of 3 trials found isometric exercise as effective as isotonic exercise for immediate pain relief, and its authors listed isometric exercise among promising interventions with inadequate evidence.[12]

What the guideline states and what the trials measured

The 2024 guideline (grade A, strong evidence) states that clinicians should use tendon loading exercise, with loads as high as tolerated, as a first-line treatment to improve function and decrease pain for individuals with midportion Achilles tendinopathy who do not have presumed frailty of the tendon structure. It describes tendon loading as eccentric, concentric, isometric, isotonic and plyometric use of the plantar flexor (calf) muscles, and reports improvements of 18 to 21 points on the VISA-A scale by 12 weeks in the studies it reviewed.[3] It notes that these studies mostly represent non-acute Achilles tendinopathy with pain for more than 3 months in physically active people, so generalisation is limited for people with an acute duration of symptoms or a low level of physical activity.[3]

The guideline also states (grade E, theoretical or foundational evidence) that individuals using tendon loading exercise should exercise at least 3 times a week at an intensity as high as tolerated, and (grade B) that clinicians should advise that complete rest is not indicated and that individuals should continue their activities within their pain tolerance. It states that exercise frequency (once a day to 3 times a week), number of sessions (24 to 168) and duration of care (6 weeks to 6 months) did not seem to influence outcomes, and that the most commonly reported harm of exercise was temporary symptom aggravation.[3]

Among other interventions, the guideline states that clinicians may use heel lifts to temporarily reduce ankle dorsiflexion during activity (grade C), should not use low-level laser therapy (grade C) and should not use therapeutic ultrasound alone (grade C), and that no recommendation can be made on orthoses because evidence is contradictory (grade D).[3]

For patellar tendinopathy, a 2021 network meta-analysis of 37 randomised trials assessing 33 interventions rated the evidence for the highest-ranked options as low or very low strength, and its authors concluded that eccentric loading, with or without adjuncts, should remain the first-line treatment.[12] The 2021 trial of progressive tendon-loading exercise concluded that it was superior to eccentric exercise therapy; the return-to-sport rate was 43% vs 27% and exercise adherence was 40% vs 49% after 24 weeks, and neither difference was statistically significant.[10] These are group results from trials, not a prediction for any individual.

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The 2024 guideline lists conditions to consider in the differential diagnosis of non-traumatic pain at the back of the ankle, including a partial tear of the Achilles tendon, bursitis, posterior ankle impingement, sural nerve irritation, stress fractures, systemic inflammatory disease and insertional Achilles tendinopathy. It states that imaging is not required to diagnose Achilles tendinopathy but is recommended when the diagnosis is uncertain, when response to care is delayed or when invasive treatments are being considered.[3]

The 2024 guideline reports that a systematic review of 10 cohort studies identified 9 risk factors for midportion Achilles tendinopathy, including prior lower-limb tendinopathy or fracture and use of ofloxacin (quinolone) antibiotics, and notes a high risk of bias in the studies identifying risk factors.[3]

The NHS (UK) Tendonitis page states that pain that is sudden and severe and happened during an accident or activity may mean a torn (ruptured) tendon, sometimes with a popping or snapping sound. It states to see a GP if symptoms after a joint injury do not improve within a few weeks, and to get help from NHS 111 for a lot of pain or a suspected ruptured tendon.[14]

The NHS (UK) heel pain page lists sudden sharp pain in the heel, swelling, a popping or snapping sound during the injury and difficulty walking as possible signs of a heel fracture or a ruptured Achilles tendon. It states to contact NHS 111 for signs including severe heel pain after an injury, a snap, grinding or popping noise at the time of injury, not being able to walk or to walk on tiptoes, or swelling and bruising in the calf and ankle, which it states might be signs of a broken heel bone or a broken ankle.[16]

The NHS (UK) knee pain page states to get advice from NHS 111 if a knee is very painful, cannot be moved or bear weight, is badly swollen or has changed shape, locks, gives way or painfully clicks, or if there is a very high temperature with redness or heat around the knee, which it states can be a sign of infection. It states to see a GP if knee pain does not improve within a few weeks.[17]

Frequently asked questions

What is the tendon pathology continuum?

A model proposed by Cook and Purdam in 2009 with three stages (reactive tendinopathy, tendon dysrepair and degenerative tendinopathy), in which load is the primary stimulus that moves a tendon along the continuum.[1]

What does the 2024 Achilles tendinopathy guideline state about exercise?

It states (grade A) that clinicians should use tendon loading exercise, with loads as high as tolerated, as a first-line treatment for midportion Achilles tendinopathy without presumed frailty of the tendon structure.[3]

Did heavy slow resistance and eccentric training differ in Achilles tendinopathy?

In a 12-week randomised trial of 58 patients, both gave equally good improvements that lasted to 52 weeks; compliance was higher with heavy slow resistance (92% vs 78%).[6]

How common is jumper's knee in elite athletes?

A study of 613 Norwegian elite athletes found current jumper's knee in 14.2%, ranging from none in cycling and orienteering to 44.6% in volleyball.[8]
