Strength training and menopause: what the research shows

What NICE, the NHS, a randomised hot-flush trial and reviews found on strength training, hot flushes, fitness and bone after menopause.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

NICE's menopause guideline states that people experiencing menopause should be told the importance of maintaining muscle mass and strength through physical activity.[3] The NHS lists regular exercise, with a focus on weight-bearing exercises to build strength, as this can help protect against weakening bones, and lists regular exercise and CBT among things that can help manage hot flushes.[2]

In a Swedish randomised trial, 58 postmenopausal women (mean age 55; mean 7.1 moderate or severe hot flushes a day) did 15 weeks of resistance training. Hot flush frequency changed by −43.6% in the training group and −2.0% in the control group (mean difference −2.7 a day).[4]

A Cochrane review of 5 trials (733 women) rated the evidence as low quality and, comparing exercise with no active treatment (3 trials, 454 women), found no evidence of a difference in frequency or intensity of vasomotor symptoms.[5]

A 2024 meta-analysis of 12 studies in healthy postmenopausal women found benefits of resistance training on physical fitness, including upper and lower body strength, and stated there is more debate about its influence on bone mineral density and anthropometric variables.[6]

A Cochrane review of 31 trials (1,817 women) found that women with stress urinary incontinence in pelvic floor muscle training groups were 8 times more likely to report cure than controls (56% vs 6%); intervention content and duration varied considerably between trials.[8]

Doses and protocols the studies used

Hot-flush trial: resistance training 3 times a week, 8 exercises, 2 sets of 8 to 12 repetitions, for 15 weeks.[4]

The 2024 meta-analysis describes resistance training 3 days a week in 60-minute sessions as a starting point for promotion.[6]

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous) a week, and muscle-strengthening activities involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.[1]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The NHS lists wearing lightweight clothing, keeping cool, using a fan or having a cold drink among ways to ease hot flushes.[2]

The UK consensus statement on osteoporosis states that people with vertebral fracture or multiple low-trauma fractures should usually exercise only up to an impact equivalent to brisk walking, and that targeted strength and balance training is the starting point it gives for people at risk of falls.[7]

ACSM's 2015 screening model bases pre-exercise medical clearance on current activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity.[10]
