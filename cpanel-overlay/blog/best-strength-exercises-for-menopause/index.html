Best strength exercises for menopause: 7 moves and a sample week

Menopause changes a lot at once. Strength training won't stop it. It helps you stay strong through it. Two or three short sessions a week. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 11 November 2026

Hot flushes at 3 a.m. Knees that complain on the stairs. Shopping bags that feel heavier than they used to. Menopause and the years around it change a lot at once.

NICE, which writes England's clinical guidelines, tells doctors to explain the importance of keeping muscle mass and strength through physical activity.[3] The NHS advises regular exercise, with a focus on weight-bearing exercise to build strength, as this can help protect against weakening bones. It also lists regular exercise among the things that can help with mood changes and hot flushes.[2]

In a Swedish trial, 58 postmenopausal women who had at least 4 moderate or severe hot flushes a day did 15 weeks of strength training, 3 times a week. Their moderate and severe hot flushes fell by 43.6% on average, against 2% in the group that kept their usual activity. One small trial.[4]

The wider picture is less certain. A Cochrane review of 5 trials with 733 women found too little evidence to say whether exercise in general treats hot flushes.[5] For hot flushes NICE offers HRT and suggests considering menopause-specific CBT.[3] So train for strength first. If your flushes ease, that is a bonus.

A 2024 review of 12 randomised trials in healthy postmenopausal women found that strength training improved upper and lower body strength and aerobic fitness. Its effects on bone density and body composition were less clear.[6]

This is coaching, not treatment. It sits alongside the care your doctor gives you. Read "Before you start".

The 7 best strength exercises for menopause

  1. 1Goblet squat to a chair
  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift
  3. 3One-arm row
  4. 4Incline push-up
  5. 5Step-up
  6. 6Prone back extension
  7. 7Pelvic floor squeeze

  1. 1Goblet squat to a chair

    Why
    Your legs carry you up stairs and out of chairs. The UK consensus on exercise for osteoporosis recommends resistance training to keep bones strong.[7]
    How
    Hold a dumbbell, a water bottle or a filled backpack at your chest. Feet a little wider than your hips. Sit back and down until you touch the chair, then stand. Too much? No weight, a higher chair. Easy? Pause just above the seat.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift

    Why
    Strong hips and hamstrings for lifting things off the floor. The hinge keeps your spine long; the same consensus advises avoiding postures with a lot of spinal bending.[7]
    How
    Hold two dumbbells or bags in front of your thighs, knees soft. Push your hips back and slide the weights down your thighs, back long, until you feel a stretch behind your legs. Stand up by pushing your hips forward. Too much? Lighter, or a shorter range, stopping at the knees.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3One-arm row

    Why
    Works the upper back that holds you tall, and balances all the pushing.
    How
    One hand and knee on a bench or sturdy chair, back flat. Pull a dumbbell or backpack up towards your hip, elbow close. Lower slowly. Too much? A lighter load, or a band. Easy? Heavier, with a two-second pause at the top.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4Incline push-up

    Why
    Chest, shoulders and arms, scaled to your level so you can build it week by week.
    How
    Hands on a counter or a sturdy table, body in one line from head to heels. Lower your chest to the edge, then push away. Too much? A higher surface, or the wall. Easy? A lower surface, then the floor.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 6 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Step-up

    Why
    One leg at a time, for strength and balance together. The consensus advises strength and balance training to reduce falls.[7]
    How
    Use the bottom stair, a hand on the rail or wall. Put your whole foot on the step, press through it and stand tall, then step down slowly. Too much? A lower step. Easy? Hold a weight in the other hand.
    Dose
    2 sets of 8 to 10 each leg. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Prone back extension

    Why
    Strengthens the muscles along your spine. The consensus recommends spinal extension exercise to improve posture.[7]
    How
    Lie face down, a folded towel under your forehead, arms by your sides. Squeeze your shoulder blades together and lift your chest a few centimetres, neck long, eyes on the floor. Hold for 2 seconds, lower slowly. Too much? Lift only your head and shoulders, or do it standing against a wall.
    Dose
    2 sets of 6 to 10. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  7. 7Pelvic floor squeeze

    Why
    Leaking when you cough, laugh or lift is common. A Cochrane review of 31 trials with 1,817 women found that pelvic floor muscle training can cure or improve stress urinary incontinence.[8]
    How
    Sit or lie comfortably. Squeeze and lift as if stopping wind and the flow of urine at the same time. Keep breathing; don't squeeze your buttocks or hold your breath. Let go fully between squeezes. Add some quick squeezes too.
    Dose
    Up to 10 slow squeezes of up to 10 seconds, then up to 10 quick ones, most days. Start with what you can do well.

How much, how hard, how often

WHO, for adults: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening work for the major muscle groups on 2 or more days.[1]

In the hot flush trial, women trained 3 times a week: 8 exercises, 2 sets of 8 to 12, with loads raised as they got stronger.[4] The 2024 review suggests 3 days a week as a starting point.[6] Two or three days works. Sets should feel hard but tidy: keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal.

Progression: when you beat the top of the rep range by 1 or 2, add a little load, about 2 to 10%, or slow the lowering.[9]

Impact: the osteoporosis consensus pairs strength training with impact exercise for bones. If you have had a spine fracture or several low-trauma fractures, it advises impact only up to brisk walking.[7] The sample week keeps impact to a few heel drops. That cap is a coaching choice, not a research limit.

Hot flush mid-session? Pause. Train in a cool room, in light clothes, with a fan and a cold drink nearby. The NHS lists these among the ways to manage hot flushes.[2]

Pelvic floor: the trials varied a lot in what they prescribed.[8] If you're not sure you're doing it right, a pelvic health physio can check.

A sample week of strength training for menopause

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it with your doctor to your body, your medicines and your history.

  • Monday, about 35 minutes: goblet squat, one-arm row, incline push-up, back extension. 2 to 3 sets each. Pelvic floor squeezes to finish.
  • Tuesday: a 20 to 30 minute brisk walk.
  • Wednesday, about 35 minutes: Romanian deadlift, step-up, incline push-up, back extension. Then heel drops: rise on your toes, drop onto your heels, 2 sets of 10, holding a rail.
  • Thursday: a walk, or rest. Pelvic floor squeezes.
  • Friday, about 35 minutes: repeat Monday.
  • Saturday: something you enjoy: a walk with someone, dancing, a class.
  • Sunday: rest.
  • Load cap for this sample week: weights you can move for 8 to 12 smooth reps. No maximal lifts, no training to failure, no loaded deep forward bends, no jumping. Heel drops capped at 2 sets of 10; skip them if you have osteoporosis or a past fracture until a physio okays them. A bad night's sleep? Do one round and call it a win.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[10] Strength training does not replace medical care. NICE offers HRT for hot flushes and suggests considering menopause-specific CBT for hot flushes, and for low mood or sleep problems that come with them. Talk to your doctor about what fits you.[3] If you have osteoporosis or have had a spine fracture, get a physio's advice first: avoid loaded deep forward bending, and keep impact to brisk-walking level unless they say otherwise.[7] Leaking that is getting worse, a feeling of heaviness or a bulge in the vagina: see your doctor or a pelvic health physio. Stop for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.
