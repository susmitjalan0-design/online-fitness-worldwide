Strength training and testosterone: what the research shows

What reviews and trials report on testosterone in the minutes after a session, on resting levels over weeks of training, and on whether those changes track gains in muscle and strength.

By Susmit Jalan, strength and conditioning coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 21 November 2026

The cited sources separate two measures. A 2005 review reports that testosterone rises acutely for about 15 to 30 minutes after a resistance exercise session when the stimulus is adequate, and that many studies show no significant change in resting testosterone during training, even as strength and muscle size increase.[1]

Trials in young men that compared those short rises with training outcomes found no link. Over 12 weeks in 56 men, acute rises in free testosterone, growth hormone and IGF-1 did not correlate with gains in lean mass or leg-press strength.[3] In a 15-week arm-training study, raising post-exercise hormone levels did not change muscle growth or strength gains.[2]

This page is education only. It reports study findings and makes no claim that any form of training changes hormone levels in a given person. The trials cited here enrolled men only.

What the studies measured

  1. 1The short rise after a session
  2. 2Resting levels over weeks of training
  3. 3Hormones and muscle growth
  4. 4Men aged 60 and over

  1. 1The short rise after a session

    In the sources
    A 2005 review reports acute rises in testosterone during the 15 to 30 minutes after resistance exercise, largest with high volume, moderate-to-high intensity, short rests and a large muscle mass.[1]

  2. 2Resting levels over weeks of training

    In the sources
    The same review reports that many studies show no significant change in resting testosterone during training, despite gains in strength and muscle size.[1]

  3. 3Hormones and muscle growth

    In the sources
    In 49 resistance-trained young men over 12 weeks, no hormone measured in blood or muscle was consistently associated with muscle growth. Muscle androgen receptor content was linearly related to the change in lean mass and fibre size.[4]

  4. 4Men aged 60 and over

    In the sources
    A 2018 meta-analysis of 22 studies found no significant effect of resistance training on resting testosterone in men aged 60 and over, and small, significant increases after endurance and interval training.[5]

Study designs and limits

In West and colleagues' 2010 study, 12 young men trained each arm separately for 15 weeks: one arm with arm curls alone, the other with the same curls followed by high-volume leg exercise that raised testosterone, growth hormone and IGF-1. Muscle cross-sectional area rose 12% and 10% respectively, with no difference between conditions, and strength gains did not differ.[2]

In the 56-man cohort, cortisol and growth hormone responses were weakly correlated with gains in muscle fibre size, and no hormone response correlated with strength gains.[3] The older-men meta-analysis pooled 9 randomised and 13 uncontrolled trials.[5]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The NHS states that testosterone levels in men fall by about 1% a year from around age 30 to 40, which is unlikely to cause problems in itself, and that late-onset hypogonadism is an uncommon, specific condition rather than a normal part of ageing. It states that diagnosis is based on symptoms and blood tests, and that people with symptoms are to see a GP.[6]

ACSM's 2015 screening model bases pre-exercise medical clearance on current activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity.[7]

This page reports what the cited sources found and state. It gives no advice and recommends no course of action. It is not medical advice.

Frequently asked questions

Does testosterone go up after a strength training session?

A 2005 review reports acute rises for about 15 to 30 minutes after resistance exercise when the stimulus is adequate, largest with high-volume, short-rest sessions that work a large muscle mass.[1]

Does strength training raise resting testosterone?

The same review reports that many studies show no significant change in resting levels during training.[1] A 2018 meta-analysis in men aged 60 and over found no significant effect of resistance training on resting testosterone.[5]

Do bigger hormone rises lead to more muscle or strength?

In young men, trials found no link between acute rises in testosterone, growth hormone or IGF-1 and gains in lean mass or strength.[2][3] A 12-week study found muscle androgen receptor content, not blood hormone levels, related to muscle growth.[4]

What does the NHS state about falling testosterone with age?

It states that levels fall by about 1% a year from around age 30 to 40, which is unlikely to cause problems in itself, and that late-onset hypogonadism is uncommon and diagnosed from symptoms and blood tests.[6]
