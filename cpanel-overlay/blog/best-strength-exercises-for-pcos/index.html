Best strength exercises for PCOS (PMOS): 7 moves and a sample week

PCOS is a hormone and metabolic condition, not just an ovary one. Lifting won't cure it. It can help your insulin, your waist and your strength. Lift 2 or 3 non-consecutive days a week, walk most days. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 14 November 2026

Irregular periods. Weight that's hard to shift. Tired a lot. Polycystic ovary syndrome affects about 1 in 8 women. In May 2026 an international consensus gave it a new name: polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, PMOS. Both names will be used for a few years.[4] Same condition. I'll say PCOS here.

The NHS lists the main symptoms: irregular periods, extra hair growth or hair loss, weight gain or difficulty losing weight, acne, tiredness, depression or anxiety. It says you're more likely to have it if a close relative does, or if you're from an Asian background. Its advice includes eating a balanced diet, exercising regularly and losing weight if you're overweight.[2]

The 2023 international PCOS guideline keeps a healthy lifestyle at the centre of care, with awareness of weight stigma. It also says the evidence is still low to moderate quality.[3] So let's be honest about what exercise does and doesn't do.

A review of 18 trials found that, compared with no exercise, exercise lowered fasting insulin, insulin resistance, total and LDL cholesterol and triglycerides. Against usual care, it improved fitness and cut waist size by about 2.6 cm. The effects were modest and the evidence low or very low quality.[6]

A Cochrane review of 15 trials found lifestyle programmes may cut weight by about 1.7 kg and lower the free androgen index, a marker of male-type hormones. No trial measured live birth or period regularity.[5] Exercise is not fertility treatment.

Strength work in particular: 45 inactive women with PCOS did progressive strength training 3 times a week for 4 months. Testosterone and waist size fell and muscle mass rose. There was no control group, so treat it as a promising signal, not proof.[8]

This is coaching, not medical care. Got a diagnosis, symptoms you're not sure about, or take regular medicines? Check with your doctor before you start. Read "Before you start".

The 7 best strength exercises for PCOS

  1. 1Goblet squat to a chair
  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift
  3. 3Incline push-up
  4. 4One-arm dumbbell row
  5. 5Step-up
  6. 6Farmer's carry
  7. 7Glute bridge

  1. 1Goblet squat to a chair

    Why
    Thighs and buttocks are your biggest muscles. Big muscles use a lot of glucose, more work per rep.
    How
    Hold one dumbbell or a full water bottle at your chest. Feet a little wider than hips, a chair behind you. Sit back until you touch the chair, chest up, knees in line with your toes. Stand up tall. Too much? Sit fully, then stand, no weight. Easy? A heavier weight, or a 3-second lowering.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift

    Why
    Back of the legs and the back itself. The hinge you use to lift a bag from the floor.
    How
    Stand tall, weights in front of your thighs, knees soft. Push your hips back and slide the weights down your legs with a long, flat back. Stop when you feel the back of your thighs, then drive your hips forward to stand. Too much? Hands to just above the knees, no weight. Easy? Heavier weights, or a slower lowering.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3Incline push-up

    Why
    Chest, shoulders and arms in one move. The height of your hands sets how hard it is.
    How
    Hands on a kitchen counter or a sturdy table, a little wider than your shoulders. Body in one line from head to heels. Lower your chest to the edge, then push away. Too much? Hands on a wall. Easy? A lower surface, then the floor.
    Dose
    3 sets of 6 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4One-arm dumbbell row

    Why
    Pulls balance the pushes. Upper back and arms, for posture and carrying.
    How
    One hand and one knee on a bench or sturdy chair, the other foot on the floor. Back flat. Pull the weight towards your hip, elbow close to your side, and lower slowly. Too much? A lighter weight, or a band tied to a door handle. Easy? A heavier weight, or a 2-second pause at the top.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Step-up

    Why
    One leg at a time, like stairs. Strength, balance, and your heart rate climbs.
    How
    A sturdy step or the bottom stair, a hand on the wall or rail. Put your whole foot on the step and push up through it until you stand tall. Step down slowly. Too much? A lower step. Easy? A higher step, or a dumbbell in each hand.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 10 each leg. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Farmer's carry

    Why
    Grip, shoulders and trunk, working together. A lot of muscle, simple to learn.
    How
    A heavy dumbbell, bag or bucket in each hand. Stand tall, shoulders down, and walk with short, steady steps. Don't lean to one side. Too much? Lighter loads, or a shorter walk. Easy? Heavier loads, or a longer walk.
    Dose
    3 to 4 walks of 20 to 40 metres, or 30 to 45 seconds. Stop while your grip and posture are still good.

  7. 7Glute bridge

    Why
    Buttocks and the back of the legs, lying down. Kind to knees and backs.
    How
    Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Squeeze your buttocks and lift your hips until your body makes a line from shoulders to knees. Lower slowly. Too much? A smaller lift. Easy? A 2-second hold at the top, a weight on your hips, or one leg at a time.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

How much, how hard, how often

WHO, for adults: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening work for the major muscle groups on 2 or more days.[1]

Exercise and Sports Science Australia's 2024 PCOS position statement uses the same base: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 non-consecutive days. For modest weight loss, at least 250 minutes of moderate or 150 of vigorous, plus the 2 strength days.[7]

The same statement asks exercise professionals to keep weight stigma and the mental load of PCOS in mind.[7] So the scale is one number, not the only one. We also track your lifts, your walking, your waist and how you feel.

How hard: sets should feel hard but tidy. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal.

Traffic-light pain check, 0 to 10, during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or worse the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. A coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

Progression: when you beat the top of the rep range by 1 or 2 with clean reps, add a little load, about 2 to 10%.[9]

Impact: the sample week has no jumping and no running. Brisk walking covers the aerobic part. That cap is a coaching choice, not a research limit. Build up first, then add more if you enjoy it.

A sample week of strength training for PCOS

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it with your doctor to your body, your medicines and your history.

  • Monday, about 40 minutes: goblet squat, one-arm row, Romanian deadlift, incline push-up. 3 sets each. Farmer's carry to finish.
  • Tuesday: a 30 to 45 minute brisk walk. You can talk, but not sing.
  • Wednesday: rest, or an easy walk.
  • Thursday, about 40 minutes: step-up, incline push-up, one-arm row, glute bridge. Farmer's carry to finish.
  • Friday: a brisk walk, a cycle or a swim, 30 to 45 minutes.
  • Saturday, about 30 minutes: repeat Monday, 2 sets each. Or something active you enjoy: a long walk, a dance class.
  • Sunday: rest, or an easy walk.
  • Load cap for this sample week: weights you can move for 8 to 12 smooth reps. No maximal lifts, no training to failure, no jumping, no running. Bad period pain or a very tired day? Do half and walk. It still counts.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[10] Strength training does not replace medical care. The NHS says to see a GP if you think you might have PMOS, or your symptoms are affecting your everyday life, work and relationships. It says PMOS can raise the risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease, and regular check-ups help.[2] Outside the UK, that means your doctor. Pregnant, or having fertility treatment? Ask your doctor what suits you now. Sore knees, hips or back? Start with the easier version of each move and build up. Stop for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.
