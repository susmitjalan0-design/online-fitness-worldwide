PCOS (PMOS) and strength training: what the research shows

What the NHS, the 2023 international guideline, an exercise position statement, reviews and a resistance trial found on exercise and PCOS.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

An international consensus chose polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) as the new name for polycystic ovary syndrome, with a transition period under way.[4] The NHS lists a higher likelihood of PMOS if a close relative has it or for people from an Asian background.[2] A Cochrane review states that PCOS affects 8% to 13% of reproductive-aged women.[5]

The 2023 international guideline maintains emphasis on healthy lifestyle, emotional wellbeing and quality of life, with awareness and consideration of weight stigma, and states that the evidence remains of generally low to moderate quality.[3]

A meta-analysis of 18 trials found that, compared with control, exercise reduced fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, total and LDL cholesterol and triglycerides (low or very low-quality evidence). Compared with usual care, exercise improved VO2max (3.84 ml/kg/min), waist circumference (−2.62 cm) and body fat percentage (−1.39%). No effect was found for blood pressure, fasting glucose or HDL cholesterol.[6]

A Cochrane review of 15 studies (498 participants) found lifestyle intervention may reduce weight (−1.68 kg) and the free androgen index (low-quality evidence). No studies assessed live birth or miscarriage, or reported menstrual regularity.[5]

In a study of 45 sedentary women with PCOS (and 52 without), progressive resistance training three times a week for 4 months reduced plasma testosterone, fasting glucose and waist circumference, and increased muscle mass indexes compared with baseline.[8]

Doses and protocols the studies used

The 2024 position statement gives targets for individuals with PCOS of 150 to 300 minutes of moderate or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening on two non-consecutive days; for further benefits and modest weight loss, at least 250 minutes moderate or 150 minutes vigorous, plus the two strength days.[7]

The same statement states that exercise professionals should consider the psychological burden, including weight stigma, and the high prevalence of comorbidities.[7]

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous) a week, and muscle-strengthening activities involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.[1]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The NHS states to see a GP if someone thinks they might have PMOS or the symptoms are affecting everyday life, work and relationships.[2] The resistance training study compared results with baseline.[8]

ACSM's 2015 screening model bases pre-exercise medical clearance on current activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity.[10]
