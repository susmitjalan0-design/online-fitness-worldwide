Exercise for balance and fall prevention: what the research shows

What guidelines and trials found on exercise and falls in older adults: effect sizes, exercise types and the doses studied.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that, as part of weekly activity, older adults should do varied multicomponent physical activity that emphasises functional balance and strength training, at moderate or greater intensity, on 3 or more days a week, to enhance functional capacity and to prevent falls.[1] The NHS falls page states that guidelines for older adults recommend being active every day and doing activities that improve strength and balance at least twice a week.[2]

A Cochrane review of 108 trials (23,407 people living in the community; average age 76) found that exercise reduces the rate of falls by 23% (rate ratio 0.77; 59 studies) and the number of people having one or more falls by 15%, with high-certainty evidence.[5]

A meta-analysis of 88 trials (19,478 participants) found exercise reduced the rate of falls by 21% overall. Greater effects came from programmes that challenged balance and involved more than 3 hours of exercise a week; in combination these were linked to a 39% reduction in falls (rate ratio 0.61).[6]

The 2022 World Guidelines for falls prevention recommend exercise programmes for community-dwelling older adults that include balance-challenging and functional exercises (for example sit-to-stand and stepping), three or more times a week, individualised, progressed in intensity for at least 12 weeks and continued longer for greater effect. They recommend adding Tai Chi and/or individualised progressive resistance strength training when feasible.[4]

In a trial of women aged 80 and over in New Zealand (116 exercise, 117 control), a home strength and balance programme led to fewer falls over one year (0.87 vs 1.34 falls per person per year) and a relative hazard of 0.61 for a first fall with injury.[8]

Doses and protocols the studies used

A meta-analysis of balance training in healthy older adults found the largest effects with a training period of 11 to 12 weeks, three sessions a week and a total of 36 to 40 sessions.[7]

ACSM's 2009 position stand recommends a 2 to 10% load increase when the current load can be done for one to two repetitions over the target.[9]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The NHS falls page lists reasons to see a GP (worry about balance or mobility, or having had a fall) and to call 999 (a person who has fallen may have injured the head, back, neck or hip, or cannot get up). It states that a falls service may check for muscle weakness, weak bones or eyesight problems and review medicines.[2]

ACSM's 2015 screening model bases pre-exercise medical clearance on current activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity.[10]
