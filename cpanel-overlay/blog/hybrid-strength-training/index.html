Hybrid strength training: what the concurrent-training research shows

What studies report when strength and endurance training are combined in the same programme, outside any race preparation: effects on strength, muscle size, power and endurance.

By Susmit Jalan, strength and conditioning coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 21 November 2026

Researchers call combined strength and endurance training concurrent training. A 2022 meta-analysis of 43 studies found that adding aerobic training to an identical strength programme did not compromise maximal strength or muscle growth, but attenuated explosive strength, more so when both were done in the same session.[3]

Earlier work reported interference. A 2012 meta-analysis of 21 studies found larger effect sizes for strength, muscle size and power with strength training alone than with concurrent training, and linked the interference to the type, frequency and duration of endurance work.[2] In a 1980 study, strength in a combined group rose like the strength-only group for 7 weeks, then levelled off and declined in weeks 9 and 10, while the rise in maximal oxygen uptake was unaffected.[1]

WHO's 2020 guidelines recommend both aerobic activity and muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week for adults.[6]

Variables the studies examined

  1. 1Running or cycling
  2. 2Same session or separate sessions
  3. 3Order within a session
  4. 4Training status

  1. 1Running or cycling

    In the sources
    In the 2012 meta-analysis, concurrent training with running, but not cycling, decreased muscle growth and strength.[2] The 2022 meta-analysis found no difference between cycling and running.[3]

  2. 2Same session or separate sessions

    In the sources
    In trained individuals, concurrent training reduced lower-body one-repetition maximum only when both were done in the same session.[4]

  3. 3Order within a session

    In the sources
    A 2018 meta-analysis found lower-body one-repetition maximum 3.96 kg higher when strength preceded endurance; order did not affect aerobic capacity.[5]

  4. 4Training status

    In the sources
    Concurrent training reduced lower-body strength gains in trained individuals, not in untrained or moderately trained ones.[4]

Effect sizes and protocols reported

The 2012 meta-analysis reported mean effect sizes for strength of 1.76 with strength training, 1.44 with concurrent training and 0.78 with endurance training; for muscle size, 1.23, 0.85 and 0.27. Frequency and duration of endurance training were negatively correlated with gains in muscle size, strength and power.[2]

In the 1980 study, the strength group trained 30 to 40 minutes a day, 5 days a week, and the endurance group 40 minutes a day, 6 days a week, for 10 weeks; the combined group did both. Maximal oxygen uptake rose by about 20 to 25% in both endurance groups.[1]

Frequently asked questions

What is concurrent training?

The research term for combining strength and endurance training in one programme. A 2022 meta-analysis of 43 studies compared it with an identical strength programme alone.[3]

Does cardio reduce strength gains?

A 2022 meta-analysis found no compromise to maximal strength or muscle growth, but attenuated explosive strength.[3] A 2021 meta-analysis found reduced lower-body strength gains in trained individuals when both were done in the same session.[4]

Does the order of strength and endurance work matter?

A 2018 meta-analysis found lower-body one-repetition maximum 3.96 kg higher when strength came first in the session, with no effect of order on aerobic capacity.[5]

Does running interfere more than cycling?

The 2012 meta-analysis found running, but not cycling, decreased muscle growth and strength;[2] the 2022 meta-analysis found no difference between the two.[3]
