Core exercise and lower back health: what the research shows

What guidelines and reviews found on exercise for long-term low back pain, which exercise types were compared, and prevention.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

The NHS states that back pain, particularly lower back pain, is very common, usually improves within a few weeks, and can sometimes last longer or keep coming back. Its self-care list includes staying active and continuing daily activities, and not staying in bed for long periods.[3]

WHO's guideline on chronic primary low back pain suggests structured exercise therapy or programmes for adults, as a conditional recommendation based on low-certainty evidence.[1] NICE guideline NG59 states: consider a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind–body or a combination) for people with a specific episode or flare-up of low back pain.[2]

A 2021 Cochrane review of 249 trials in adults with low back pain lasting more than 12 weeks found moderate-certainty evidence that exercise reduced pain compared with no treatment, usual care or placebo (mean difference −15.2 points on a 0 to 100 scale at earliest follow-up). The effect on functional limitations was small and below the authors' threshold for a clinically important difference.[4]

A Cochrane review of 29 trials (2,431 people) found low- to high-quality evidence that motor control ("core") exercise is not clinically more effective than other exercise for chronic non-specific low back pain.[6]

A network meta-analysis of 89 studies (5,578 patients) ranked Pilates highest for pain, and resistance training and stabilisation/motor control exercise highest for physical function, compared with true control.[5]

On prevention, a meta-analysis found exercise alone reduced the risk of low back pain by 33% (risk ratio 0.67; 8 trials, 1,634 people).[7] A second review (21 trials, 30,850 participants) found moderate-quality evidence that exercise combined with education reduced the risk of an episode (risk ratio 0.55).[8]

Doses and protocols the studies used

The prevention meta-analysis concludes that strengthening combined with either stretching or aerobic exercise, 2 to 3 times a week, can reasonably be recommended for preventing low back pain in the general population.[7]

ACSM's 2009 position stand recommends loads of 8 to 12 repetition maximum for novices, and a 2 to 10% load increase when the current load can be done for one to two repetitions over the target.[9]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The NHS lists reasons to see a GP, including back pain that does not improve after a few weeks of home treatment, stops day-to-day activities, does not improve with rest or is worse at night, or comes with unexplained weight loss. It lists reasons to call 999 or go to A&E, including pain, tingling, weakness or numbness in both legs; loss of feeling around the genitals or anus; changes in bladder or bowels; chest pain; or pain that started after a serious accident.[3]

ACSM's 2015 screening model bases pre-exercise medical clearance on current activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity.[10]
