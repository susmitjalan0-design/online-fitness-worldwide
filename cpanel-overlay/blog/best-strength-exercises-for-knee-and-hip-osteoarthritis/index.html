Best strength exercises for knee and hip osteoarthritis: 7 moves and a sample week

Exercise is a core treatment for knee and hip osteoarthritis. Strong thighs and hips help sore joints. Two or three short sessions a week. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 12 November 2026

Stiff for the first few minutes after you get up. A knee that aches on the stairs. A hip that grumbles getting out of the car. Osteoarthritis is common, and it is easy to start moving less because of it.

NICE, which writes England's clinical guidelines, calls therapeutic exercise a core treatment for osteoarthritis, along with weight management if appropriate. It tells doctors to offer exercise tailored to the person, for example local muscle strengthening and general aerobic fitness.[3] The international OARSI guidelines also list structured land-based exercise as a core treatment for knee and hip osteoarthritis.[8]

A Cochrane review of land-based exercise for knee osteoarthritis pooled 44 trials with 3,537 people. Compared with no exercise, exercise reduced pain by about 12 points on a 0 to 100 scale, and improved physical function by about 10 points.[4]

For the hip, the evidence is smaller. A Cochrane review of 9 trials with 549 people found exercise reduced pain by about 8 points and improved function by about 7 points on a 0 to 100 scale. The drop in pain was still there 3 to 6 months after the programmes ended.[5]

In Denmark, the GLA:D programme gives people with knee or hip osteoarthritis education plus 12 supervised exercise sessions over 6 weeks. In registry data from 9,825 people, average pain fell by 12.4 points on a 0 to 100 scale at 3 months. That was a before-and-after registry with no control group.[6]

This is coaching, not treatment. Check with your doctor or physio before you start, especially if a joint is hot, swollen or newly painful. Read "Before you start".

The 7 best strength exercises for knee and hip osteoarthritis

  1. 1Sit-to-stand from a chair
  2. 2Seated knee extension
  3. 3Step-up
  4. 4Glute bridge
  5. 5Side-lying hip abduction
  6. 6Single-leg balance at the counter
  7. 7Calf raise

  1. 1Sit-to-stand from a chair

    Why
    Getting up from a chair is one of the first things osteoarthritis makes hard. It trains thighs and hips together. GLA:D tracks progress with a 30-second chair-stand test.[6]
    How
    Sit near the front of a sturdy chair, feet hip-width, a little behind your knees. Lean forward, nose over toes, and stand up without using your hands. Sit down slowly, under control. Too much? A higher seat, a cushion, or hands on the armrests. Easy? Hold a weight at your chest, or lower more slowly.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Seated knee extension

    Why
    Works the quadriceps, the muscles at the front of the thigh, on their own. In a review of 48 trials, quadriceps-specific exercise reduced knee pain more than general lower-limb exercise.[7]
    How
    Sit tall on a chair. Straighten one knee until the leg is nearly level, toes up. Hold for 2 seconds, lower slowly. Too much? A smaller range, no weight. Easy? An ankle weight, or a resistance band looped round the chair leg.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15 each leg. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3Step-up

    Why
    Stairs, kerbs, buses. One leg at a time builds strength for the steps you actually climb.
    How
    Use the bottom stair, a hand on the rail. Put your whole foot on the step, press through it and stand tall, then step down slowly. Keep the knee roughly in line with your toes. Too much? A lower step or a small block. Easy? A weight in the other hand.
    Dose
    2 sets of 8 to 10 each leg. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4Glute bridge

    Why
    Strong buttock muscles support the hip when you stand up and walk. Kind to sore knees, because you are lying down.
    How
    Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Squeeze your buttocks and lift your hips until your body is in a line from shoulders to knees. Hold 2 seconds, lower slowly. Too much? A smaller lift. Easy? A weight across your hips, or one leg at a time.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Side-lying hip abduction

    Why
    Works the muscles on the side of the hip that keep your pelvis level each time you stand on one leg, which is every step you take.
    How
    Lie on your side, bottom knee bent, top leg straight in line with your body. Lift the top leg to about hip height, toes pointing forward, and lower slowly. Don't roll backwards. Too much? A smaller lift, or do it standing at a counter. Easy? A band above your knees or an ankle weight.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Single-leg balance at the counter

    Why
    GLA:D's exercise is neuromuscular: it trains control and balance as well as strength.[6] Steadier on one leg, steadier on the stairs.
    How
    Stand at a kitchen counter, fingertips on it. Lift one foot and stand tall on the other, knee soft, pelvis level. Too much? Both hands on the counter, or the toes of the lifted foot resting on the floor. Easy? Fingertips off the counter, then turn your head slowly side to side.
    Dose
    3 holds of up to 30 seconds each leg, most days. Wobbling badly? Back to both hands on the counter.

  7. 7Calf raise

    Why
    Your calves push you forward with every step and help with stairs and balance.
    How
    Hold a counter or a wall. Rise onto the balls of both feet, pause, then lower slowly over 3 seconds. Too much? A smaller rise. Easy? One leg at a time, or a weight in one hand.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

How much, how hard, how often

WHO, for adults: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening work for the major muscle groups on 2 or more days. Over 65? Add balance and strength activity on 3 or more days.[1]

How often: in a review of 48 knee osteoarthritis trials, supervised exercise at least 3 times a week reduced pain more than less frequent exercise. Intensity, session length, baseline pain and how bad the X-ray looked did not change the effect.[7] So aim for 3 days a week. Two still counts.

How hard: sets should feel hard but tidy. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal.

Traffic-light pain check, 0 to 10, during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5, and back to your usual by the next morning: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or a joint that is more painful or swollen the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. This traffic light is a coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding. NICE says joint pain may increase when you start exercising, and that regular, consistent exercise still helps the joints in the long term.[3]

Progression: when you beat the top of the rep range by 1 or 2 and pain stays green, add a little load, about 2 to 10%, or slow the lowering.[9]

Impact: the sample week has no running and no jumping. Walking, cycling or swimming cover the aerobic part. That cap is a coaching choice, not a research limit. Want to get back to running or sport? Plan it with your physio.

Weight: if you are carrying extra weight, NICE says any weight loss is likely to help, and losing 10% of body weight is likely to be better than 5%.[3]

A sample week of strength training for knee and hip osteoarthritis

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it with your doctor to your body, your medicines and your history.

  • Monday, about 30 minutes: sit-to-stand, seated knee extension, glute bridge, side-lying hip abduction. 2 to 3 sets each. Balance holds to finish.
  • Tuesday: a 20 to 30 minute walk at a pace that keeps pain in the green, or an easy cycle.
  • Wednesday, about 30 minutes: step-up, seated knee extension, glute bridge, calf raise. Balance holds to finish.
  • Thursday: a walk, a cycle or a swim, or rest.
  • Friday, about 30 minutes: repeat Monday.
  • Saturday: something you enjoy: a walk with someone, gardening, a class.
  • Sunday: rest. A few gentle knee bends and hip circles if you feel stiff.
  • Load cap for this sample week: weights you can move for 8 to 15 smooth reps. No maximal lifts, no training to failure, no running, no jumping, no deep loaded squats. Step no higher than a normal stair. A flare-up day? Do half, in the green, and call it a win.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[10] Strength training does not replace medical care. The NHS says to discuss any new exercise plan with your GP or physiotherapist before you start, and warns that doing too much too quickly, or the wrong sort of exercise, may damage your joints.[2] Outside the UK, that means your doctor or physio. A joint that is suddenly hot, red and swollen, especially with a fever; pain after a fall; a leg you cannot put weight on; a knee that locks or keeps giving way: stop and get it checked the same day. Had a joint replacement or other surgery? Follow your surgeon's or physio's plan first. Stop for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.
