Fitness during a long or unplanned blackout: what official guidance and research show

What health agencies state on darkness, falls, carbon monoxide, heat, cold and drinking water when the power is off for a long time, and what studies report on training without equipment.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 21 November 2026

None of the cited sources studies exercise during a blackout. Official guidance on long or unplanned power cuts covers hazards that also apply to activity at home: low light and falls, fire from candles, carbon monoxide from generators and indoor burners, heat and cold without electric cooling or heating, and safe drinking water.[1][2] Research on training without equipment and on fluids during exercise is summarised below.

The CDC states that when power outages occur during emergencies such as hurricanes or winter storms, using other sources of fuel or electricity for heating, cooling or cooking can cause carbon monoxide to build up in a home, garage or camper and poison the people and animals inside.[1] Ready.gov states that extended power outages may disrupt communications, water and transport and prevent the use of medical devices.[2]

WHO lists unsafe stairs, lighting or walking surfaces among the risk factors for falls, alongside impaired balance or vision, and estimates about 842,000 deaths from falls worldwide in 2023, making falls the second-leading cause of unintentional injury death after road injuries.[4] The NHS fall-prevention list includes not having too much clutter at home, or things that could be tripped on such as loose wires or rugs.[12]

The CDC states that battery-powered flashlights and lanterns are used rather than candles or gas lanterns, to minimise the risk of fire; where candles are used, it states that they are kept away from anything that can catch fire and that someone always stays near them.[1] Ready.gov's preparation steps include flashlights for every household member.[2]

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that adults should do muscle-strengthening activities involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week, and that bouts of activity of any duration count.[9] WHO's COVID-19 guidance on staying active at home names press-ups, sit-ups and squats using body weight.[13] In an 8-week trial in 18 young men, push-ups at a load similar to 40% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) bench press, and bench press at 40% 1RM, produced comparable gains in bench-press 1RM and in triceps and chest muscle thickness.[10]

What official sources state on heat, cold and fluids

WHO states that heat stress is a leading cause of weather-related deaths and that power shortages can accompany heatwaves. Its measures include avoiding strenuous activity during the hottest time of day, using night air to cool the home, closing and covering windows during the day when it is hotter outside, spending 2 to 3 hours a day in a cool place, and drinking water regularly (1 cup an hour and at least 2 to 3 litres a day). It states that electric fans are used only below 40°C, because above that temperature fans heat the body.[5]

The NHS lists people who are physically active outdoors, such as runners, cyclists and walkers, among those more vulnerable in a heatwave. Its tips include avoiding exercise or activity that makes the body hotter, drinking extra fluids, and cool showers; it states that electric fans can help if the temperature is below 35 degrees.[6] The CDC states that fans will not prevent heat-related illness when it is really hot, and that even a few hours in air conditioning, such as at a shopping centre or public library, can help.[1]

The NHS states that keeping warm in winter can help prevent colds, flu and more serious problems such as heart attacks, strokes, pneumonia and depression, and that homes are heated, if possible, to at least 18°C in rooms used regularly.[7] For a power outage, the CDC lists extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm winter coats among heat sources, and describes hypothermia (body temperature below 95°F, 35°C) as a medical emergency.[1]

ACSM's 2007 position stand on exercise and fluid replacement states that the goal of drinking during exercise is to prevent excessive dehydration (more than 2% body-weight loss from water deficit) and excessive changes in electrolyte balance, and that sweat rates and sweat electrolyte content vary considerably between individuals.[8] The CDC states that water purification systems may not be fully working when the power goes out, and that safe water for drinking includes bottled, boiled or treated water.[1]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

Carbon monoxide: the CDC states that a generator or other petrol-powered engine should never be used inside a home, basement or garage, or less than 20 feet (about 6 metres) from any window, door or vent; that a charcoal grill, hibachi, lantern or portable camping stove should never be used inside a home, tent or camper; and that a gas range or oven should never be used to heat a home.[1] The NHS lists using barbecues or camping stoves inside as a cause of carbon monoxide build-up, and lists symptoms including headache, dizziness, feeling or being sick, weakness, tiredness and confusion, chest and muscle pain, and shortness of breath. For people who think they have carbon monoxide poisoning, it states: stop using the appliance if possible, go outside, and seek medical advice as soon as possible.[3]

Warning symptoms: ACSM's 2015 screening recommendations list signs and symptoms that include pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw or arms, shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion, dizziness or fainting, palpitations, and unusual fatigue. They state that physically active people who develop such signs or symptoms discontinue exercise and seek medical clearance before resuming exercise of any intensity.[11]

Heat illness: the CDC states that in heat stroke body temperature can rise to 106°F (about 41°C) or higher within 10 to 15 minutes, and that heat stroke can cause death or permanent disability without emergency care.[1]

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to exercise in the dark during a blackout?

No cited source tests exercise in the dark. WHO lists unsafe lighting, stairs or walking surfaces among the risk factors for falls,[4] and the CDC states that battery-powered flashlights and lanterns are used instead of candles or gas lanterns to minimise the risk of fire.[1]

Can a camping stove, barbecue or generator be used indoors when the power is out?

The CDC states that a charcoal grill, hibachi, lantern or portable camping stove should never be used inside a home, tent or camper, and that a generator should never be used inside a home, basement or garage or less than 20 feet from a window, door or vent.[1] The NHS lists using barbecues or camping stoves inside as a cause of carbon monoxide build-up.[3]

What do health agencies state about heat when there is no power for cooling?

WHO lists using night air to cool the home, blocking direct sunlight during the day, cool showers, drinking water regularly and spending 2 to 3 hours a day in a cool place.[5] The CDC states that people seek shelter with friends or at a community shelter if conditions are too hot or too cold.[1]

How warm is a home kept in cold weather, according to the NHS?

The NHS states that, if possible, homes are heated to at least 18°C in the rooms used regularly.[7] The CDC lists extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm winter coats as heat sources during a power outage.[1]

How much fluid is needed when exercising in the heat?

ACSM's 2007 position stand gives the goal of preventing more than 2% body-weight loss from water deficit during exercise, and states that sweat rates vary considerably between individuals.[8] WHO's heat guidance lists 1 cup of water an hour and at least 2 to 3 litres a day.[5]

How is this page different from the power outage page?

This page covers long or unplanned outages, where darkness, alternative heating and cooking, heat, cold and water supply come into play. The power outage page covers short or scheduled cuts of a few hours and studies of short bouts of activity.
