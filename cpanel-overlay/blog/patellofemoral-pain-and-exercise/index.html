Patellofemoral (kneecap) pain and exercise: what the research shows

What a clinical practice guideline, a consensus statement and systematic reviews report on how common kneecap pain is, exercise therapy and long-term outcomes.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

The 2019 clinical practice guideline of the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy (American Physical Therapy Association) describes patellofemoral pain (PFP) as a common condition with poorly defined pain behind or around the kneecap that can worsen with loading activities such as squatting, prolonged sitting, stairs, jumping or running. Its diagnostic criteria are pain behind or around the kneecap, pain reproduced by squatting, stair climbing, prolonged sitting or other activities loading the knee in a flexed position, and exclusion of all other conditions that may cause anterior knee pain.[1]

A systematic review of 23 studies reported an annual prevalence of patellofemoral pain of 22.7% in the general population and 28.9% in adolescents; pooled point prevalence in adolescents was 7.2%.[5] The guideline states that PFP is not a self-limiting condition and that 50% to 56% of adolescents report persistent knee pain 2 years after their initial diagnosis.[1] In a long-term follow-up of two trials, 60 participants (19.3%) answered at 5 to 8 years: 57% reported unfavourable recovery, and 48 of the 50 who had knee X-rays showed no signs of radiographic knee osteoarthritis.[7]

A Cochrane review of 31 trials (1,690 participants) found very low-quality but consistent evidence that exercise therapy may result in clinically important reductions in pain and improvements in function, and in better long-term recovery. Pooled data from 5 studies (375 participants) favoured exercise over control for short-term pain during activity (mean difference −1.46 on a 0 to 10 scale). The authors rated the evidence very low quality, generally because of serious design flaws and small numbers of participants, and state that there is insufficient evidence to determine the best form of exercise therapy.[3]

In the same review, 3 studies (104 participants) favoured hip plus knee exercise over knee exercise alone for short-term pain during activity (mean difference −2.20); for function and recovery, neither approach was shown to be superior.[3] A systematic review of 14 studies found strong evidence that hip (proximal) combined with quadriceps rehabilitation decreased pain and improved function in the short term, with moderate evidence for the medium term.[4]

The 2018 consensus statement, voted on by a panel of 41 patellofemoral pain experts, supports exercise therapy (especially combined hip-focused and knee-focused exercise), combined interventions and foot orthoses to improve pain and/or function. It does not recommend patellofemoral, knee or lumbar mobilisations in isolation, or electrophysical agents.[2]

A meta-analysis of 18 prospective studies (4,818 participants, 483 of whom developed PFP) found that age, height, weight, body mass index, body fat and Q angle were not risk factors for future PFP. Quadriceps weakness was a risk factor in military recruits, and higher hip abduction strength was a risk factor in adolescents.[6]

What the guideline and consensus state on exercise and other treatments

The 2019 guideline (grade A, strong evidence) states that clinicians should include exercise therapy with combined hip- and knee-targeted exercises to reduce pain and improve patient-reported outcomes and functional performance in the short, medium and long term, that preference to hip-targeted exercise may be given in the early stages of treatment, and that the combination is preferred over solely knee-targeted exercises.[1]

The guideline states that clinicians may use tailored patellar taping with exercise therapy for short-term (4 weeks) pain reduction; should not prescribe patellofemoral knee orthoses, including braces, sleeves or straps; should prescribe prefabricated foot orthoses for people with greater than normal pronation, only in the short term (up to 6 weeks) and combined with exercise therapy; and should not use manual therapy in isolation or biophysical agents such as ultrasound, cryotherapy or electrical stimulation.[1]

The 2018 consensus reports uncertainty about patellar taping and bracing, acupuncture and dry needling, manual soft tissue techniques, blood flow restriction training and gait retraining.[2]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The 2019 guideline's diagnostic criteria include exclusion of all other conditions that may cause anterior knee pain, including tibiofemoral pathologies.[1]

The NHS states to see a GP if knee pain does not improve within a few weeks, and to get advice from 111 if the knee is very painful, cannot be moved or take weight, is badly swollen or has changed shape, locks, gives way or painfully clicks (painless clicking is described as normal), or with a very high temperature and redness or heat around the knee.[8] ACSM's 2015 screening model bases pre-exercise medical clearance on current activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity.[9]
