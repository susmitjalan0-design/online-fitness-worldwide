HYROX training plan for beginners: 12 weeks, 7 rules that matter

Eight rounds. One kilometre of running, then one station. Here is how a coach would build the 12 weeks, with the race facts checked against HYROX's own pages.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 2 November 2026

HYROX is 8 rounds. Each round is a 1 km run, then one station. Same format in every city.[1]

This post gives you 7 rules and a 12-week sample plan. Rules first. Plan after.

It is general. It can't know your run pace, your lifts, your injuries or your race date. A coach in the session can.

Weights are the 2026/27 season's, in kg with pounds in brackets (rounded). Rules change. Read the current rulebook before race day.[2]

This is independent coaching. It is not an official HYROX plan.

HYROX training plan for beginners: 7 rules that matter

  1. 1Know the race you are training for
  2. 2Respect the running
  3. 3Build the base before you load it
  4. 4Lift for the stations
  5. 5Practise the stations that cost time
  6. 6Rehearse once, not every week
  7. 7Taper for two weeks, not zero

  1. 1Know the race you are training for

    Why it matters
    8 rounds of a 1 km run plus a station, in this order: SkiErg 1,000 m, sled push 50 m, sled pull 50 m, burpee broad jumps 80 m, row 1,000 m, farmers carry 200 m, sandbag lunges 100 m, wall balls 100 reps.[1][2]
    How to train it
    Open women: sled push 102 kg (225 lb) and pull 78 kg (172 lb), both including the sled; carry 2 × 16 kg (35 lb); lunge bag 10 kg (22 lb); wall ball 4 kg (9 lb) to 2.70 m (about 9 ft). Open men and Pro women: push 152 kg (335 lb), pull 103 kg (227 lb), carry 2 × 24 kg (53 lb), bag 20 kg (44 lb), ball 6 kg (13 lb); target 3.00 m (about 10 ft) for men, 2.70 m for Pro women.[2]
    How much
    Pro men: push 202 kg (445 lb), pull 153 kg (337 lb), carry 2 × 32 kg (71 lb), bag 30 kg (66 lb), ball 9 kg (20 lb). Anyone can enter Open. You must be 16 or older on race day.[2]

  2. 2Respect the running

    Why it matters
    In HYROX's own sports-science report, 11 recreational athletes simulated an Open race. The median was 86.2 minutes: 51.2 running and 32.8 on the stations. Tiny sample. Read it as a hint, not a law.[3]
    How to train it
    Typical Open finish times in RoxTimes' analysis of 1,274,586 official finishes: men 1:27:59, women 1:36:00.[4] That is a third-party median, not an official target. Your first goal is to finish well, not to match a median.
    How much
    So run 3 times a week. Most of it easy. You should be able to talk.

  3. 3Build the base before you load it

    Why it matters
    The WHO says adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days.[5] That is your floor, not your finish line.
    How to train it
    Raise running volume in small steps. In one study of 874 novice runners, those who raised weekly distance by more than 30% over two weeks seemed more vulnerable to distance-related injuries than those under 10%. The study was exploratory and the result was not statistically significant.[8]
    How much
    Weeks 1 to 4: 3 easy runs and 2 strength days. No hero sessions.

  4. 4Lift for the stations

    Why it matters
    Sleds, carries, lunges and wall balls are strength under fatigue. ACSM recommends 2 to 3 strength days a week for novices, at loads around 8 to 12 reps to failure.[6]
    How to train it
    Squat, hinge, push, pull, carry. Add station practice after the lifts are solid.
    How much
    A meta-analysis of 43 studies found that adding aerobic training to strength training made no clear difference to maximal strength or muscle size, but explosive strength was lower, more so when both were in the same session.[7] Split runs and lifts when you can.

  5. 5Practise the stations that cost time

    Why it matters
    Typical Open splits (men / women, RoxTimes): sled pull 4:58 / 5:47, burpee broad jumps 5:38 / 6:45, wall balls 7:03 / 6:42, Roxzone transitions 7:24 / 7:57. Third-party medians.[4]
    How to train it
    Once a week, run 1 km, then practise one or two stations. Technique first. Then weight.
    How much
    HYROX itself says to try the sled push at least once before race day, in grippy shoes.[1]

  6. 6Rehearse once, not every week

    Why it matters
    A half simulation (4 rounds, run plus station) shows you your pacing and your weak station.
    How to train it
    Do it around week 9 or 10. Race weights or lighter. Then rest.
    How much
    Full simulations too often will leave you tired on race day.

  7. 7Taper for two weeks, not zero

    Why it matters
    A meta-analysis of tapering found the best results from a 2-week taper with training volume cut by 41 to 60%, with intensity and frequency kept.[9]
    How to train it
    Cut the volume. Keep a few short, sharp efforts. Same number of sessions, shorter.
    How much
    Race week: nothing new. Check the rulebook, your ID, your shoes.

Count back 12 weeks from your race. If you have less, compress the base. Don't skip the taper.

A 12-week sample plan

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it to your body, your health and your history.

  • Weekly shape: Mon strength A (squat, hinge, push, carry). Tue easy run, 30 to 40 minutes. Wed station practice, 30 minutes. Thu rest or a walk. Fri strength B (lunge, pull, press, carry). Sat easy run, 40 to 60 minutes. Sun rest.
  • Weeks 1 to 4, base: 3 easy runs of 20 to 40 minutes, growing slowly. 2 strength days, 3 sets of 8 to 12. Stations: light, for technique.
  • Weeks 5 to 8, build: 3 runs a week, one with 4 × 1 km at steady, controlled effort and 2 minutes of walking between. Strength heavier, 3 to 4 sets of 6 to 10. Once a week: 1 km, then a station, 3 rounds.
  • Weeks 9 and 10, specific: one half simulation of 4 rounds. Practise your weakest station twice a week.
  • Weeks 11 and 12, taper: about 40 to 60% less volume across the two weeks. Same number of sessions. A few short efforts at race pace.
  • Adjust it to your body, your health and your history. If a week goes wrong, repeat the week. Don't double up.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model says: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[11] Race-style work is hard: heavy lifts, jumps, burpees, sprints and carries. Build up to it over weeks. Don't test a maximum lift alone or without a spotter or a coach watching. Sled pushes, burpee broad jumps and wall balls are hard on knees, back and shoulders. Build the load over weeks. Ask a doctor first if you have a heart, blood-pressure or joint condition.
