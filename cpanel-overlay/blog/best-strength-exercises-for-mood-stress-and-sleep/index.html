Strength training, mood, anxiety and sleep: what the research shows

What meta-analyses, NICE and the NHS found and state on resistance training and depressive symptoms, anxiety and sleep.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

The NHS states that regular exercise can boost mood in people with depression and is especially useful for people with less severe depression.[2]

A meta-analysis of 33 randomised trials (1,877 participants) found resistance training was associated with a reduction in depressive symptoms (mean effect 0.66; number needed to treat 4). Total prescribed volume, health status and strength improvements were not significantly associated with the effect; trials with blinded allocation or assessment showed smaller reductions.[4]

A meta-analysis of 16 articles (922 participants) found resistance training reduced anxiety symptoms (effect 0.31), with larger effects in healthy participants (0.50) than in those with a physical or mental illness (0.19).[5]

A 2024 network meta-analysis of 218 studies (14,170 participants) found moderate reductions in depression versus active controls for walking or jogging (g −0.62), yoga (−0.55), strength training (−0.49), mixed aerobic exercise (−0.43) and tai chi or qigong (−0.42). Effects were proportional to the intensity prescribed. Strength training and yoga appeared the most acceptable. Confidence was rated low for walking or jogging and very low for other treatments.[6]

A systematic review of 13 studies found that chronic resistance exercise improved all aspects of sleep, with the greatest benefit for sleep quality; the benefits were attenuated when resistance exercise was combined with aerobic exercise and compared with aerobic exercise alone. Acute effects were poorly studied and inconsistent.[7]

Doses and protocols the studies used

NICE describes group exercise for depression as a programme designed for people with depression, provided by a trained practitioner, usually more than 1 session a week for 10 weeks, usually with 8 participants, including moderate-intensity aerobic exercise.[3]

The NHS states that people who have not exercised for a while start gradually and build up towards 150 minutes a week.[2]

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that adults should do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (or 75 to 150 minutes vigorous) a week, and muscle-strengthening activities involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.[1]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The NHS states that people who have been feeling down for a while and do not feel like their usual self see a GP to discuss symptoms and treatment choices.[2] In India, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare runs the Tele MANAS mental health helpline: 14416 or 1-800-891-4416.[8]

ACSM's 2015 screening model bases pre-exercise medical clearance on current activity, signs or symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity.[10]
