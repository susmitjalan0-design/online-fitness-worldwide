Strength training for teenagers: what the research shows

What the AAP, WHO, the NHS and meta-analyses found and state on resistance training in young people: strength, injuries, growth and doses.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 20 November 2026

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) states that children and adolescents can gain strength with resistance training with low injury rates if the activities emphasise proper technique and are well supervised, that resistance training includes body weight movements, and that measures of muscular fitness in youth have declined. It states that well-designed programmes have not been shown to have a negative effect on growth plate health, linear growth or cardiovascular health, and that gains in childhood strength are primarily attributed to neurological mechanisms, without resultant muscle hypertrophy.[3]

WHO's 2020 guidelines state that children and adolescents should do at least an average of 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous, mostly aerobic, activity across the week, with vigorous activities and those that strengthen muscle and bone on at least 3 days a week.[1] The NHS states that young people can use weights for resistance exercise if a qualified adult trains them to use weights correctly and supervises them.[2]

A meta-analysis of resistance training in children and adolescents found an overall effect size of 1.12 on strength; the ability to gain strength seemed to increase with age and maturity, with no noticeable boost during puberty.[7] A meta-analysis of 43 studies in youth athletes found moderate effects on muscle strength and vertical jump (SMD 0.8 to 1.09) and small effects on sprint, agility and sport-specific performance (SMD 0.58 to 0.75).[8]

A meta-analysis of randomised trials in youth sport found neuromuscular training reduced lower-extremity injury (incidence rate ratio 0.64); the estimate for knee injury (0.74) was not statistically significant.[10] A review states that most reported injuries in young lifters can be classified as accidental, and that lack of qualified instruction, poor technique and inappropriate loads could partly explain some of them.[6]

A review of 110 studies found strong evidence of an inverse association between muscular fitness and total and central adiposity and cardiometabolic risk factors, and a positive association with bone health and self-esteem; evidence for musculoskeletal pain and cognitive ability was inconsistent or uncertain.[9] These are associations, not trials.

  4. 4Free weights, resistance bands, kettlebells, medicine balls, child-size machines

Doses and protocols the studies used

The AAP states that strength gains occur with programmes of at least 8 weeks at 2 to 3 times a week, and that programmes lasting more than 23 weeks are most effective for maximal strength gains.[3]

The AAP cites expert recommendations of 1 minute of rest between sets for beginners, increasing to 2 to 3 minutes as training intensity increases.[3]

The AAP recommends at least 1 to 2 days a week off competitive athletics, sport-specific training and practice, and counting resistance training time within total training volume.[3]

In the youth-athlete meta-analysis, the most effective parameters were more than 23 weeks, 5 sets per exercise, 6 to 8 repetitions, 80 to 89% of 1RM and 3 to 4 minutes' rest.[8]

What the guidelines and sources state on safety

The AAP states that consultation with a medical professional is required before resistance training for young athletes with poorly controlled hypertension or uncontrolled seizure disorders, and that youth treated with anthracycline chemotherapy are at increased risk of cardiotoxicity during resistance training.[3]

The NHS lists, for children and young people with a health condition, medical advice about exercising, and lists starting any new exercise slowly and increasing it bit by bit.[2]

This page reports what the cited sources found and state. It gives no advice and recommends no course of action. It is not medical advice.
