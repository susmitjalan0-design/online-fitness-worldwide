Best strength exercises for teenagers: 7 moves and a sample week

Teenagers can strength train safely when technique comes first and a qualified adult supervises. Light loads, 1 to 3 reps in reserve, and a parent in the loop.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 18 November 2026

Can teenagers lift? Parents ask me this a lot. The better question is how. Done well, strength work builds a body that copes with sport, growth spurts and long days at a desk.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says children and adolescents can gain strength with resistance training, with low injury rates, when the focus is on proper technique and the training is well supervised. It isn't only weights. Bodyweight moves count. The AAP also notes that measures of muscular fitness in young people have declined over the years.[3]

The myths. Does lifting stunt growth? The AAP says well-designed programmes have not been shown to harm the growth plates, height or the heart. Will it make them bulky? Before puberty, strength comes mainly from the nervous system learning to use the muscle. During and after puberty, the muscle itself can also grow.[3]

What the guidelines ask. WHO: young people aged 5 to 17 should average at least 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous, mostly aerobic activity across the week, with vigorous activity and activities that strengthen muscle and bone on at least 3 days a week.[1] The NHS says the same for ages 5 to 18. It adds that young people can use weights if a qualified adult trains them to use the weights correctly and supervises them.[2]

What the research shows. A meta-analysis of controlled trials in under-18s found an overall effect size of 1.12 for resistance training on muscle strength. The ability to gain strength seemed to rise with age and maturity, with no noticeable boost during puberty. Longer programmes and more sets did better.[7] In youth athletes aged 6 to 18, a meta-analysis of 43 studies found moderate effects on strength and jumping, and small effects on sprinting, agility and sport-specific performance.[8]

Injuries. A review of 25 studies in youth sport pooled the randomised trials of neuromuscular training programmes: the risk of lower-limb injury was lower (incidence rate ratio 0.64). For knee injuries the estimate pointed the same way but was not statistically significant.[10] A review of young athletes found that most reported injuries in young lifters could be classed as accidental. It said a lack of qualified instruction, with poor technique and the wrong loads, could partly explain some of them.[6]

Health. A review of 110 studies linked stronger muscles in young people with less total and central body fat, better cardiometabolic risk factors, better bone health and higher self-esteem. Links with musculoskeletal pain and with thinking skills were inconsistent or uncertain, and most links were low to moderate.[9] Links across many young people. Not proof that lifting causes each one.

Youth resistance training has its own guidelines: from the US National Strength and Conditioning Association (NSCA),[4] and from a 2014 international consensus endorsed by sports medicine, exercise science and paediatrics organisations.[5] The NSCA's view: many of the benefits adults get from resistance training are attainable by children and adolescents who follow age-specific guidelines.[4]

This is coaching, not medical care. Parent? Read this with your teenager. And read "Before you start" first.

The 7 best strength exercises for teenagers

  1. 1Goblet squat
  2. 2Hip hinge, then Romanian deadlift
  3. 3Push-up: counter, then floor
  4. 4One-arm row
  5. 5Reverse lunge
  6. 6Dead bug
  7. 7Pogo hops and soft landings

  1. 1Goblet squat

    Why
    The base of jumping, sprinting and getting up off the floor. Legs, hips and trunk. The first lift to own.
    How
    No weight first, arms out in front. Feet a little wider than your hips. Sit down between your heels, chest up, knees following your toes. Stand. 12 clean reps? Hold a light dumbbell, or a backpack with a few books, at your chest.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Hip hinge, then Romanian deadlift

    Why
    Picking things up with a long back. It's the pattern behind deadlifts, and behind a good jump.
    How
    Broomstick along your back, touching your head, upper back and tailbone. Knees soft. Push your hips back until you feel the backs of your thighs. Stand up by squeezing your glutes. Stick stays in contact all the way? Then a light dumbbell.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3Push-up: counter, then floor

    Why
    Pushing strength for sport and for getting off the floor. Bodyweight, scaled to you.
    How
    Hands on a kitchen counter or a sturdy table, body straight from head to heels. Lower your chest to the edge. Push away. 12 clean ones? Go lower: a step, then the floor.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 6 to 12. Go lower only when every rep stays straight. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4One-arm row

    Why
    Pulling balances all the pushing, and all the hours bent over a phone or a desk. Upper back and grip.
    How
    One hand and one knee on a sturdy bench or chair, back flat. Dumbbell or backpack in the other hand. Pull it to your hip. Lower slowly.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Reverse lunge

    Why
    One leg at a time, like running, changing direction and stairs. It shows you your weaker side.
    How
    Stand tall. Hold a wall if you need to. Step one foot back and lower the back knee towards the floor. Push through the front foot to stand. All the reps on one side, then the other.
    Dose
    1 to 2 sets of 6 to 10 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Dead bug

    Why
    Trunk control: ribs and pelvis stay still while the arms and legs move. The AAP calls core work foundational for sport.[3]
    How
    Lie on your back, arms up, knees bent over your hips. Breathe out and press your lower back gently into the floor. Slowly lower one arm and the opposite leg. Back still. Return. Switch sides.
    Dose
    1 to 3 sets of 6 to 10 each side. Slow. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  7. 7Pogo hops and soft landings

    Why
    Landing well is a skill. Jumping, landing and balance work are part of integrative neuromuscular training, which the AAP says can improve motor skills and lower the risk of sports injury. It also notes that explosive efforts can stress the growth areas where tendons attach, until the skeleton is closer to mature. So the jumps stay small and few.[3]
    How
    Stand tall. Small bounces on the balls of your feet, landings quiet. Then a small jump up, and land softly: knees over toes, hips back. Hold for 2 seconds. Quiet feet means good control.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 5 landings, shoes on, on a non-slip floor. Rest fully between sets. No box jumps and no jumping down from a height. Sore heel, or sore just below the kneecap? Leave the jumps out and tell a parent.

How much, how hard, how often

How often: 2 to 3 sessions a week, on days that aren't back to back, at least 20 to 30 minutes each. Strength gains have been shown with programmes of at least 8 weeks, at 2 to 3 sessions a week. In the research the AAP cites, programmes longer than 23 weeks gave the biggest gains.[3]

How much, to start: 1 to 2 sets of 8 to 12 reps at a light load while the technique is learned. Once the technique is consistent, add weight in steps of 5 to 10% and drop the reps a little. Later: 2 to 4 sets of 6 to 12 at a low to moderate load.[3] The heavy, low-rep work in the athlete studies[8] comes much later, with high skill and in-person coaching. Not from a blog.

Rest: about 1 minute between sets for beginners. More as the work gets harder.[3]

How hard: keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal. Technique sets the load. Not the number on the dumbbell, and not a friend's video.

Traffic-light check, 0 to 10, for pain during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or worse the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. Pain in a joint, at the heel or just below the kneecap, or any pain that changes how you move, is never amber: stop and get it checked. The colours are a coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

Count the whole week. School sport, PE, practice and the gym all add up. The AAP asks for strength work to be counted in total training time, and recommends at least 1 to 2 days a week off competitive sport and sport-specific training.[3]

Warm up with movement. Cool down with easy stretching. Eat and drink enough: the AAP says fuel matters for recovery and for growth.[3]

And the aerobic target still stands: an average of 60 minutes a day across the week, mostly aerobic.[1] Strength sits on top of it. It doesn't replace it.

A sample week of strength training for a teenager

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it to the teenager's age, skill, sport and health, with a parent or guardian involved.

  • For a healthy teenager with no medical condition that needs a doctor's input, no current injury, and a parent or guardian on board. It fits around school sport.
  • Monday, about 30 minutes: warm-up, goblet squat, push-up, one-arm row, dead bug. 1 to 2 sets of each.
  • Tuesday: sport, PE or active play.
  • Wednesday: no lifting. Walk, cycle, play.
  • Thursday, about 30 minutes: warm-up, pogo hops and soft landings, hip hinge, reverse lunge, push-up, dead bug. 1 to 2 sets of each.
  • Friday: sport or active play. Saturday: match day, or a long walk or ride.
  • Sunday: full rest.
  • Load cap for this sample week: bodyweight, then a light dumbbell or backpack you can move for 12 clean reps. No max lifts or max tests, no training to failure, no jumping down from a height, no workouts against the clock. Tired after a match? Drop a set before you drop a session.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. Under 18: a parent or guardian should know about it and agree. The NHS says to get medical advice about exercising with a health condition, to choose exercise that suits age, ability and experience, to start slowly and build bit by bit, and that young people can use weights if a qualified adult trains and supervises them.[2] The AAP says to see a doctor before starting resistance training with uncontrolled high blood pressure, uncontrolled seizures, certain heart conditions, or past chemotherapy with an anthracycline drug, and a paediatric cardiologist with complex congenital heart disease. It advises against resistance training with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pulmonary hypertension or Marfan syndrome. Any sign of illness, injury or overuse gets checked before training starts again.[3] The AAP also notes that most reported lifting injuries happen on home gym equipment, in unsupervised settings, often with unsafe behaviour.[3] So: no heavy lifting alone at home, no maxing out, collars on any bar. Worried about eating, weight or body image, or pressure to bulk up fast? Talk to your doctor: the AAP asks for careful screening for these in teenagers drawn to competitive bodybuilding.[3] Chest pain, fainting, a racing heart, or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort: stop and get medical help now.
