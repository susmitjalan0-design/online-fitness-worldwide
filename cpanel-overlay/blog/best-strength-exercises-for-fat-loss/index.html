Best strength exercises for fat loss: 7 moves and a sample week

Fat loss comes mostly from eating a little less. Lifting helps you keep muscle while you lose fat. Walk most days, lift 2 or 3 days a week. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 13 November 2026

Let's be straight first. You lose fat when you eat and drink fewer calories than your body uses. The NHS says it, and so does NICE, which writes England's clinical guidelines.[2][3] No exercise works around that.

So why lift? A review of 58 trials in healthy adults found that full-body strength training, compared with no exercise, cut body fat by about 1.5 percentage points and fat mass by about half a kilo. It also reduced visceral fat, the fat around the organs.[5] Small, but real. And you get stronger.

Walking still matters. In STRRIDE AT/RT, 119 inactive adults with overweight or obesity trained for 8 months. Aerobic training cut more body weight and fat than strength training alone. Strength training, alone or combined, added more lean mass.[6] So you do both.

Muscle is the part you want to keep. In a trial of 160 older adults with obesity on a weight-loss programme, all three exercise groups lost about 9% of body weight. Lean mass fell by 2 to 3% in the strength and combined groups, against 5% with aerobic training alone. The combined group improved physical function the most.[8]

A Cochrane review of 43 trials found exercise alone gave small weight losses, but exercise plus diet lost about 1.1 kg more than diet alone. Even exercise alone improved blood pressure, triglycerides and fasting glucose.[4] Exercise helps your health even if the scale doesn't move.

This is coaching, not medical care. Got a health condition or take regular medicines? Check with your doctor before you start. Read "Before you start".

The 7 best strength exercises for fat loss

  1. 1Goblet squat
  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift
  3. 3Incline push-up
  4. 4One-arm dumbbell row
  5. 5Split squat
  6. 6Farmer's carry
  7. 7Dead bug

  1. 1Goblet squat

    Why
    Thighs and buttocks are your biggest muscles. Big muscles, more work per rep.
    How
    Hold one dumbbell or a full water bottle at your chest. Feet a little wider than hips. Sit down between your heels, chest up, knees in line with your toes. Stand up tall. Too much? Sit to a chair and stand up again. Easy? A heavier weight, or a 3-second lowering.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift

    Why
    Trains the back of the legs and the back itself. The hinge you use to lift a bag or a child.
    How
    Stand tall, weights in front of your thighs, knees soft. Push your hips back and slide the weights down your legs with a long, flat back. Stop when you feel the back of your thighs, then drive your hips forward to stand. Too much? A shorter range, hands to just above the knees, no weight. Easy? Heavier weights, or a slower lowering.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3Incline push-up

    Why
    Chest, shoulders and arms in one move. The height of your hands sets how hard it is.
    How
    Hands on a kitchen counter or a sturdy table, a little wider than your shoulders. Body in one line from head to heels. Lower your chest to the edge, then push away. Too much? Hands on a wall. Easy? A lower surface, then the floor.
    Dose
    3 sets of 6 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4One-arm dumbbell row

    Why
    Pulls balance the pushes. Upper back and arms, good for posture and carrying shopping.
    How
    One hand and one knee on a bench or sturdy chair, the other foot on the floor. Back flat. Pull the weight towards your hip, elbow close to your side, and lower slowly. Too much? A lighter weight, or a band tied to a door handle. Easy? A heavier weight, or a 2-second pause at the top.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Split squat

    Why
    One leg at a time. Strength and balance, and it shows up any side-to-side difference.
    How
    Stand in a long stride, back heel up, a hand on a wall or a chair. Lower straight down until both knees are bent, then push up through the front foot. Too much? A smaller range, both hands supported. Easy? A dumbbell in each hand.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 8 to 10 each leg. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Farmer's carry

    Why
    Grip, shoulders and trunk, working together. Your heart rate climbs too.
    How
    A heavy dumbbell, bag or bucket in each hand. Stand tall, shoulders down, and walk with short, steady steps. Don't lean to one side. Too much? Lighter loads, or a shorter walk. Easy? Heavier loads, or a longer walk.
    Dose
    3 to 4 walks of 20 to 40 metres, or 30 to 45 seconds. Stop while your grip and posture are still good.

  7. 7Dead bug

    Why
    Teaches your trunk to stay still while your arms and legs move. A gentle finisher.
    How
    Lie on your back, arms up to the ceiling, knees bent at 90 degrees above your hips. Keep your lower back resting on the floor. Slowly lower one arm and the opposite leg, then return and switch. Too much? Move only the legs, or tap a heel to the floor. Easy? Slower, with a longer reach.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 6 to 10 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

How much, how hard, how often

WHO, for adults: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening work for the major muscle groups on 2 or more days.[1]

For weight loss, more movement helps. ACSM says 150 to 250 minutes a week of moderate activity gives only modest weight loss, and more than 250 minutes a week is linked to clinically significant weight loss. It also says strength training does not add to weight loss on the scale, but may increase lean mass and fat loss.[7]

NICE says most people may need 45 to 60 minutes of moderate activity a day to prevent obesity. After losing weight, 60 to 90 minutes a day may be needed to keep it off. It also says to build up to this gradually, from where your fitness is now.[3] Walking counts.

Pace: the NHS suggests losing weight steadily, about 0.5 to 1 kg a week. Losing just 5 to 10% of your body weight can improve your health.[2]

How hard: sets should feel hard but tidy. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal.

Traffic-light pain check, 0 to 10, during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or worse the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. A coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

Progression: when you beat the top of the rep range by 1 or 2 with clean reps, add a little load, about 2 to 10%.[9]

Impact: the sample week has no jumping and no running. Brisk walking covers the aerobic part. That cap is a coaching choice, not a research limit. Heavier bodies and sore knees do better building up first.

A sample week of strength training for fat loss

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it to your body, your health and your history.

  • Monday, about 40 minutes: goblet squat, one-arm row, Romanian deadlift, incline push-up. 3 sets each. Farmer's carry to finish.
  • Tuesday: a 30 to 45 minute brisk walk. You can talk, but not sing.
  • Wednesday, about 40 minutes: split squat, incline push-up, one-arm row, dead bug. Farmer's carry to finish.
  • Thursday: a brisk walk, a cycle or a swim, 30 to 45 minutes.
  • Friday, about 40 minutes: repeat Monday.
  • Saturday: something active you enjoy: a long walk, a hike, a game with the kids.
  • Sunday: rest, or an easy walk.
  • Load cap for this sample week: weights you can move for 8 to 12 smooth reps. No maximal lifts, no training to failure, no jumping, no running. A tired, stressed or short-sleep day? Do half and walk. It still counts.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[10] Strength training does not replace medical care. The NHS says to see a GP if your BMI is in the overweight or obese range and you want help to lose weight, your waist is more than half your height, you have a condition such as diabetes, high blood pressure, joint pain or sleep apnoea, or you have, or think you may have, an eating disorder. On weight-loss medicines, it says you still need a lower-calorie balanced diet and regular activity.[2] Outside the UK, that means your doctor. Sore knees, hips or back? Start with the easier version of each move and build up. Stop for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.
