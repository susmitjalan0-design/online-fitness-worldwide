Best strength exercises for fatty liver (MASLD): 7 moves and a sample week

Fatty liver, now called MASLD, often has no symptoms. Exercise can cut liver fat, even before the scale moves. Lift 2 or 3 non-consecutive days a week, walk most days. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 15 November 2026

Fatty liver is common. Most people with it feel nothing. It's usually found on a blood test or scan done for something else.[2] Its new name is metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, MASLD. It replaces non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, NAFLD.[3] Same condition. I'll say fatty liver here.

The NHS says you're more likely to have it if you carry extra weight, especially around your stomach and waist, aren't very active, have type 2 diabetes, PCOS, high blood pressure or high cholesterol, or are over 50. Its advice: a healthy, balanced diet, at least 150 minutes of exercise a week, losing weight if you're overweight, keeping alcohol within limits and not smoking.[2]

The 2024 European guideline from the liver, diabetes and obesity societies recommends physical activity to reduce liver fat, preferably more than 150 minutes a week of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous activity. With overweight, it sets weight-loss targets: at least 5% to reduce liver fat, 7 to 10% to calm inflammation, 10% or more to improve scarring (fibrosis).[3]

Here's the part I like. A review of 12 controlled trials found exercise lowered liver fat, even with little or no weight loss. It did not change ALT, a liver blood test.[4] A later review of 14 trials, 551 people, measured liver fat by MRI. People who exercised were about 3.5 times more likely to cut it by 30% or more. That took about 150 minutes a week of brisk walking or the equivalent; smaller doses didn't show it.[5]

Strength work in particular. A review found both aerobic and resistance exercise reduced liver fat, and resistance work did it at a lower effort. That may suit you if your fitness is low.[6] In a small UK study, 8 weeks of resistance exercise cut liver fat by 13% with no change in body weight.[7] In a randomised trial, 3 months of strength training 3 times a week lowered liver fat on ultrasound more than stretching did, with less trunk fat and more lean mass.[8] Small studies. A good signal, not the whole answer.

What's less clear: the same guideline says the evidence that exercise improves liver scarring or long-term liver outcomes is less robust than for heart and metabolic health.[3] So we train for liver fat, strength and fitness. Your doctor tracks the liver.

This is coaching, not medical care. Got a diagnosis, abnormal liver tests, or take regular medicines? Check with your doctor before you start. Read "Before you start".

The 7 best strength exercises for fatty liver

  1. 1Goblet squat
  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift
  3. 3Incline push-up
  4. 4One-arm dumbbell row
  5. 5Glute bridge
  6. 6Half-kneeling one-arm overhead press
  7. 7Farmer's carry

  1. 1Goblet squat

    Why
    Thighs and buttocks are your biggest muscles. More muscle working, more fuel used.
    How
    Hold one dumbbell or a full water bottle at your chest. Feet a little wider than hips. Sit down between your heels, chest up, knees in line with your toes. Stand up tall. Too much? Sit to a chair and stand up again. Easy? A heavier weight, or a 3-second lowering.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  2. 2Dumbbell Romanian deadlift

    Why
    Back of the legs and the back itself. The hinge you use to lift a bag off the floor.
    How
    Stand tall, weights in front of your thighs, knees soft. Push your hips back and slide the weights down your legs with a long, flat back. Stop when you feel the back of your thighs, then drive your hips forward to stand. Too much? A shorter range, hands to just above the knees, no weight. Easy? Heavier weights, or a slower lowering.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  3. 3Incline push-up

    Why
    Chest, shoulders and arms in one move. The height of your hands sets how hard it is.
    How
    Hands on a kitchen counter or a sturdy table, a little wider than your shoulders. Body in one line from head to heels. Lower your chest to the edge, then push away. Too much? Hands on a wall. Easy? A lower surface, then the floor.
    Dose
    3 sets of 6 to 12. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  4. 4One-arm dumbbell row

    Why
    Pulls balance the pushes. Upper back and arms, for posture and carrying.
    How
    One hand and one knee on a bench or sturdy chair, the other foot on the floor. Back flat. Pull the weight towards your hip, elbow close to your side, and lower slowly. Too much? A lighter weight, or a band tied to a door handle. Easy? A heavier weight, or a 2-second pause at the top.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12 each side. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  5. 5Glute bridge

    Why
    Buttocks and the back of the legs, lying down. Kind to knees and backs.
    How
    Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart. Squeeze your buttocks and lift your hips until your body makes a line from shoulders to knees. Breathe out as you lift. Lower slowly. Too much? A smaller lift. Easy? A 2-second hold at the top, a weight on your hips, or one leg at a time.
    Dose
    2 to 3 sets of 10 to 15. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  6. 6Half-kneeling one-arm overhead press

    Why
    Shoulders and arms, with your trunk holding you steady. One side at a time.
    How
    Kneel on one knee, the other foot in front. Dumbbell at the shoulder on the side of the down knee. Squeeze that glute. Press straight up, then lower to the shoulder with control. Breathe out as you press. Too much? Sit tall on a chair with a lighter weight, or press only to head height. Easy? A slightly heavier weight, or a 3-second lowering.
    Dose
    2 sets of 6 to 10 each side. Skip it if pressing overhead hurts. Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.

  7. 7Farmer's carry

    Why
    Grip, shoulders and trunk, working together. A lot of muscle, simple to learn.
    How
    A heavy dumbbell, bag or bucket in each hand. Stand tall, shoulders down, and walk with short, steady steps. Don't lean to one side. Too much? Lighter loads, or a shorter walk. Easy? Heavier loads, or a longer walk.
    Dose
    3 to 4 walks of 20 to 40 metres, or 30 to 45 seconds. Stop while your grip and posture are still good.

How much, how hard, how often

WHO, for adults: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening work for the major muscle groups on 2 or more days.[1]

For fatty liver, the European guideline says more than 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, or 75 of vigorous, fitted to what you like and can do.[3] In the MRI review, about 150 minutes a week of brisk walking was the dose that worked.[5]

The strength programmes that helped mostly ran 3 times a week. One review put the typical one at 45 minutes, 3 times a week, for 12 weeks.[6][8] So 2 or 3 strength days, plus walking, is the target.

Carrying extra weight? Losing some helps the liver most, and food does most of that work. Lifting helps you keep muscle while you lose fat. Normal weight with fatty liver? The guideline still recommends diet and exercise to reduce liver fat.[3]

How hard: sets should feel hard but tidy. Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Failure is not the goal.

Traffic-light pain check, 0 to 10, during and after each set. Green, 0 to 2: carry on. Amber, 3 to 5: keep the same dose, don't add. Red, above 5, or worse the next morning: go back a step, with less range, less load or fewer sets. A coaching rule of thumb, not a research finding.

Progression: when you beat the top of the rep range by 1 or 2 with clean reps, add a little load, about 2 to 10%.[9]

Impact: the sample week has no jumping and no running. Brisk walking covers the aerobic part. That cap is a coaching choice, not a research limit. Build up first, then add more if you enjoy it.

A sample week of strength training for fatty liver

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it with your doctor to your body, your medicines and your history.

  • Monday, about 40 minutes: goblet squat, one-arm row, Romanian deadlift, incline push-up. 3 sets each. Farmer's carry to finish. A 10-minute walk after.
  • Tuesday: a 40 to 50 minute brisk walk. You can talk, but not sing.
  • Wednesday, about 40 minutes: glute bridge, incline push-up, overhead press, one-arm row. Farmer's carry to finish. A 10-minute walk after.
  • Thursday: a 40 to 50 minute brisk walk.
  • Friday, about 30 minutes: repeat Monday, 2 sets each.
  • Saturday: a longer walk, a cycle or a swim, 45 to 60 minutes. Something you enjoy.
  • Sunday: rest, or an easy walk.
  • Load cap for this sample week: weights you can move for 8 to 12 smooth reps. No maximal lifts, no training to failure, no breath-holding, no jumping, no running. Very tired day? Do half and walk. It still counts.
  • Keep 1 to 3 good reps in reserve (RIR); stop before technique changes.
  • Pain check: mild discomfort that settles soon is fine. Pain that rises during a set means make it easier: less range or less load. Sharp pain, swelling, pain spreading down a limb, numbness, a joint giving way or locking means stop and get it checked. I'm a coach, not a physio or doctor.

Before you start

General information, not medical advice. ACSM's screening model: if you are inactive, have no symptoms and no known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, you can start light to moderate exercise without medical clearance and build up gradually. If you have one of those conditions, or symptoms such as chest pain, unusual breathlessness or dizziness, get medical clearance first.[10] Strength training does not replace medical care. The NHS says to see a GP if you think you could have fatty liver disease, or you think you're at higher risk. It advises check-ups at least once a year, and a liver specialist if there is damage such as fibrosis or cirrhosis.[2] Outside the UK, that means your doctor. Alcohol: the European guideline discourages drinking with fatty liver, and says to stop completely with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis.[3] Cirrhosis, a swollen tummy or legs, or told you have varices? Get your liver team's OK before you start, and don't hold your breath while lifting. Sore knees, hips or back? Start with the easier version of each move and build up. Stop for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, dizziness or faintness.
