Best exercises for desk workers: 7 moves that fit a workday

You sit for work. Fine. Here is what to do about it, in the order I would coach it.

By Susmit Jalan, coach at Online Fitness Worldwide. Updated 22 October 2026

Most desk days look the same. Long sits. Short walks. A stiff neck by four.

The guidance is simple. WHO and the NHS both say: sit less, break up long sits, and train every major muscle group on at least two days a week.[1][3]

None of this needs a gym. A chair, a desk, a backpack and a resistance band will do.

The 7 best exercises for desk workers

  1. 1Walk breaks, every 20 to 30 minutes

    Why
    The cheapest move on this list. In a small lab study, adults who got up for 2-minute walks every 20 minutes had lower blood sugar and insulin after a drink than when they sat straight through.[4]
    How
    Set a timer. Stand, walk to the far tap, take one flight of stairs. Calls on your feet count.
    Dose
    2 to 3 minutes. Every 20 to 30 minutes of sitting.

  2. 2Band lateral raise, for the neck and shoulders

    Why
    In a randomised trial of office workers with frequent neck and shoulder pain, 2 minutes a day of band lateral raises, 5 days a week for 10 weeks, reduced pain compared with a control group.[5]
    How
    Stand on the band. Arms slightly forward, elbows soft. Up to shoulder height in about 2 seconds. Down in about 2.
    Dose
    One set, about 2 minutes, most workdays. Heavier band when it gets easy.

  3. 3Sit-to-stand (chair squat)

    Why
    Legs and hips are the biggest muscles you own, and a desk uses them least. This is a squat with the chair as your depth check.
    How
    Feet hip-width. Sit back until you touch the chair. Don't flop. Stand up through the whole foot.
    Dose
    3 sets of 8 to 12. Hold a backpack when 12 is easy.

  4. 4Backpack hip hinge

    Why
    Trains the back of the body: glutes, hamstrings, back. The NHS lists legs, hips and back among the major muscle groups to strengthen.[3]
    How
    Hug a loaded backpack. Soft knees. Push the hips back, back flat, until the hamstrings tighten. Stand tall.
    Dose
    3 sets of 10.

  5. 5Band or backpack row

    Why
    Typing keeps your arms in front of you for hours. A row trains the other direction: the upper back.
    How
    Band round a table leg or door handle. Pull the elbows to your ribs. Pause. Return slow.
    Dose
    3 sets of 10 to 15.

  6. 6Desk push-up (incline push-up)

    Why
    Chest, shoulders and arms, which are also on the NHS list.[3] The desk sets the difficulty. Higher is easier.
    How
    Hands on a sturdy desk edge. Body in one line. Chest to the edge. Push away.
    Dose
    3 sets. Stop 2 reps before your form breaks.

  7. 7Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch

    Why
    After a long sit, many people feel tight at the front of the hip. It is the stretch we close the hour with.
    How
    One knee on a cushion. Squeeze the glute on that side. Shift forward a little. Breathe.
    Dose
    30 to 60 seconds a side. After the strength work, or after a long call.

How much is enough?

WHO: 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days.[1][2]

If you sit a lot, more helps. A meta-analysis of over a million adults found that about 60 to 75 minutes a day of moderate activity appeared to remove the higher risk of death linked to sitting more than 8 hours a day.[6] That is an observational finding, not a promise.

Don't start there. Start with the breaks and two strength days.

A desk-day week

Sample only. Not a prescription. Adjust it to your body and your diary.

  • Every workday: walk breaks, and one set of band raises.
  • Two days a week, for example Monday and Thursday: moves 3 to 6, three rounds.
  • After the last call: the hip flexor stretch.
